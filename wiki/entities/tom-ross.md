## His material — attributed to him, fenced

- **Why communities**: serving at scale; **recurring revenue** against the feast-and-famine of project
  work; and members helping members *"while you sleep."*
- ★ **The warnings, which are the useful half**: a community owner is a **facilitator**, not the star —
  *"it is not like social media, all eyes on you"*; **never sell lifetime memberships**; you cannot be
  hands-off; *"community doesn't sleep"*; and members *"often won't actually do what you want… they're not
  always going to behave like robots."*
- **Any niche works** — he cites communities for face painting and for arborists.
- ★ **The podcast test** for a value proposition: *"[name] helps [this person] achieve [this outcome]"*,
  and if you can't repeat it verbatim every time you're interviewed, it isn't one. His own: *"Learn
  Community helps you to launch and grow a successful online community."*
- **Four kinds of value** a community can offer: **help, action, learning, connection.**
- ★ **Scarcity over discounts at launch**: *"it's much better to say we're only going to let in 20 members
  for this first cohort — and people fight over those 20 slots, **and there's no discount required.**"*
- **Price-lock as retention**: announce that the price will rise; locked-in members feel good and churn
  less.
- **Landing page anatomy** — qualification → value → features/benefits → CTA → objections/FAQ → second CTA,
  case studies woven through. And the permission: **you don't need one at launch** (he ran six months on a
  waitlist and a payment page).
- **His growth model**: community value × market size × positioning fit × marketing effort/skill — with the
  observation that people over-invest in the first term when the real weakness is usually marketing or
  validation.

## What is Chris's, from the same conversation — keep separated

- ★★ **"I abhor discounts."** Asked whether scarcity beats discounting: *"you want to say **this group is
  valuable — and the reason it's valuable is because it's going to be very intimate.** So I prefer doing
  that versus giving a discount. **I abhor discounts, by the way.**"* See `pricing` §48, where the
  **risk-share** is recorded as what he offers instead.
- ★ **The honest Pro Group admission** — dated operational reality, and unflattering: *"every point you've
  outlined is 100% true. **They don't do what you want. They're like cats in a room.** We introduce new
  learning paths, new tools and resources, **we have an AI agent to help them — and they just don't do
  it.**"* His conclusion: you have to *"train or encourage people to use the things you've built for
  them."*
- ★ **The Pro Group value proposition**, given on the spot against Ross's template: *"**The Futur Pro Group
  helps creative entrepreneurs scale their business.**"*
- **His diagnosis of bad value propositions**: *"it's just a lot of **word salad.**"*
- **He declines false precision** on Ross's growth equation — *"interesting formula… a little bit harder to
  actually do as a formula, but they're good general areas."*

## Convergence to note, not merge

Ross's *"20 members for this first cohort, no discount"* and **Daniel Priestley's** staging-area sequence
([[daniel-priestley]]) are **the same mechanism arrived at independently**, and Chris applies both to the
Pro Group cap in the same period. At synthesis, record the convergence — do not present either as novel,
and do not let the combination read as Chris's own invention. His contribution is the **honesty
constraint** (`business` §44: scarcity must be true).
