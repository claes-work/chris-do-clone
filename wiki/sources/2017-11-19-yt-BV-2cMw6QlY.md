## Summary

The closing episode of a four-part coaching series. Chris opens by asking the two coachees what
they felt before coming on the show, then what was most and least helpful about the coaching. Their
answers set up Chris's teaching: the strategic logic of **dropping small clients to make room for
bigger ones**, the shape of the client market (many low-paying clients at the bottom, few
high-paying ones near the Fortune 100, guarded by gatekeepers), patience with the ramp from small
to six/seven-figure work, and a rare dated data point on **Blind's own early website pricing**
($5,000 for the first modern-era site, ~$20,000 for the very next one). He distinguishes his two
coaching modes — pure mindset work with one coachee versus actionable-but-still-mindset work with
the other — states his expectation that people work hard if they want what others don't have, and
closes on the shift from "making things" to "making a difference." Both coachees credit the
community (Facebook groups) as the thing that ends creative isolation; Chris frames the communities
as deliberately built for exactly that reason, and one coachee offers an extended riff on the
community being "how college should have been."

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2017-11-19

_Paraphrased unless quoted. Coachee statements are fenced separately below._

- **The client pyramid / "big-game hunting" (sales-clients, business).** As you move up the client
  scale in budget and stature, "the closer they are to the Fortune 100, the fewer of them there
  are and the harder it is to get through their gatekeepers." At the bottom "there's tons of
  opportunity everywhere but the pay isn't good." The real task is finding your own balance between
  pay level and client volume: "if you're me, you're going big-game hunting" — one six/seven-figure
  jobs, few of them out there, "but once you get one you can feast for some time."
- **Dropping small clients is about capacity, not snobbery.** You drop clients "because you need to
  make room for yourself and… at the end of the day, if you really think about it, they're not
  worth your effort anyways."
- **Patience with the ramp is explicitly permitted.** Even with goals of 20/50/60/80/100-thousand-
  dollar jobs, "it just does take time and it's okay for it to take time."
- ★ **Blind's early website pricing — dated, self-reported (business/biography).** "The first
  website we designed in the modern era — not like the first two years in business — we did it for
  five thousand dollars," covering design, development, some writing and image sourcing. Chris says
  at the time he thought "wow, this sucks, I don't want to do this, it's terrible," so on the very
  next website "it was something in the twenty thousand dollar range, and they just kept going up."
  He also describes debriefing after that job into a list of "things we never want to do again."
  A concrete, dated anchor for the price-raising narrative already in the corpus. ★
- **Post-call debrief as a practice.** Chris endorses the coachee's habit of asking after every new-
  business call "what did I do well, what could I do better," and adds his own version: getting on
  a call with a **"peak performance partner"** to relay what happened and get an outside
  perspective.
- **Why the communities exist (business/content-strategy).** "Most of us are isolated in our world
  and we have no one to talk to, so we have no idea — did I do it right, am I driving myself crazy,
  or was this a figment of my imagination?" The community was created to answer that.
- **His two coaching modes, described explicitly (teaching philosophy).** With one coachee the
  session is "as direct as possible when it comes to mindset — you come in with an agenda and as
  soon as we start talking that goes out the door." With the other it is "more actionable, but it's
  still mindset": to do the concrete things she has to change her mindset around valuing her time
  and how she spends it. His net diagnosis for her: "she needed to do more — so if she wasn't going
  to add more time, she needs to use her time better."
- **Work-ethic expectation, stated with a caveat.** "When I say I work hard and I expect people to
  work hard if they want to get what other people don't have… you guys will have to find your own
  balance, but for me it's a never-ending endeavor and I don't mind — this is what I love to do."
- ★ **"I've learned to love making a difference more than making things"** — an explicit statement
  of the shift from craft output (a logo, a package, an identity system) to impact as the thing he
  now wants to make. A compact, quotable articulation of a belief already present in the corpus;
  worth promoting as a dated formulation. ★
- **On creative education (design-craft/mindset).** Schools look at courses "as individual classes
  that are part of some kind of system and not… holistically," failing to ask "what does the 21st-
  century creative need to have" — his answer: the whole mind, "not just the right brain but the
  left brain," put together. He frames the community as filling a need school leaves open.

## Fenced — the two coachees (guests; do NOT train the persona)

_Unnamed in the transcript. Recorded as context only._

- Most helpful, per coachee A: dropping smaller clients to aim for bigger ones, and the mindset
  shift from "I need to pay my bills" (which Chris names as **a scarcity mindset**) to giving value
  and helping clients reach their goals — which then led her into strategy and into the initial
  calls.
- Least helpful, per coachee A: the advice to simply "add an extra hour of work," which was skimmed
  over quickly; she'd have preferred a conversation about being more productive with existing time.
  She resolved it herself with the **FOCUS worksheet** (an existing Futur coaching artifact).
- Coachee B: several sessions "felt like I should be on a therapist's couch"; the turning point was
  being told to open up and make small talk, which made business conversations easier. Still torn
  about pursuing smaller businesses; states an explicit goal of "twenty-five [thousand] minimum for
  a website" rather than seven thousand.
- Both credit the Facebook community for ending isolation; coachee B's riff: it's "how college
  should have been… people are there and participating because they want to, not because they're
  just trying to earn the degree."
