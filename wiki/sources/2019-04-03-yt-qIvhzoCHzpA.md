# Designers React to Critique of Their Work — Young Guns Ep. 11 (2019-04-03)

> **Attribution:** the airline-branding challenge results episode. It **intercuts Chris's critique
> with the participants' reactions to it**, so unlike [[2018-06-13-yt-FsyJodhEze0|Ep. 6]] there is
> substantial Chris-attributed critique here. Participants' lines are `[PARTICIPANT]` and are **not**
> persona data. ⚠️ Participants are early-career designers and **members of the public — not named
> here.**

## Summary

★★★★ **A dense design-critique source**, and the corpus's best example of **presenting a rebrand as
an evolution rather than a replacement**. It also gives a **second and third instance of him
publishing disagreement with his own critique** — including a participant disputing his typographic
hierarchy call on the record.

## Key claims (2019-04-03)

### ★★★★★ Present a rebrand as an EVOLUTION — the risk argument

Praising a participant who showed the existing mark morphing step by step into the new one:

> ★★★★★ *"**I like how you presented your thinking**… when you show **THIS EVOLUTION of the existing
> [mark] and making it evolve into the [new form]**, I think that's super smart — because **it's often
> very difficult for existing brands to see the connection between WHERE THEY ARE — the legacy and
> the equity they've built — and WHERE YOU'RE TAKING THEM.** And showing them in this very thorough,
> logical, step-by-step process you're taking them through, **SO IT DOESN'T SEEM AS FOREIGN TO THEM.
> IT COMES FROM SOMETHING.**"*

★★★★ **And the lineage he invokes:** *"**I think Mr Paul Rand would be proud of you — BECAUSE THIS IS
HOW HE WOULD PRESENT HIS LOGOS.**"*

✅ **This is a selling argument dressed as a craft note.** It belongs beside *clients choose the least
risky option* in `topics/sales-clients` — the evolution sequence exists to **lower the perceived risk
of the change**, not to look good.

### ★★★★ Typeface as a cultural signal, not a taste preference

On a delicate high-contrast serif chosen for a German airline:

> ★★★★ *"**Normally I would say: bravo, bravo, excellent typeface, love it, would use it for lots of
> different things.** [But] **it does feel quite FOREIGN to [the brand]. I think it feels so foreign
> to German design**… it's like **[the brand] and [that face] just don't seem to want to dance
> together. IT'S TOO DELICATE. IT'S TOO FINE.**"*

✅ Note the structure: **he separates the quality of the typeface from its fitness for the brief**,
and rejects it on fit alone.

### ★★★★★ The premium-brand critique — the sharpest passage in the episode

On applications that didn't match the audience the participant had themselves depicted:

> ★★★★★ *"**You put a gentleman in here in a business suit. He looks a little bit older, more mature,
> looks like he travels well and he has good taste — AND THEN EVERYTHING IN IT DOES NOT FIT WITH HIM.
> It feels very pastel. IT FEELS LIKE IT COULD BE A WELLNESS SPA. IT FEELS LIKE IT COULD BE A MAKEUP
> BRAND.** It doesn't feel like it fits with the brand and, in meaning, **what I would expect his
> style and taste to be.**"*
>
> ★★★★★ *"**My first impression is — I'm sorry — kind of a MALL AD FOR A MASSAGE PARLOR. 'You have 15
> minutes? I'm going to give you a little back rub.' THAT'S WHAT IT FEELS LIKE. This does not feel
> like that PREMIUM thing that we want it to be.**"*

★★★★ **The generalisable note under the joke:** *"**often this is how stock ads for travel or airlines
look — and WE WANT TO STEER AWAY FROM THOSE TYPICAL IMAGES.**"* ✅ The test he applies is **internal
consistency**: the work contradicts the customer the designer themselves put on the page.

### ★★★★ Choosing a limiting brief when an expansive one was offered

> ★★★★ *"**I'm a little confused — because you were given the opportunity to design a PREMIUM CLASS
> airline, and what you've done instead is to choose an airline that's SUPER BUDGET. So you have to
> find your own brief in a way that's VERY LIMITING. I personally would have EMBRACED this and picked
> an airline where I CAN GO HOG WILD and do all the kinds of tricks.**"*

### ★★★★ Praise is specific, not generic

> ★★★★ *"The one that I'm **blown away by the most** is your **3D extruded metal letterform** — this
> looks so convincing that **I just can't believe it's Photoshop.** … **Use these kinds of comping
> skills in the future for your mockups**: the metallic finish, the reflections, the 3D extrusion,
> the beveled edges. **Well, well done. Probably the best mockup that we've seen thus far.**"*

✅ Worth recording as a counterweight to the bluntness material — **when he approves, he itemises
why**, in the same specific register he uses to criticise.

### ★★★★ The lockup instruction — and a participant disputing it on the record

His note: *"use the [existing] idea exactly the way you've drawn it, but **integrate it with a more
abstract symbol** — and then just use [a neutral grotesque] and say **'[Sub-brand] by [Parent]' in
the SAME LINE, SAME WEIGHT, everything's the same.**"*

> ⚠️ **`[PARTICIPANT]`, published in the episode:** *"**while I agree with the sentiment of what Chris
> said, I'm going to have to DISAGREE with him about turning that mark into an N**"*, and *"**while I
> agree with changing the font, I'm going to have to disagree with keeping it in the same line, size
> and weight — simply because of HIERARCHY reasons.** I think the tagline is not nearly as important
> as the brand name."*

✅ **A second and third instance of the practice found at [[2018-06-13-yt-FsyJodhEze0|Ep. 6]]:** he
publishes participants who argue back, on substance, without rebuttal. **Not persona data — but the
editorial choice is his**, and it is now attested across two episodes ten months apart.

### ★★★★ How he handles the people who lost

His read of the reactions, given to camera:

- *"**[The winner] being the winner — I think for him, THE VALIDATION THAT HE DIDN'T GO TO SCHOOL AND
  HE CAN DO THIS must be incredible for a young person like that.**"*
- *"**[Another's] reaction I've read in multiple ways** — at first I think he was happy for him, but
  then I think **he let his true emotions come out**, which is: I think **he was hurt a little bit.**
  He probably felt like he had a good shot at winning it — **WHICH HE DID, BY THE WAY. It just came
  down to a FEW SMALL THINGS that put [the winner] over the edge, that's all.**"*
- ★★★★ *"**Hang in there, guys. Nothing to be ashamed of. You can hold your head high — YOU ALL DID
  GREAT WORK.**"*

★★★ **And once again he invites the audience to overrule him:** *"**how do you feel about the person
that I picked? DO YOU THINK SOMEBODY ELSE SHOULD HAVE WON? DO YOU WANT TO MAKE A DIFFERENT CASE FOR
IT?**"*

★★★ **One regret about the process, stated plainly:** *"I was a little nervous… **I wish he got here
during the midpoint, because I can already see some problems**"* → and to the participant afterwards:
*"**there's a part of me that wishes you had done this earlier — that you had COMMITTED TO DOING SOME
DESIGN WORK AND PUTTING YOURSELF OUT THERE, so I can give you this kind of feedback.**"*

> ⚠️ **The neatest formulation of that lesson is the PARTICIPANT'S, not his:** *"instead of waiting to
> see if you're on the right track, **it's better to do the work first — and if you fail, if it
> doesn't turn out good, that's much better, because then you have something to build on.**"*
> **Do not attribute this to Chris.** His version is the wish above.
