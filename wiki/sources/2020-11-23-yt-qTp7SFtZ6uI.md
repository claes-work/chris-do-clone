# One thing you should never do in sales — Breaking Bad breakdown (2020-11-23)

> **Attribution:** a solo film-breakdown video. Dialogue from the television clip is
> **screenwriters' work performed by actors** and is **never** persona data — only Chris's
> analysis and his own account are.

> ⏳ **An early source (November 2020)** — it predates most of the sales material in this corpus by
> four to five years, and several later frameworks turn out to have their **first recorded
> statement here.** See the precedence notes below.

## Summary

★★★★★ A complete, numbered **six-step sales process** — the only one of its kind in the corpus —
wrapped around a single rule: **stop selling once the customer has decided to buy.** It closes with
a first-person story of very nearly losing a Coldplay sign-off by exactly that mistake, and the
diagnosis is better than the anecdote: **the producer wasn't careless, he was in love with the
plan.**

## Key claims (2020-11-23)

### ★★★★★ Going past the sale

> ★★★★★ *"It's a term called **going past the sale**, or **selling past the close.** This
> essentially means that **you continue trying to convince a customer of the benefits of making a
> purchase AFTER the customer has already decided to make that purchase** — which then results in a
> negative outcome."*

> ★★★★★ **The rule:** *"**STOP SELLING WHEN THE CUSTOMER HAS FORMED THE INTENT TO BUY. Stop.
> There's nothing to be gained and everything to lose by continuing to sell.**"* → *"**Quit while
> you're ahead.**"*

★★★ **Why it happens at all:** *"people who are **less experienced in sales or lack the
confidence** go past the sale and jeopardise the job."*

### ★★★★★ The six steps to closing a sale

**1. Prospecting** — searching for potential customers, with research to understand their needs.
*"**Not everyone you find will have a problem you can solve.**"*

**2. Qualifying** — ★★★★★ **three criteria, and all three are required:**

> *"**Available budget — can they afford your product or service. Do they have THE POWER to make
> decisions. And do they have THE DESIRE to buy.**"*

★★★ **A rough sizing heuristic:** *"a good rule of thumb is to **take the number of employees and
multiply that by \$200,000** to estimate their annual revenue."* ⚠️ *Offered as a rule of thumb, not
a formula.*

Then the qualified lead (**QSL**) gets *"a highly targeted message."* Sample subject-line angles he
lists: a **question about a goal** · *"a mutual connection recommended I get in touch"* · **X tips
or ideas for a pain point you know they have** · an **idea for a topic the prospect cares about** ·
and a **feeling** — *"put in an emotion: let me help."* ⚠️ He credits a HubSpot article by author
for the fuller list.

**3. Approach** — *"your initial outreach… where you **get their attention, make a favourable
impression, and build rapport. YOU DO THIS BY ASKING THOUGHTFUL QUESTIONS** — listen to their
thoughts, stories, concerns and questions."*

> ★★★★★ **His three sample questions:** *"**What does a home run look like to you?**" · "**What's
> one thing on your desk that you'd like to get rid of?**" · and **Michael Bungay Stanier's**
> favourite: "**What's on your mind?**"*

> ★★★★★ **And the instruction under all of them:** *"**Remember: SEEK TO UNDERSTAND, NOT TO
> DIAGNOSE.**"*

**4. Presentation** — features, advantages, benefits (**FAB**), *"aligned with what you learned in
the approach."*

> ★★★★ **The accountability in it:** *"**it'll become painfully obvious if you didn't ask the right
> questions, didn't listen, or have an incomplete understanding of the customer's problems.**"* →
> the opener he recommends: *"**Based on our last conversation together, you said…**"* then restate
> **the top three concerns you heard.**

**5. Objection** — ★★★★★ and this is the reframe that matters:

> ★★★★★ *"**Don't be disheartened — RAISING OBJECTIONS IS A SIGN OF THE CUSTOMER'S INTEREST.
> Otherwise they would just politely nod and move on. They just need more information.**"* →
> *"**My advice: TAKE NOTES. It communicates to the other person you are paying careful
> attention.**"*

**6. Close** — *"**most customers won't tell you outright they're ready to purchase. YOU MUST ASK
FOR THE SALE.**"*

> ★★★★★ **Three buying signals:** ① *"they're asking about **terms and conditions** — this is very
> good"* ② *"they're asking you to **check your schedule and availability**"* ③ *"they're asking for
> **referrals of previous customers**."*

> ★★★★★ **The hypothetical close, with its rationale:** *"you can close by **posing a HYPOTHETICAL
> question. Why a question? IT REDUCES THE PRESSURE of not having to make a decision right
> now.**"*
>
> **The script:** *"'**Since there seems to be nothing else, I'd like to ask you: if you get a
> credible proposal by Wednesday that addresses your concerns about [clear customer-centric
> messaging, search optimisation, gathering emails] for under \$50,000 — would you be able to move
> forward?**' **And then let them respond.**"* → then: *"'**Great. I'll have something in your inbox
> Tuesday by end of day. How does that sound?**'"*

### ★★★★★ The Coldplay story — nearly going past the sale with Chris Martin

**The setting, first-person:** an interactive music video for **Coldplay's sixth album, *Ghost
Stories*** — *"an animated music video where you can **choose your own ending**."* The band had
signed off on the idea but had seen nothing. *"**This meeting was a make-it-or-break-it moment for
us. A lot of work was riding on the line.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"Midway through reviewing the animations, **Chris Martin waves his hand to signal he's
> seen enough. We all go silent, sitting on the edge of our seats.** Chris breaks the silence,
> smiles and says: **'I love it.'** It was **the three sweetest words we could hear** from his
> lips."*

> ★★★★★ **And then:** *"**my executive producer asked: 'Do you want to see the rest of it?'**"* →
> *"**Recognising what was about to happen — and the awkward silence that followed — I JUMPED IN TO
> PULL THE EMERGENCY HANDBRAKE: 'I don't think Chris needs to see the rest. I'm sure he has other
> more important things to do. Let's show him around the office.'**"*

★★★★★ **The diagnosis, which is the valuable part and is generous to the producer:**

> ★★★★★ *"Why would a seasoned producer who should know better not stop? **Simple: HE WAS IN LOVE
> WITH THE PLAN.** When you and your team have a big important client meeting, it's natural to
> plan, rehearse, create visual aids and prepare responses to any and every possible question.
> **THE PITFALL OF OVER-PREPARING can lead you to having TUNNEL VISION and a STICK-TO-THE-SCRIPT
> MENTALITY — blinding you to your ability to READ THE ROOM and be fully present. My executive
> producer couldn't hear that the client was happy.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**He had seen enough and expressed his explicit trust in us. What additional benefit was
> there in pursuing more feedback? HOW MUCH MORE OF A YES COULD WE GET?** Had we continued, Chris
> could have changed his mind, picked apart shots — who knows what could have happened."*

## ⏳ Precedence — what this 2020 source establishes first

Several ideas the corpus records from 2025–26 have their **earliest statement here**:

| Idea | Later telling | First recorded here |
|---|---|---|
| **Objections signal interest** | *"invite the no"* (2025-10-09); *pre-empt the objections* (2025-12-11) | *"raising objections is a sign of the customer's interest"* |
| **Qualify on budget, power, desire** | the three call questions (2026-01-22) | the qualifying triad, stated as a numbered step |
| **Ask, don't tell** | *"statements tell, questions sell"* (2021-03-18); *"I never assume them to tell the truth"* (2025-11-15) | *"seek to understand, not to diagnose"* |
| **Restate their concerns back** | the discovery method throughout | *"based on our last conversation together, you said…"* |

✅ **The six-step process itself has no later equivalent** — the corpus's sales material becomes
more sophisticated after this but never again gets laid out as a single numbered sequence.
