## ★★★★ Chris-attributed material

### ★★★★ Two dated income figures

> ⏳ ★★★★ *"**Not that long ago, when I was 16 or 17 years old, I was making three dollars and
> 25 cents an hour.**"*

> ⏳ ★★★★ *"I remember **the first time we got this giant check from a client** — it was **more money
> than I made in a whole year**. And… when we saw this check for **$68,000**, I'm like: *oh, that's
> interesting, let me just deposit it.* **And that was that.**"*

⚠️ **Neither figure is dateable to a year from this clip.** *"16 or 17"* places the wage in the late
1980s given the biography's **1972** birth year (⚠️ corrected 2026-07-29 — this page originally
wrote "~1973", which was my own loose inference, not what `biography.md` records; the late-1980s
conclusion is unaffected), but **he does not say so**, and the $68,000 cheque is
placed only as *"not that long ago"* relative to the wage — which cannot be right literally and is
best read as narrative compression. **Recorded as stated, not reconstructed.**

### ★★★★ The mechanism — deliberate desensitization to numbers

> ★★★★ *"The thing I want you guys to get in your mind is this: **I want you to become somewhat
> desensitized to numbers.**"*

✅ **This is offered as the ENABLER of the refusal**, not as a separate lesson. The clip opens
*"that's why **saying no to a $350,000 job didn't hurt us at all**"* — and the two wage anecdotes are
the evidence for why it didn't. The claim is that **a large number stops being an emotional event
once you have watched your own scale change**, and that this is what makes walking away survivable.

⚠️ **The corpus already holds the CONCLUSION extensively** — `walk away from / turned down / saying
no to` returns **16**, and firing clients returns **14**. **What is new is the stated psychological
precondition**, plus the two figures that ground it.
