## Summary

A compact clip on the same thesis Chris argues at length four days earlier in
[[2023-11-01-yt-xiNHfB8FVwY]]: if AI can generate any image, video, poem, book or song, then the
scarce skill becomes **discernment — taste**. Chris opens by naming what he thinks the required
education looks like ("a broad understanding of art, music, literature, history, with a heavy dose
of: do you understand what good music sounds like?") and offers a Pharrell Williams anecdote — from
old footage of Pharrell working with Justin Timberlake on *Justified*, turning to camera to say "this
is going to be hot, this guy's got really good taste, he's willing to push and try different genres."
He then puts the pedagogical question to the guest: **if you were a professor or design department
chair, what would you introduce into the curriculum to give a person a fighting chance?**

The guest resists, arguing taste may be partly innate and that the existing studio system (do the
work, do the crits, get consensus, fail intuitively — "you touch the stove, it's hot") is already the
mechanism; "if there was a silver bullet for taste, I would have tried to do that a long time ago."

Chris then makes the clip's most valuable move — a **self-deprecating admission that he personally
had bad taste**, and an argument that this proves taste is teachable. His answer to his own question
is a concrete four-part curriculum: great history teaching, so references are precise enough for
prompt engineering; rhetoric; critical dialogue and analysis; and exposure/craft experience.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2023-11-05

- ★ **Taste/discernment is the skill AI can't take (design-craft/mindset).** "Given that if AI can
  make anything — it could generate any kind of image, it could produce videos from nothing, it can
  write poetry, it could write books, it could generate songs in the style of so many different
  people — I think one of the biggest skills that you're going to need to have is a broad
  understanding of art, music, literature, history, with a heavy dose of: do you understand what
  good music sounds like? Do you know what a good poem sounds like? **Because if it can make
  anything, then it's discernment between those things.**" ★
- ★ **He admits he had bad taste — and uses it as the proof that taste is learnable (mindset/
  biography, self-reported, new).** "Maybe this is not good for me to admit, but I'm going to say it:
  I think for a period of time I did not have good taste at all in many different things." The
  confession is specific and self-mocking — "two of my favorite colors used to be purple and teal,
  let's not even go into it." His causal account: "I grew up in the valley, in Silicon Valley, as
  the first-generation immigrant. I wasn't exposed to art, architecture, photography, fine art —
  none of that stuff. And so I'm just kind of like a kid with Valley taste." Then ArtCenter: "I
  start to get exposed to the legends, the people who are written in the history books, and I start
  to understand things from a different perspective, and with increased exposure I start to learn
  things." **Because he is an educator, he says, "I have to believe that this stuff can be taught —
  like everything. It's just that we don't understand things well enough."** ★
- **Craft experience creates appreciation (design-craft).** "I take a nature materials class and they
  teach you how to fabricate a desk unit, so now you have an appreciation for craftsmanship — because
  it's so hard to get a perfect edge."
- ★ **How he trains taste in his own children (biography/parenting, self-reported — new detail).**
  "What I try to do with my own children is when we go out into the world, I expose them to things
  and I say, look how this is constructed, this is quality, this is not quality — and we keep going
  through that: that's good design, that's bad design. And so they start to train themselves."
  A rare, concrete parenting practice; note SUBJECT.md's rule that his children's names are not
  public — none appear here. ★
- ★ **His four-part curriculum answer (teaching philosophy — the clip's payoff).**
  1. **History, taught alive.** "We have to have really great history teachers who make this stuff
     come alive, because we need to know where things come from **so that we can be precise in our
     prompt engineering**" — his example of precision: "I think it's a little bit of a Matisse, but
     in the early works, with a little bit of a [X] kind of approach; put those two together and do
     it this way." Without those reference points, "**the machine is leading the artist**."
  2. **Rhetoric** — "so we know how to speak, we know how to think critically, we know how to make an
     argument for things."
  3. **Critical dialogue and analysis** — "so that when we look at a painting or we listen to a
     piece of music, we can break down the layers to understand the subcomponents, so we know how
     they're arranged and why they work together, so that we can do similar things — or at least
     direct a machine to do that." ★
- **Taste is legible in the people who have it (culture).** Endorses the Pharrell/Timberlake framing
  and the guest's grouping of Pharrell, Justin Timberlake and Rick Rubin as synthesizers who "take
  stuff that is influenced by a number of different things and see how they connect."

## Fenced — unnamed guest (does NOT train the persona)

_`attribution: uncertain` — speaker not identified anywhere in the clip._

- Taste is intangible and possibly partly innate: "some people are born with it, some people
  intuitively do it, some people grind and they just look at more things."
- AI "is real good at synthesizing and creating something that's come before"; the human edge is
  **pivoting** — sensing when an audience is numb to a trend and going against it. "To a large part
  artists are contrarians."
- Cites the modernism → postmodernism swing, then the web forcing legibility, then better screens
  enabling "a return back to more heritage logo design" — trend cycles as evidence that prediction,
  not production, is the human skill.
- Names Pharrell, Justin Timberlake and Rick Rubin as high-intuition synthesizers who work across
  disparate influences.
- Cites **Dave Trott**'s "1 + 1 = 3": "the problem with creative people isn't that they don't know
  enough — they just know too much about one thing… they need to be able to pull from more disparate
  sources." (Captions render the name as "Alan Trot.")
- On teaching it: the mechanism is doing the work and failing intuitively — "you touch the stove,
  it's hot, then I don't touch it again"; the crit/consensus system already does this, and the
  internet allows "a wider net… more of a broader swath in our feedback loops."
- Concedes: "if there was a silver bullet for taste, I think I would have tried to do that a long
  time ago myself."
