# Storytelling Secrets That Captivate ANY Crowd

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — mixed interview; only Chris Do's lines train the persona.** The guest is
> **Karen Eber**, owner of **Eber Leadership Group** and author of *The Perfect Story: How to Tell
> Stories That Inform, Influence, and Inspire* (Amazon, released 2023-10-03; the episode was recorded
> pre-release). Self-reported: keynote speaker, works with C-suite teams, has a talk on ted.com given
> at TEDx Purdue. Eber supplies the storytelling science and frameworks. Chris interviews her but
> contributes substantial first-person material — including **the most direct account of his refugee
> childhood and internalized shame in the corpus**, delivered live as a demonstration of her
> prompting technique. Only Chris-attributed material below is persona-eligible; Eber's teaching
> content is fenced as context.

## Summary

A long, genuinely two-sided conversation on storytelling, structured around several live
demonstrations. Eber opens with her own signature story (two different-colored eyes; the invented
green-crayon origin she told as a child to defuse strangers' questions). Chris advances a theory —
that American schooling trains us out of storytelling by rewarding short, precise, correct answers —
which Eber substantially endorses.

The sharpest exchange is a disagreement Chris deliberately refuses to smooth over: he raises the
adage "**don't let facts get in the way of telling a good story**," attributing it to a coach. Eber
says flatly "**I hate that.**" They then negotiate the distinction across several minutes and land in
substantive agreement — embellishment is fine in comedy and performance; resequencing, compression
and omission are legitimate craft everywhere ("a story is not a listing of facts"); **fabrication in a
business setting destroys trust** because "we sniff out when we make up things."

Eber then puts Chris on the spot twice. First: "tell me about a time when you almost failed." Chris
defers, and she instead tells her own TED-headquarters story of blanking on stage. Chris responds
with two "dumpster fire talk" stories he witnessed. Second, and far more importantly, near the end
she demonstrates that **constraints generate stories** by asking Chris two questions — "tell us about
your childhood" (broad) and "tell me about a sound or smell that reminds you of home" (constrained) —
and the contrast produces the episode's most valuable persona material.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2023-11-07

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★★ **His childhood, stated directly and without hedging (biography — the most explicit version in
  the corpus).** Asked "tell us about your childhood": "**I've had a really rough childhood. As an
  immigrant to this country, as a refugee fleeing Vietnam, I've always felt out of place. It's like
  the eternal 'a stranger comes to town.' We didn't speak the language. We didn't understand the
  culture, and I was the subject of a lot of ridicule — and I felt it, and I internalized this
  negative external talk into negative internal talk. So for a period of time, up until about 17 or
  18, I was ashamed to be who I was. I had a lot of identity conflicts, and I've worked really hard
  to overcome those things.**" Note the specific age marker (**17 or 18**) for when the shame period
  ended, and the explicit mechanism (external ridicule → internalized negative self-talk). ★★
- ★ **The mung bean / Lunar New Year sense memory (biography, new).** Asked for a sound or smell that
  reminds him of home: "There's a smell… it's from green bean or mung bean, and it comes from — it's
  usually served inside of the pastry, and it's a little bit sweet… it is a thing that I connect to
  **Lunar New Year**, and it's something you have like once a year. So anytime I have this green mung
  bean, it takes me right back there to my culture, to a very special time — because as a child,
  Lunar New Year meant **you get money in red envelopes**. So it was like the best time ever." (When
  Eber asks whether new pajamas were part of it, he says no.) ★
- ★ **Where he now stands on others' opinions (mindset — a stated arc, dated).** "I'm at that place
  where I've stopped caring about what people think. I've gone through this pretty large arc myself,
  in terms of caring only about what people think and not having any of my own identity and feelings,
  and just being super intense and uptight — to this other thing." His prescription for his own
  audience: "**Let's go for radical transparency. Speak your mind, own your truth, and if people
  don't like you, so be it. Not everybody's meant to like you, and you don't like everybody anyways,
  so it's okay.**" ★
- ★ **The Europe workshop stomach story (biography/voice, self-reported — new, and a good voice
  sample).** On tour in Europe doing full-day workshops, he told his crew he'd eaten something he
  shouldn't have ("I usually don't eat breakfast; my stomach is just all acid right now… I might have
  to do some damage in the bathroom") and arranged for someone to take over the stage if needed. His
  friend was "mortified" and told him **not** to tell the audience. He told them anyway, verbatim.
  "They laughed. But you know what — I got to tell you, that put me really at ease… I wasn't
  thinking, as I'm talking, like you get that cold sweat and you're like, I need to leave but I
  can't." He didn't end up needing the bathroom. Afterward a friend said "Chris, I wish I had that
  bravery… people love you for it, but I can't do that. This is kind of the exception to the rule."
  Chris's reply: "**No, I'm not. You could do this too.**" ★
- **His editing defense in the facts-vs-story disagreement (content-strategy/teaching).** He concedes
  the trial standard ("if you're on trial, you probably shouldn't make up anything") but defends
  perspective and craft: his son says "Dad, that's not how the story went," and Chris's answer is
  "**well, that's your version of the story, and since I'm on stage and I have the mic, I will tell
  it my way**" — grounded in "I have my take on what happened, and I'm going to tell the story the
  way I experienced it, that's true and authentic to myself." As an instructor he will "change
  sequence" and "edit out the parts that… don't heighten the contrast of the conflict."
- **Dramatize or it's just facts (teaching craft).** "When I teach storyboarding and storytelling from
  a director's point of view — and this could be fiction or non-fiction — I always tell them: you
  have to make me feel it, so you have to dramatize the moments… slow time down, tell me senses that
  you're experiencing. Otherwise it's just facts, and just reading across a bunch of facts is not
  interesting to anybody."
- **Why we stop telling stories — his schooling theory (mindset/culture).** "In school, especially
  here in America, when you're asked a question, you want to be brief and you want to be succinct.
  We're rewarded for giving short, precise, accurate answers"; we've also seen ramblers and found it
  distasteful, "so we start to tell ourselves a new story that we only speak when we're spoken to."
  The result is that storytelling feels safe only in social circles and "everything else seems like
  it's kind of out of bounds." Eber endorses this.
- **Pixar's rule #1, cited as a personal belief (content-strategy).** "I believe in Pixar's 21 rules
  of storytelling. Rule number one is **the audience admires you more for your struggle than for
  your success**. And we know this, we feel it every time we hear someone talk about how they
  struggled and they say it in a genuine way, not to manipulate you — you just feel a greater
  connection and bond to that person."
- ★ **Hiding is the thing that prevents connection (content-strategy — his standing diagnosis).**
  "Your reluctance to actually tell your story is the very thing that's keeping them away from you.
  You're building an unnatural barrier, and subconsciously you're doing this." Of his own circles:
  "they're so afraid of other people's opinions that they're always hiding, they're always editing,
  they're always censoring things — and I tell them that's the best way for you to keep a distance
  from the audience you're trying so hard to build a connection with." ★
- ★ **Polish repels, real attracts — the YouTube observation (content-strategy).** "The videos that
  are highly produced appeal to a smaller segment than the videos that feel like it's just some cheap
  camera that they're using, the exposure's wrong and it's handheld and it's not perfect. **When we
  want perfect, we'll watch Netflix, we'll watch Disney, we'll watch HBO or Hulu. When we want real
  and authentic, we'll watch YouTube.**" Cites **MrBeast** as deliberately instructing his team —
  despite being able to afford anything — to "shoot a certain way that it doesn't look great, but it
  looks real." His conclusion: "for me to be more attractive, you've got to be willing to show some
  ugly stuff, and then you're going to own it. **And if you can own it, no one can hurt you
  anymore.**" ★
- **On the artificiality of the talk format (content-strategy).** He challenges Eber directly: TED
  talks are memorized then "forgotten" so they don't sound memorized, "but as we've seen many people
  do before, they tell the exact same story, the exact same awkward pause, the exact same laugh —
  that's not real," and the version most people see is edited. "We're feeding into this weird loop of
  what it's supposed to be like, and it's hurting us." (Eber corrects one premise: her ted.com talk
  was unedited, stumbles included.)
- **Two "dumpster fire" talks he witnessed (anecdotes, self-reported).** (1) Early in his speaking
  career, an outdoor LA venue, ~50–60 people: a woman in the green room told him "I don't get nervous
  about these kinds of things" — which Chris calls "an omen" — then froze on stage and restarted
  three times. (2) At a design conference, a speaker read her entire keynote aloud, hit a technical
  problem, blamed the tech team on stage in real time, tried three times and stopped: "that was the
  end of her talk." His framing throughout: "designers are not public speakers, we like to just make
  things visual." His own prep habit: "whenever I get up to speak, I always think, what happens if my
  slides go? — which my slides are just images, but sometimes in the course of an hour they serve as
  a cue to me of what's coming next."
- **What to do when it goes wrong (public speaking).** "Embrace the moment… just let the audience
  know: hey, I forgot what I'm talking about, everybody. I've lost myself in my own story. Remind me
  again." And of an audience member's better story: "**that's a better story than the one I'm going
  to tell — why don't you come out and we'll tell the story together.**" The stakes he names: "the
  worst possible outcome is for it to scar you psychologically… and you'll deprive the world of your
  story, your insight, your wisdom, and your wit."
- **Nudging a friend to stop editing herself (voice/teaching, self-reported).** Of a European friend
  who cuts the best parts out of her stories: "Why did you edit all the other part out?… That's the
  part that I love." He retold her own story back to her, inventing the parts he didn't know; her
  reaction: "that's so good — why can't I do this?" His answer: "Why don't you do this? The whole
  point is for you to build connection and to be real and to show up as yourself." He also notes a
  cultural read: "there is a cultural difference for sure, depending on where you are in Europe and
  America — they're a lot more reserved."
- **Minor personal data points (biography/voice).** His current favorite band: **Twenty One Pilots**
  ("right now I like 21 Pilots"), and he has seen them live. Asked what he'd cook for a guest: "my
  signature dish would be to go to the restaurant to pick up something and share it with you, **cuz I
  don't cook** — I'd probably pick up something really healthy… some kind of salad with some protein
  that's prepared very simply with really fresh ingredients. It's not fancy, but it's tasty and it's
  good for you." He also mentions telling stories about his wife and sons on stage and their live
  reactions ("Oh my god, I can't believe you said that, Chris"), and refers to his kids as **now
  adults**.

## Fenced — Karen Eber (guest; does NOT train the persona)

_Attributed to Karen Eber; context only._

- **Her signature story:** one brown eye, one green; as a child she invented eating every green crayon
  in the box to defuse the same repeated question. "People tell me 30 years later they still think of
  me when they see crayons."
- We don't lose storytelling ability, **we repress it** out of fear of judgment — especially in
  business settings and on social media, where "your tolerance for what you will sit through is
  really small and it got smaller during the pandemic."
- **Three parts to storytelling:** the story you tell; the story you tell yourself that allows you to
  tell it; the story in the audience's brain.
- **Four-part story structure:** context, conflict, outcome, **takeaway**. Cites **Robert McKee**'s
  *Story*: "no conflict, no story."
- **The neuroscience:** data engages only Wernicke's area (language comprehension); sensory story
  engages far more surface area. **Neural coupling** — the listener's brain activity mirrors the
  teller's. Experiences are "stamped with emotions" into long-term memory, and decisions are made
  subconsciously at an emotional level before rationalization is applied. Pain chemicals
  (adrenaline, cortisol) vs. pleasure/bonding chemicals (dopamine, serotonin, **oxytocin**) — oxytocin
  "can't be manufactured, it can't be commanded," and rising oxytocin signals "this person feels safe
  to be around," increasing trust.
- ★ **"You do not need an origin story as an entrepreneur."** There is rarely one story that sums you
  up; what you need are your customers' pain points, "the punch-them-in-the-bruise moments," and
  aspirations. "**The smaller, more specific stories are what really connect with people.**" Amazon's
  origin (selling books) is irrelevant to who they are now.
- **"Every story is personal, but personal doesn't mean private."** Each person must locate their own
  privacy line and accept it varies. Her own: she doesn't tell stories about her family ("it's their
  privacy"), but will happily tell one about a mistake or failure.
- **Her TED failure story:** invited to speak at TED headquarters with one week's notice (most people
  spend six months); blanked near the end; recalled improv rules (look someone in the eye; fall on
  the floor); the audience applauded and her inner voice called it "pity applause," which snapped her
  back. A TED employee told her "you recovered better than most people do." She later learned TED
  routinely edits talks. Lesson: "so much of the story in your head isn't the one that people
  experience… no one's looking at you as much as you think."
- **Recovery tactics:** ask the audience "remind me, what was my last sentence?"; pause for a drink;
  make a joke ("I am living everyone's worst fear… but fortunately I am still clothed"). "They're
  going to feel bad if you quit and you don't continue, but they're there to help you."
- **On rehearsed-but-live:** the "greatest hits" defense — audiences want to hear Blurryface or Piano
  Man live even having heard the recording a hundred times — "but it can't feel so formulaic that no
  one needs to be in the room for you to tell it." Cites **Drew Dudley**'s "lollipop moments" TED talk
  at length.
- ★ **"Whom am I okay disappointing?"** — her question to set people free. You are not going to please
  everyone, nor should you try. Illustrated with the dinner-party dish: in a room of 100, some want
  meat, some salty, some Italian — "would you take that personally? No."
- **Endless story ideas come from constraints,** not blank slates: specific prompts ("a sound or smell
  that reminds you of home") tell the brain which file to open. Build running lists of fragments
  without worrying where they'll be used. Her system: **Google Keep** for walking ideas, migrated
  periodically to a master **Excel** spreadsheet.
- ★ **"Talks you have to memorize. Stories you just have to remember."** A story bank is "your
  insurance policy against those blank-out moments."
- **Success = connection + how you make them feel.** Recounts keynoting fresh off an all-night stomach
  virus in a 90° room with broken AC, six industrial fans, and back-lit invisible slides — "and you
  know what? They loved it."
- **Her book:** *The Perfect Story* — the science, then the full process (finding ideas, tailoring to
  audience, structure, sequencing, storytelling with data, avoiding manipulation, navigating
  vulnerability, using your body), with closing interview vignettes per chapter (a physician, a
  neuroscientist, a video-game writer, an improv comedian, the TED Radio Hour host, a Sundance
  co-founder, an executive producer of The Moth).
