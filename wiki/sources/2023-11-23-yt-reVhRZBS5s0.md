## Summary

The clip isolates two connected arguments from the Adobe MAX pricing talk.

**First, the ethical one.** Chris runs the room through a live exercise: a client says they want a
logo *in order to get noticed*. He asks the audience to list everything one could do to get noticed
— ads, social media marketing, branding, giveaways, promos, collabs, sponsored posts, PR firms,
publicity stunts, content marketing — and then points out that **the logo never made the list**. "Isn't
that weird, that the thing that we sell to get attention is not even on the list? It didn't even
occur to us to put it on the list." From that he derives the duty: hold the client's needs above your
own, present the full menu, and let them decide. If a logo genuinely isn't the answer, "you're not
supposed to keep selling that anymore." He then shows the escape hatch — most clients, having heard
the alternatives, argue themselves back into wanting the logo — and frames the whole move with a
medical analogy: a doctor who recommends other conversations first, and a patient who says "I already
had those, doc."

**Second, the presentation one.** If your buyers are image-conscious discerning buyers of high-quality
goods, you must ask whether *your own* work is attractive to that person — "I see your websites, I've
seen them; they're not that good." The number-one mistake is dropping logos onto a grid, which sells
the mark rather than what the mark feels like in application. The fix is to **close the imagination
gap** for a client who doesn't have one: mockups, photography, generative tools, hard art direction.
Illustrated with the Apple retouching claim and the Steve Jobs store-lighting story.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2023-11-23

- ★ **The logo isn't on the list of things that get you noticed — and that's a problem you must name
  out loud (sales-clients/ethics).** After the audience exhausts the get-noticed options without ever
  saying "logo": *"Isn't that weird, that the thing that we sell to get attention is not even on the
  list?"* ★
- ★ **Service means holding the client's need above your own — including out of a sale.** *"What we're
  supposed to do is we're supposed to hold a client's needs above ours. That's what it means to be of
  service to someone. And if you're here to sell logos and the answer is clearly not a logo, you're
  not supposed to keep selling that anymore — in fact, you should not be selling it at all."* The
  stated purpose of a sales conversation: *"to help the clients get clarity over their problem. And
  once we get clarity of the problem we can say, oh, there's a misalignment, it doesn't fit
  anymore."* He calls presenting the alternatives a **"duty and obligation if you're running an
  ethical business that is in service to others."* ★
- ★ **But the client decides, not you.** *"You do not want to decide this for them."* And the payoff
  he demonstrates: when the client says "we're already doing all that, I still want this" — *"you're
  back in business, baby. All you have to do is say it. They get to decide."* Analogy: the doctor who
  says you should probably have three other conversations first, and the patient who replies "I
  already had those, doc — I want your opinion, you come highly recommended." ★
- **Sell to image-conscious buyers — then audit whether you attract them.** People who care about
  image treat the logo as one of its most important aspects. "Then what you have to do is look at
  yourself, look at your website, look at your grams and everywhere else you post, and ask yourself:
  am I attractive to that kind of person who is a discerning buyer of high-quality goods? **And you
  have to be really honest with yourself. I see your websites. I've seen them. They're not that
  good.**" His own tell: "when I buy a shirt and it's an ugly logo I put the shirt back… I'm very
  snooty that way. All of it has to go together."
- ★ **The number-one presentation mistake: selling the mark instead of the mark in application.**
  *"You're just going to put your logos like this on a grid. That's all you're going to do. So what
  you're doing is you're just selling the mark and not what the mark feels like in application."*
  The fix — **close the imagination gap**: "because these people don't have a great imagination, you
  have to close the imagination gap. You have to put the logo on things for them," using photography,
  mockups or generative tools — *"you have to art-direct the heck out of this."* ★
- **The Apple retouching argument (industry claim, self-reported via friends).** "Apple spends an
  incredible amount of money taking photos of products that do not exist… that phone that's on the box
  is not the phone, that is 35 phones pasted together so it looks a certain way — the highlights, the
  shadows, the reflections. No one photograph can capture all of that. **How do I know? I have friends
  that shoot for Apple.**" Paired with the Steve Jobs story: Jobs walked into an Apple store asking why
  the products didn't look like the ads, didn't realise an army of retouchers was responsible, and
  "said change all the lighting in the store" — hence the soft box over the display tables. *Recorded
  as Chris's account; the Jobs anecdote is widely circulated and not independently verified here.*
- ★ **What he actually sells.** *"You're selling the romance of the logo, of what it can be, not the
  actual making of it… I sell the conversation. I sell the dream. I sell the potential. I sell my
  listening skills. The making of it is the easiest part. What are we making, why are we making it —
  that's the hard part. That's what they'll pay you for."* ★
- **A stray domestic aside (voice).** On barring his own team from answering so the room can struggle:
  "I love my wife — she goes sometimes to my workshops and my classes and I'll ask her, what do you
  have? She's like, I have the answer. I'm [like], baby, you've been through this 14 times. If you
  don't have the answer today, we're in trouble, babe. Our kids have no shot in the universe."
