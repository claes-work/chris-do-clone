## Summary

The most complete single articulation of Chris's value-pricing method in the 2023 corpus, taught live
with two role-plays and a whiteboard.

He opens by attacking vocabulary — **stop saying "freelancer"** — then establishes the economic frame
(**opportunities must exceed capacity**, credited to David C. Baker, with scarcity as the lever), and
the posture (**be a discriminating seller of creative services**). He diagnoses the standard sales
sequence — fit check, deliberate, second call, present a bid — as an anxiety-manufacturing delay
machine, using a roller-coaster analogy: the terror is the glide before the drop, so **warp to the
drop**. He challenges the room to name any other market where you can't find the price, dismantling
"real estate" and "art gallery" as counter-examples, and lands on the discomfort thesis: opacity makes
buyers uncomfortable, and uncomfortable buyers spend less.

The conceptual core is a single distinction he insists on: **the buyer determines value, the seller
determines price.** From there the sales process becomes one question — *what is valuable to you?* —
pursued through **why** questions until you reach a number, then quantified as the **two B's:
Baseline and Benchmark** (where are you now, where do you want to be), with a **Delta**, a timeframe,
and a plausibility check ("I cannot promise you results… that would make you complicit in something
wrong").

The second half applies it to the hardest case — a logo, where value seems unmeasurable — via the
get-noticed exercise (see [[2023-11-23-yt-reVhRZBS5s0]]) and a **reverse-psychology** opener ("I'm
not even sure your logo is bad — why do you want a new one?"). The session's most instructive
sequence is a role-play that **fails on purpose**: Lucas cannot hold the CEO character, and Chris uses
the collapse to teach how to disqualify a client politely.

It closes with two named techniques: **intentional listening** (never introduce a new word), and the
three-word answer to "how will I measure that?" — ***"I don't know."***

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2023-11-26

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **"The buyer determines value; the seller determines price."** The episode's thesis, and he makes
  the room prove it: challenged by an audience member who argues the seller determines value because
  the seller has more information, he tests it against NFTs — would you buy one? no — *"you wouldn't
  buy the NFT because it has no value to you. So who gets to determine value?"* Conclusion: *"you get
  to determine the price, they get to determine value. So when we're talking to clients we need to
  know what they find to be valuable — and when we find that, selling becomes really easy."* ★
- ★ **Stop calling yourself a freelancer.** *"Let's eliminate the word freelance… let's call ourselves
  independent business owners, or a business with an employee of one."* His reasoning: "a freelancer
  is a person who sells their time for money, works in somebody else's rules" — *"if we keep using it
  the wrong way we'll never get out of this."* ★
- ★ **Confidence is a ratio: opportunities ÷ capacity (credited to David C. Baker).** *"Your confidence
  is when your opportunities exceed your capacity to do the work."* With one opportunity, confidence is
  low; with 35 opportunities and capacity for three, "your confidence score will be 10." So **drive
  capacity down and opportunities up** — "whenever possible we want to create artificial scarcity, and
  you don't have to be artificial about it." Illustrated with the queue for a free T-shirt that people
  join precisely because it's long. ★
- ★ **Be a discriminating seller of creative services.** *"We mistakenly believe that just because
  somebody comes calling we've got to do their business."* The reframe: "if you don't fit here, you
  deserve someone who's going to be excited and passionate about what it is that you do. I'm just not
  that person." And the second-order effect: *"the more discriminating you are… you're communicating
  scarcity."* Also names the first call a **"fit check"** — deliberately two-way: "they get to check
  if you're a good fit for them, and you have to check if they're a good fit for you." ★
- ★ **Focus the offer or word of mouth can't carry it.** When Nick lists graphic design, layout,
  project management "and like 15 more deliverables," Chris cuts in: *"the more complicated you make
  something sound, the harder it is for somebody to remember it [and] tell someone else"* — and word
  of mouth is how most of the room gets business. He then walks Nick *down* the abstraction ladder
  (problem solver → communicating feelings visually → **logo**) rather than up. ★
- ★ **Warp to the drop — delay manufactures anxiety.** "What is the scariest part of a roller coaster?
  … It's the part where you glide for half a second before you drop. It's a moment of anticipation
  that creates the greatest fear — but once you're in the drop, you're good." Applied: *"the hours,
  the days and the minutes that transpire between the moment of first contact to this point creates
  more anxiety, gives your brain more time to freak the F out."* So talk about money early: *"we want
  to just rip the bandage off."* ★
- ★ **No other market hides the price.** He invites counter-examples and dismantles them. **Real
  estate:** the audience member insists price isn't the first criterion, and Chris presses until it
  collapses — "there's this thing called Redfin and you just search by price point and by
  neighbourhood… if it's out of your price point there's no point for you to go and see it, it's a
  waste of my time." **Art galleries:** he concedes some withhold price, then uses the buyer's own
  discomfort as the evidence — "as a buyer, uncomfortable… and when you're in an uncomfortable state,
  are you more likely to make a positive decision to spend money, or less?" His conclusion: *"we're so
  scared to talk about money that we're creating uncomfortableness and opaqueness."* ★
- **Maybe is worse than no.** Ranking the three answers: yes, then no, then maybe — *"often times maybe
  is just a time killer. This is a vampire. They suck your time. I think it's worse than no."*
- ★ **The one question that matters, and the missing "why".** He audits Ethan's discovery questions
  (KPIs, timeline, current-site problems, CMS workflow) and rules them insufficient: *"all your
  questions are not helping them to surface what is valuable to them. It looks like it on the surface."*
  The missing move: *"why is this important to you? What business problem does this solve? How does
  this improve your bottom line?"* And when they name an issue, *"you must say: how is this impacting
  your business?"* — if it doesn't, "move on… what does matter to you?" ★
- **Stay in the problem; don't rush to solutions.** *"Do not go to solutions. Stay in the problem…
  You want to stay in this diagnostic phase for as long as you can bear — and you can bear a lot."*
  Only once "the client nods and [says] that's it, that's it" can you talk solutions.
- ★ **The two B's: Baseline and Benchmark.** *"The two B's you have to master: what is the baseline and
  what is the benchmark. Where are you now, and where do you want to be."* Between them sits the
  **Delta**, and the follow-up questions are the value conversation: how many more units, over what
  timeframe, and *"if you achieve this, what impact would that make on your business?"* — which he
  praises as **a hypothetical, "a beautiful way to phrase something,"** because it's easy to answer.
  Then talk like a business person: *"is that net or gross? … what are your net margins?"* ★
- ★ **Refuse work you know can't deliver.** If the client asks for a 17× return in 30 days, "spider
  sense is going off… **I cannot promise you results. Do not sign up for work that you know you cannot
  do — that would make you complicit in something wrong.**" Instead reset expectations as a business
  adviser: "why don't we set more realistic goals… I think we should try to go for 1.2×. Is that
  reasonable?" ★
- ★ **Reverse psychology for logo work.** *"In my experience, clients come and ask us to make new logos
  [and] often times they do not need a new logo… In fact, I'm not even sure your logo is bad. Why do
  you want a new logo?"* The effect is that the client argues *for* the purchase — "they're selling
  themselves again." He adds the meta-instruction: "don't tell your clients this. If you tell your
  clients this and you say you heard it from me, then you can't use it on them." ★
- ★ **Intentional listening — never introduce a new word.** *"You listen to every word that they say —
  every single word. You don't inject new words, you don't misinterpret, you don't try to repackage."*
  When the client says "get noticed," you say *noticed* back, not *goals* or *success* or *KPIs* —
  because "new words have new meanings, that's why it's a new word. Then you're not listening… and
  they want to know: are you hearing me, are you seeing me?" ★
- ★ **The three-word answer to "how will I measure that?" — "I don't know."** Then repeat their
  question straight back: *"I don't know. How will you measure that?"* His reasoning: *"the truth is,
  listen to the question — 'how will I measure that?' Well, I don't know how you measure things. The
  only way I can know is to ask you how you measure it."* He generalises it ("how do I know buying your
  product will make me happier?" → "I don't know, what makes you happy?") and stresses repeating their
  **exact** language, "like a monkey." *"'I don't know' is a very powerful statement. You should use it
  more often."* ★
- ★ **The disqualification script (taught by letting a role-play fail).** When Lucas jumps between a
  $2,000 logo, a $2M rollout, 10%, conversion and a refresh, Chris breaks character and models the exit:
  *"I'm sensing this is not a good fit. You deserve someone who's going to be as passionate about your
  project as you are. I just don't see myself being that person. Much respect to you, I wish you well."*
  His stated fear: "I'll take your $200,000, you'll feel great, and you'll tell me it's about bananas
  tomorrow." He also notes why scripts fail in the wild: *"people watch a role-play, they write the
  script down. No other human behaves that exact same way… it's not important to remember the scripts,
  but to understand the concepts."* ★
- ★ **The cost-of-change anchor for a rebrand (the role-play's live number).** Against a 50-year-old
  company: what will you spend rolling the new logo across website, social, packaging, letterhead,
  uniforms, trucks, collateral? — "roughly $2 million." Then: *"is $200,000 a lot to spend against $2
  million of printing costs alone? … In what universe is 10% of the printing cost a lot of money?"*
  Followed by the pre-close: *"if you get a proposal from me that is south of $200,000, are you ready
  to move forward?"* ★
- **Set the client free.** On the fear that presenting alternatives will lose the sale: *"if you love
  something set it free — if it doesn't come back, it was never yours. Set the client free."* And on the
  psychology: "what do we want the most? The things we can't have. What do we want the least? The things
  that keep chasing us." Credits **Blair Enns**: *"when you're desperate you're very repelling — you
  might even be repulsive. Don't be repulsive."*
- **Also cited:** **Sean Cannell** — *"scared money don't make money. I see a lot of scared money right
  now."*
- **Teach critical thinking before speaking.** A recurring instruction to the room: *"before you say
  something, the first thing you should do is think your thought [and] try to disprove yourself right
  away. If you can, then you don't think that thought."*
- **What he sells (repeated from the clip).** *"I sell the conversation. I sell the dream. I sell the
  potential. I sell my listening skills. The making of it is the easiest part."* Plus the presentation
  argument — close the **imagination gap** with mockups; the Apple retouching and Steve Jobs
  store-lighting stories; and *"I see your websites. I've seen them. They're not that good."* See
  [[2023-11-23-yt-reVhRZBS5s0]] for the full treatment.
- **A dated aside on AI and craft.** On why you must sell the romance rather than the making:
  *"because as you saw with text-to-vector, it's over"* — a passing reference to Adobe's generative
  vector tooling announced at that MAX, and a rare on-the-record moment of him treating the *making*
  as commoditised.
