# How to Ask Better Questions | The Art of Listening & Communication

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do is the sole teaching voice.** A recorded talk at the **Hot Sauce
> conference 2023 in New York**, topped and tailed with a piece-to-camera. Audience members named in
> the room — **Cheyenne**, **James**, **Rasheed**, **Alex**, and an unnamed content creator he coaches
> live — contribute answers recorded as context only. `attribution: sole-subject`.

## Summary

Invited to speak on "empathy and customer centricity," Chris turns a keynote into an interactive
workshop about a single skill: **asking better questions**. It is one of the most self-aware sources
in the corpus about his own methods, because the talk is a demonstration of the thing it teaches —
and he says so out loud near the end: *"if you're paying attention and you're taking in all the meta
layers, you're probably starting to figure out what the hell I'm doing up here. Have I given any
answers yet?"*

He opens with unusual admissions: **"I hate public speaking"**, he normally makes "like 278 slides for
a 30-minute talk" and today made 17, he gave up control by sending the organisers PNGs, and he used
ChatGPT to title the talk "cuz I don't know how to write titles for my own talks."

The spine is a **trick question** — *what is more valuable, questions or answers?* — which ~80% of the
room answers "questions." He then springs the trap: *"if questions are more valuable than answers, why
did you answer a question with an answer?"* His diagnosis is schooling — we're rewarded for fast,
correct answers, so we're compelled to supply them.

The middle builds a **spectrum of question attributes** with the audience (open-ended vs. binary,
neutral vs. leading/biased, clear-and-direct vs. ambiguous, concise vs. long-winded/loaded/complex),
then he runs a live coaching session on an audience member's Instagram growth problem — using it to
demonstrate that he is asking, not answering.

It closes with **PROACT** (from *The Visual MBA*) as a listening and decision framework, a warning
about asking great questions and then not listening to the answer, and a reading list.

Also notable: a genuinely sharp moment of **live moderation** when an audience member's feedback to
the woman being coached turns harsh, and Chris intervenes on nonviolent-communication grounds.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2023-12-03

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **"I hate public speaking" (self-description, self-reported).** *"I also just want to tell you: I
  hate public speaking, and here I am as a public speaker. What I really like to do is to have
  conversations with people — so I'm going to hijack the format of a talk and just turn it into a
  slightly interactive conversation."* Corroborates and sharpens the "conversationalist, not a prep
  guy" self-description he gives in 2026. ★
- ★ **His slide habit, and breaking it.** *"If you know me, I love making slides and I'm known to make
  like 278 slides for a 30-minute talk. So today it's a very unusual thing — I made 17 slides, and
  I've given up control of my presentation. I sent the event organisers PNGs. I know — live
  dangerously."* ★
- **He uses ChatGPT to title his own talks.** "I used ChatGPT to help me do that cuz I don't know how
  to write titles for my own talks." (A small, dated, self-deprecating admission consistent with the
  2026 "use Gemini for titles" material.)
- ★ **The credentials challenge — the Karen story (teaching, self-reported).** A former student and
  protégée, an illustrator, asks how to command his prices; she credits his awards, degree, body of
  work and portfolio. His counter: *"why don't we switch portfolios? I'll give you my portfolio, I'll
  take yours, and let's go and present to the same client. What do you imagine would happen?"* She
  says no, it wouldn't work — which sets up the real answer: it isn't the portfolio. When the room
  guesses "listening" and "confidence," he presses on the catch-22: *"we have to have confidence — but
  how do you get that confidence if you don't have the experience? … We've designed an internal belief
  that sets us up for failure."* ★
- ★ **The trick question, and why we can't help ourselves.** After the room votes ~80/20 for questions
  over answers, he asks an audience member for a question ("how can I improve my business?"), answers
  it *with a question*, and then springs it: *"if questions are more valuable than answers, why did you
  answer a question with an answer?"* His theory: *"because of the way we're socialised, the way we're
  educated, we're compelled to give the answers… whoever was the fastest to raise their hand and shoot
  out the answer correctly was going to be prized, the teacher's pet."* And for the shy kid — *"I'd
  rather not give the wrong answer or be humiliated, and I'll just keep quiet."* ★
- ★ **Good questions are half the answer.** *"I'm a stickler and very passionate about questions,
  because really good questions frame the problem and are like 50% of the answer."* ★
- ★ **The attributes of a good question (built live with the room).** Open-ended (not binary/closed);
  **neutral** or impartial (not leading or biased — "leading questions have an agenda"); **clear and
  direct** (not ambiguous); **concise** (not long-winded, loaded or complex). His workshop-design
  application: *"you should ask really clear questions that are concise but have an infinite number of
  answers, so that each person can come to the table and give you what they're supposed to give you
  versus you leading them down the path."* ★
- ★ **Why questions, not answers — the four benefits (via Michael Bungay Stanier).** Citing ***The
  Coaching Habit*** (which he calls MBS's best book, and notes he had the privilege of interviewing
  him): asking more questions makes you **slower to give answers**, keeps you **curious longer**,
  **empowers the other person to think through the problem**, and — for managers and leaders —
  **stops you being the bottleneck for all decision-making**. *"We empower people, we take things off
  our plate, and it feels really good to do that."* ★
- **He self-identifies as the annoying friend.** *"I'm that kind of annoying friend — when you ask me
  questions, especially if it's not clear, you're going to get more questions back from me."*
- ★ **Introversion reframed as a sales advantage.** *"People want to talk about themselves. And as a shy,
  introverted person, that makes my job a lot easier — because I don't have to say anything. All I have
  to do is find a good question to ask you."* ★
- ★ **The origin of his question discipline — a pricey lesson (biography, self-reported).** *"This is a
  very pricey lesson I've learned. The first couple of years of my business we would pitch new business,
  the clients would say two words, and I'd be like, I'm ready to go — and we would just take ideas and
  throw it at the client one after the other, as if it was some kind of moral victory to wear them down
  with ideas that were completely irrelevant to what they wanted."* His **business coach** taught him
  to ask questions instead — "really basic ones, the ones a fifth grader can understand": *what do you
  want to accomplish; how will you measure success; what inspired you; where did this go wrong before;
  what has worked in the past; how much of a deviation do you want; who else is going to be making this
  decision; are there other stakeholders not in the room; what would they say.* His summary: *"just
  getting the permission that I can ask questions instead of providing answers changed my business."* ★
- ★ **PROACT — the listening/decision framework (from *The Visual MBA* by Jason Barron).** Five parts:
  **PRO**blem (what's the real problem — "often times people present you false problems"), **O**bjective
  (what would you like to have happen, and how will you measure success), **A**lternatives (what else
  haven't you considered — "creative people are beautiful at doing this, they're divergent thinkers"),
  **C**onsequences (of each alternative — time, money, "personal professional embarrassment if it
  fails"), **T**rade-offs (*"if we say yes to this, what are we saying no to?"*). He notes he'd been
  doing it intuitively and was "so happy I discovered it," and that it works for sales and interviews
  as much as decisions. He also flags honestly that he only read that chapter. ★
- ★ **Questions and listening are twins — and the failure mode is podcast hosts.** *"People ask great
  questions, and as soon as a person answers they don't even listen to the answer, so the question is
  nullified and made useless because you're not willing to receive new information to update your
  processor."* Then the pointed personal version: *"I'm interviewed from time to time on podcasts, and
  it feels sometimes that people are literally going down a list of questions to ask me — and as the
  guest this makes me feel terrible. Whatever I say means nothing to you, because you're more concerned
  about getting to question two, three, four and five… Is that how you want to build empathy and a deep
  meaningful relationship with me?"* ★
- **Live coaching demonstration (method).** Working the audience member's Instagram problem, he
  demonstrates his own technique explicitly: *"I will rephrase and then you say yes or no — and if I
  don't rephrase correctly, say no. Do not accept it."* He compresses her answer into bullet points
  (low engagement, low growth, confidence, more speaking engagements) and keeps asking the room to add.
- ★ **Live moderation on nonviolent communication (values — a notable moment).** When an audience member
  tells the woman being coached that her content is "not something people want to see" and then "your
  content is not entertaining," Chris stops it: *"we have to speak about what we're struggling with —
  there's a whole lot of judgment, violent words… you just triggered 45 people in the room."* He asks
  for another attempt *"in the spirit of generosity, nonviolent communication, non-judging language —
  just about what you feel,"* and when it doesn't improve, closes it down: "two strikes, we're out."
  He later recommends **Marshall Rosenberg's *Nonviolent Communication*** to that person directly, with
  a dry "I'm just saying." ★
- **Clients present false problems because they're busy.** "If you're a client you do this all the time,
  because you're so busy: solve this, I need a new website, I need a new marketing funnel — and then the
  agency, the vendors say okay, we're ready to go, because we're trained to answer questions and move
  forward."
- **Why ask before you can answer.** *"Often times we come into a problem and we ourselves haven't
  thought it through."* The questions do the client's thinking for them.
- **His reading list, given at the end:** ***What's Your Problem?*** ("an excellent book on reframing"),
  ***Socratic Selling***, ***The Coaching Habit***, ***The Visual MBA***, and ***Nonviolent
  Communication***.
- **Small voice detail.** On being asked to hand out the mic and on the stage lights: "the lights are
  right in my eye — if I look right at it I'm going to have a migraine." And on his own audience-reading:
  *"my bionic ear tells me about 80% of the room says questions."*
