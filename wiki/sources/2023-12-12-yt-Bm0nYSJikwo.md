## Summary

A whiteboard session on **designing a lead-generation funnel backwards** — and the most tactically
detailed content-strategy source in the batch.

It opens with an unplanned pricing detour: Jessica reports an **80% close rate**, and Chris immediately
diagnoses it as underpricing, restating his **three-yeses rule** and the **raise by 1.5–2×, never in
increments** rule. He then walks the room through the arithmetic of doubling price while halving close
rate — the same money for half the work — and states the goal baldly: *"get paid as much money as
possible while doing the least work possible for the fewest number of clients possible."*

The main teaching is the funnel, designed **backwards from the call to action**: decide the action
(book a discovery call) → build a **lead magnet** worth trading an email for → **chunk** it into social
content → pick the **one platform where the customers are** (he argues LinkedIn for B2B). Along the way:
**Seth Godin's permission marketing** as the frame, "give away your best three tips, not random ones,"
and a comment-keyword CTA with an explicit account of LinkedIn's engagement multiplier.

The best sequence is a live invention: he takes Russell's generic "checklist" lead-magnet idea and
rebuilds it into a **quiz that pressure-tests the client's own logo** — randomising it among competitor
marks and asking how long it takes to find, so the client discovers the problem themselves.

Then a substantial, dated **ChatGPT workflow**: build a customer profile / buyer persona, generate lead
magnet ideas, tell it to improve its own answers, feed it your writing to extract your tone of voice —
with the boundary stated clearly: *"the thing that chat doesn't do, it doesn't decide. You decide."*

It closes with the personal-trainer sales story deconstructed in [[2023-12-09-yt-naF88vS6z8o]].

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2023-12-12

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **An 80% close rate is a pricing problem, not a win.** *"What's wrong with your close rate is 80%…
  because people are saying yes too easily."* Restates the **three-yeses rule**: *"when clients say yes
  to your price three times in a row, you're charging too little. Go get some more dough."* And the
  increment rule: *"a good rule of thumb is one and a half to 2×. Don't make little increments — if
  you're charging $300 an hour, don't ask for $305, that's embarrassing… go for 450, go for 500, and
  be okay that they say no."* ★
- ★ **The stated objective function.** *"Your goal is to get paid as much money as possible while doing
  the least work as possible for the fewest number of clients possible."* Preceded by the arithmetic:
  double the price and halve the close rate and you do "half the amount of work for the exact same
  amount of money" — and can spend the freed time elsewhere. ★
- ★ **What to do when you raise and the nos pile up — two answers, in order.** *"The best answer is find
  another client. Number two is level up your skill."* Explicitly **not** the designer's reflex: "lower
  the price / do more work." He credits the marketer in the room for getting it right, and adds the
  bench-press analogy: "you just add a little more plate each time until you get there." Plus the
  reframe he uses repeatedly: **no just means next opportunity.** ★
- ★ **A dated, self-reported revenue claim.** *"This is how I grew my tiny little business from doing
  $30 an hour to $6.7 million a year — you just keep raising the price, and there is no limit to how
  much you can raise it to until you get too many nos."* > ⚠️ **Handle with care:** the entity is
  ambiguous in context (spoken while teaching price-raising; could be Blind or The Futur), and the
  figure does not reconcile cleanly with the corpus's other self-reports — The Futur at ~$3.1M (2020)
  and ~$4.5M (2022). `[self-reported]`, entity unclear, not independently corroborated. ★
- ★ **Lead volume is a skill problem before it's a sales problem.** Credits his **late business coach and
  mentor** — *"hey, passed away, God rest his soul"* — with *"you need a lot of at-bats."* The mechanism:
  "when you don't have enough leads, every lead that you get you're scared to death… you might learn a
  skill and then it takes you another three weeks to have a call again, [and] you lose that skill."
  Illustrated with his wife's tennis coach, whom he initially questioned paying for: *"the tennis coach
  feeds her the ball in the same spot over and over again, so she's getting a lot of swings in."* ★
- ★ **Design the funnel backwards from the call to action.** Start with what you want people to do
  (here: book a 30-minute discovery call), then build the steps that earn it. *"Most of you create a
  piece of content on social and you say 'book me now' — that's a lot of steps you're asking to jump
  through without building trust."* ★
- ★ **Permission marketing, credited to Seth Godin.** *"Permission marketing is when someone raises their
  hand to volunteer to participate in an ongoing marketing campaign of increasing value… we're just
  going to slowly escalate the relationship so that you're building trust and rapport over time."* Also
  cites Godin's *"do something that matters for people who care"* — with the corollary: *"if you do
  something that matters for people who don't care, it doesn't matter how good you are."* ★
- ★ **You need a starving audience that can afford you.** *"They need to want what you have and be able
  to afford to buy it."* Hence his bluntness about nonprofits and startups — "why am I so broke, Chris?
  … because they ain't got no money." His own filter, stated with a caveat: *"I like to work with
  companies that have over $250 million in revenue a year… for anybody less than that, I think they're
  poor, to be honest — if they're doing less than $250 million a year they still have this poor
  mindset."* Then immediately self-deprecates: *"so by definition I have a poor mindset, because we
  ain't doing 250."* Working backwards: firms spend ~**10% of revenue on marketing** across all
  initiatives for the year, so if your average project is $200,000 the client's revenue has to be large
  enough for it to be *"an easy yes for them."* ★
- ★ **The lead magnet.** Must be **digital** ("infinitely scalable without costing you more money, so
  that you're happy to give it out") — a PDF, video, whiteboard session, template, presets. *"The more
  valuable this is, the easier it is for people to sign up."* And it must be **aligned with what you
  sell**: he rejects a logo designer's plan to give away a sales playbook — *"if you sell logos, giving
  a sales playbook is a total misalignment."* ★
- ★ **The quiz idea, invented live (his best single move in the session).** Instead of a legibility/
  scalability checklist, build something that ingests the client's logo and **generates mockups on the
  fly** — or better, place their logo randomly in a grid of competitor marks and ask *"how long does it
  take you to find your logo?"* Also compare it against the current trend clichés (gradients, circles).
  *"Clearly you would want to design this such that it would pressure-test their logo severely, because
  the outcome you want them to come to is: oh, we have problems."* Results branch into yes / no / maybe,
  each with its own CTA — including a genuinely generous "no" branch (*"congratulations, your logo
  rocks, you're in the logo Hall of Fame"*). He names the tool they use — **ScoreApp** — with an explicit
  **disclosure**: *"full disclosure, I am working with them really soon."* ★
- ★ **Chunking.** *"Your lead magnet is one big piece of content. What you need to do is pull out a chunk
  from it and start to feed that into your content"* — with a keyword-comment CTA ("I've got 12 more,
  comment [keyword] and I'll send you a link"). The keyword *"has to be some weird word that can't be
  mistaken in normal conversation."* ★
- ★ **Give away your BEST three, not three at random.** *"If you don't give them your best stuff… they're
  like: this is stupid, the rest of this is going to be garbage. So always lead with your best content."*
  Applied reflexively to himself: *"I have to give you my best stuff. No punches pulled, nothing hidden
  behind a curtain. I give it to you freely. My problem is I don't have a call to action — but okay,
  don't study me."* ★
- **Pick one platform — theirs, not yours.** *"It has to be where your customers are going to be, not
  where you want to be."* He argues **LinkedIn** for B2B because "people at LinkedIn want to do business
  and they don't feel gross about doing [it] there," and notes that many will say Instagram "because
  you're biased and you just want to be on IG."
- **The LinkedIn engagement mechanic (dated, as-stated).** *"The general rule on LinkedIn is for every
  comment they show it in the timeline two more times"* — so comments compound. Hence: *"when you create
  a piece of content your main thing is to provoke engagement, preferably comment."* `[self-reported]`,
  platform-specific and dated.
- ★ **Why this isn't sleazy — the moral frame.** *"If you fundamentally think that you do something that's
  good in service of other people, it is your moral obligation to give it to as many people as
  possible."* And the diagnosis of why funnels feel gross: *"we feel guilty about doing this kind of
  stuff because we provided no value. We've taken their time to build a trap that gives them zero
  value."* ★
- **His default answer to cold "can we jump on a call" DMs.** *"My default answer is no… because I don't
  want to give you any of my time."* Which is precisely the argument for warming people first: *"no one
  will book a call with you if they think it's a waste of time."*
- ★ **The ChatGPT workflow, in detail (dated 2023).** Ask it to build a **customer profile / buyer
  persona** — "chat already knows this, it knows way more about marketing and building these things than
  you do… let's not try to learn what the machine already knows better than you" — specifying wants,
  needs, obstacles, dream outcomes, challenges, objections. Then generate lead-magnet ideas: *"if all 10
  are bad, you say 'think on your answer and try to improve it' — it will improve on it."* Then tone:
  feed it a sample of your writing and ask it to **identify your tone of voice**, save those words, and
  have it rewrite in that tone; or paste someone else's text to analyse theirs ("you want to sound like
  Gary Vee — it does a very good Gary, because there's a lot of GV content out there"). **The hot tip he
  flags:** ChatGPT's **settings** hold two standing prompts — tell it about you, and tell it how you want
  answers — *"the answers that you get back are totally different if you don't do that."* And the
  boundary: *"the thing that chat doesn't do — it doesn't decide. You decide. That's it. The human
  part."* ★
- **Qualify inside the quiz.** Ask when they last rebranded and what they spent, so that by the call
  *"you already know it's Mary from Texas who wants to do X, Y and Z, who's in business for 12 years, who
  last time spent $335,000 and now has a new problem"* — enabling you to be **"a discriminating seller of
  creative services"** without wasting anyone's time.
- **Cold-to-hot, not cold-to-close.** *"They've gone from cold to warm to warmer to hot. They're
  interested in buying now."* Hence the discovery call stops being a pitch: *"you don't have to sell them
  so much anymore."*
