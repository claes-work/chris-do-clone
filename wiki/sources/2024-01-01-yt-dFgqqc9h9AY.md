# How To Say Your Price - Sales Roleplay (Sales Call With Client)

> role-play section of the full masterclass published three days later,

> `attribution: sole-subject (teaching)`.

## Summary

A role-play in which Chris spends almost the entire call **arguing the client out of the purchase** —
then breaks down why.

Mo's client wants a brand identity: the current one is misaligned, unattractive to the leads they
want, and they're launching a new product line. Chris tests every premise. Would the money be better
spent on copywriting and marketing funnels? Do you actually buy from valuable companies whose identity
is trash — and can you name more where that's true or false? He walks Mo to the concession himself
(Hilton, Marriott, Norwegian Cruises are valuable with unremarkable identities; Apple-level experience
is rare).

Having disqualified "attract more customers" as the real driver, he keeps the two reasons that survive:
**status/feeling**, and **repositioning for a new product line** — for which, he says, an identity change
genuinely serves.

Then the money. He warns that *"premium logos and experiences don't come cheap — that's why they're
premium,"* extracts a budget (mid five figures, $50–60K), and immediately reframes it as **the small
part**: the rollout across ten-plus touchpoints will run **$250–400K**. When Mo balks, Chris doesn't
discount — he questions the coherence of the plan: *"you're going to spend $60,000 on a logo and you're
not prepared to spend in production for all the other touchpoints?"* Mo ends the role-play by backing
out to "let this marinate," which Chris treats as the correct outcome.

The teaching breakdown that follows is the same material analysed in [[2024-01-04-yt-tfeFSjrZ_Aw]]:
the therapist frame, "assume everything presented to you is untrue," and advocacy for the client's
business rather than for himself.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-01-01

_Paraphrased unless quoted. Fuller treatment of the analysis is on the canonical page._

- ★ **Test the stated causal claim before selling into it.** *"I would love nothing more than to design
  a brand identity system for you — however, I just need to check in with you to say: in the real world,
  do you buy products that you love from companies that are very valuable where their logo and identity
  system is trash?"* Then the counting question that does the work: *"can you name more companies where
  that's true versus false?"* ★
- **Name the alternatives that might beat you.** *"Do you think your money is better spent investing in
  copywriting and marketing funnels and marketing programs than it is in brand identity design, if your
  whole point is to attract more customers?"*
- **His stated reason for the whole manoeuvre.** *"A lot of people associate a solution with what they
  want, and they don't actually take the time to check in whether or not the market validates this…
  **I would hate for you to spend money building something in hopes of achieving a certain result when
  that's not likely going to happen.**"* And the honest self-positioning that follows: *"I care a lot
  about identity design, packaging, customer experience — but then we have to say that that's what
  matters to us, and not because we're trying to attract new customers, because there's probably safer
  bets."*
- ★ **The two reasons an identity change IS justified.** **(1) Status and feeling** — *"you don't want
  to feel like a subpar brand, you want to feel like a premium brand"* — which he concedes is hard to
  quantify and sets aside honestly. **(2) Repositioning** — *"maybe the old identity design is attached
  to certain feelings and associations that are no longer relevant… **you've outgrown the old container,
  and to get a fresh start you need to change identity.**"* He adds the classic trigger: *"often times
  when a company is inherited by the children of the founder, they want to put their stamp… that's a
  good time to change the name and the logo."* And the marketing mechanic: *"nothing gets the press
  talking more than when you start to make changes like the identity — all of a sudden people notice
  right away, because you've given it a facelift. That's why people remodel restaurants."* ★
- ★ **Premium is a commitment, not a deliverable.** *"I need you to know that premium logos and
  experiences don't come cheap. That's why they're premium."* The Apple argument: *"every component,
  from the plastic wrapper to the packaging and the friction in the box, the paper, the photography —
  it's all money. And these are all little tiny signals sent from product to customer that they might
  not be able to articulate, but they feel."* The failure mode he names: *"prospects will come to us and
  say we want to create a premium experience, but they're not really willing to back it up — and so
  there's a misalignment."* Closing image: *"otherwise it's going to be lipstick on a pig."* ★
- ★★ **The budget inversion — the clip's key move.** Having extracted $50–60K for the identity, he adds
  *"another at least a quarter million to $400,000"* for the rest, then holds the line when the client
  balks: *"you're going to spend $60,000 on a logo and you're not prepared to spend in production for
  all the other touchpoints — the website, the social media assets, the packaging, the printing?"* The
  arithmetic he shows: a new identity on an old website *"could be anywhere between $40 to $80,000 just
  to do that alone,"* and across *"at least 10 [touchpoints] in front of me"* at ~$40K each, *"there's a
  disproportionate amount of money that you're spending on the logo which you're not willing to commit
  through on the other end."* His conclusion, stated as care rather than pressure: *"if you actually
  hire someone like us to design your identity system but you cheap out on the production, the printing,
  your signage — then I think you're wasting your time and your money, and I don't want to do that to
  you. How would you like to proceed?"* ★★
- ★ **Why role-plays usually break — and a real scale check.** *"Any company or entrepreneur who's
  prepared to spend $50–60,000 on creating a premium experience has already committed way over this
  amount of money… they're not going to sit there and spend 50–60K on a logo and say let's do everything
  else for 5K each."* And the real-world anchors he gives: clients at that level *"would spend $250,000
  on one video alone, one 30-second video… $100,000 on making an explainer video for a conference
  they're going to use one time."* Plus a dated Blind-era data point: *"back in the day when we used to
  work with gaming companies for their demos for **E3**… they'd spend north of half a million dollars to
  do a product launch video that is like 30 seconds or a minute long."* He also notes the numbers are
  relative: *"had you said we have $10,000 for a logo, then I would have pulled the other numbers
  down."* ★
- **The disqualification, again.** When Mo's character drifts from "refresh" to "conversion," Chris
  breaks: *"we were working on a refresh, then we're working on conversion, now you're saying it's about
  bananas. I can't solve that problem."* And the polite exit: *"I'm sensing this is not a good fit."*
- ★ **The breakdown (summarised; full version on the canonical page).** The therapist frame — *"where
  have you seen this to be true? What evidence do you have?"*; **"assume everything that's said and
  presented to you is untrue… that's what it means to be a critical thinker"**; the generous reasoning
  (*"not that they're ill-intentioned… they just haven't had time to think about it"*); and the ethic:
  *"my job, my role, is to help them think about it as an advocate for their business and their goals,
  not as an advocate for Chris Do."* Plus *"the world is ugly — it's run by people who have no taste."* ★
- **On losing the deal.** Mo names what he's watching: *"a level of business maturity, self-confidence
  and self-awareness, a amount of stoicism, and living by the value of do what's right for the client —
  **and you may lose the business today, but you'll win the game tomorrow.**"* Chris does not dispute it.
