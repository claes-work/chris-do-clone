> client.** Mo contributes the **ICE** acronym and pushes back repeatedly; his framing questions are
> context, but the ICE coinage is explicitly his, not Chris's. `attribution: sole-subject (teaching)`.

## Summary

A full masterclass in three parts.

**Part one — the pricing models.** Chris refuses the usual advocacy framing. Asked why he champions
value-based pricing over hourly, he corrects the premise: *"I'm an advocate for using the right
pricing methodology for the task."* He states plainly that he still uses **all three** models — time-
based, project/fixed-fee, and value-based — and names when hourly is genuinely correct (fixed scope
with an undeterminable variable scope). He also corrects a common misreading: a fixed fee is *not*
cost-plus-markup, it's a guarantee of a deliverable at a price.

**Part two — the inversion.** After arguing at length that selling units of time is backwards (nobody
pays more to wait: rideshares, deliveries, line-skipping at Disneyland), he lands a deliberate reversal:
*"you should be selling time. You've been measuring time in the wrong way — you're measuring time in
which **you** work on it, not time for **the client**."* From there: the more valuable the buyer, the
more their own time is worth to them, so **you are selling their time back to them**. Supported with a
dinner-party electrician scenario, an e-commerce launch losing revenue daily, and a genuinely
illuminating aside about paying a **permit expediter** to walk plans to the city planner.

He also dismantles the "more time = higher quality = more value" chain, using Renaissance oils versus
Picasso and Pollock in the open market.

**Part three — the role-play**, in which he takes a client who wants a brand identity and spends most
of the conversation **arguing against the purchase** — the sequence detailed in
[[2024-01-01-yt-dFgqqc9h9AY]] — then breaks it down: he is doing what a therapist does, treating every
client claim as unverified, and advocating for the client's business rather than for himself.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-01-04

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **He uses all three pricing models, still, today.** *"I'm an advocate for using the right pricing
  methodology for the task… There is a time and place to charge hourly. There's nothing wrong with
  charging hourly, except when that's the only tool that you have for all situations."* When hourly is
  right: *"when you have fixed scope with variable scope and you can't, for whatever reasons,
  determine what the variable scope is going to be."* And explicitly: *"in my career, and I still do
  to this day, I consistently still use all three pricing models."* > This is an important corrective
  to the corpus's dominant value-pricing framing — record it as his stated position, not as a
  softening. ★
- ★ **What a fixed fee actually is.** *"When we describe project-based fees, it's not figuring out
  what your costs are and then doing a markup. A fixed-fee bid is basically: I'm going to guesstimate
  my cost, my profit, and some value to the client… I promise to deliver a project on this budget. It
  can have a relationship with time spent or no relationship whatsoever — but it's basically saying I
  guarantee this deliverable based on this price, unless you change scope."* ★
- ★ **Value-based pricing is the hardest AND the fairest.** *"It's the most difficult one to
  understand and to implement… because you have to have a business dialogue with your customer.
  Each customer is different, they value things differently. **It's also, coincidentally, ironically,
  the most fair pricing model** — because it could mean that you're going to price something a lot
  more and something a lot less. The pendulum swings both ways."* ★
- ★ **Choosing a model — the three questions.** *"What are you really selling? What units will we use
  to measure effectiveness? Are we selling units of time, units of product, or units of value?"* ★
- ★ **Nobody pays more to wait — the test question.** *"In what situation, when you desire a solution,
  is it worth more to you for something to take longer?"* Worked through rideshares (the faster,
  more luxurious option costs more), sneaker delivery, skipping the line at Disneyland or a hotel or
  restaurant. *"So in those instances, if you sell units of time, then you're going backwards."*
  The honest exception he supplies himself: things where **the journey is the product** — quality time
  with your child, a theme-park ride you want to last longer. *"There are things that we enjoy the
  process of doing more than the outcome."* ★
- ★★ **The inversion: sell the client's time back to them.** *"You should be selling time. You've been
  measuring time in the wrong way — you're measuring time in which you work on it, not time for the
  client."* The mechanism: *"the more valuable the person is, the more they'll pay you to buy back
  their time, because they also know that **time is a non-renewable, perishable resource. You can make
  more money, but you can't make more time.**"* Carefully restated when Mo paraphrases it loosely:
  *"the higher the net worth of the individual sitting across the table from you, the more their time
  is worth **to them**. So you're not buying — you're selling their time back to them."* The
  conditions he attaches: *"the person must value their time, and what you do must solve their
  problem — and if you can do it faster you're more likely to get the gig, but you should be able to
  charge more."* Hence rush fees and expediters. ★★
- **Worked examples of client-time value.** (1) A dinner party with a broken oven: urgency plus social
  standing makes the host's time suddenly expensive, and a well-off host pays double or triple.
  (2) An e-commerce launch: *"for every day it doesn't launch it costs me in terms of lost
  opportunity"* before Black Friday or Christmas — so the same site delivered in less time is worth
  more, and the same site in six months is worth less. (3) **Permit expediting (personal, self-reported):**
  *"I've had the opportunity to be able to buy real estate and develop property"* — plans stuck at the
  city stall the contractor, and building without approval means teardown and fines, so *"there is
  literally someone in between you and the city that expedites the plan… they literally walk your plans
  over to the city planner and say, I need a favour, Jimmy or Mary."*
- ★ **The more-time-equals-quality fallacy.** *"People think there's a false equivalency: more time
  equals higher quality. And so now what we're saying is we're selling quality, not outcome — but
  quality is really debatable and it's highly subjective."* His test: are meticulously rendered
  Renaissance oils worth more in the open market than a Picasso or a Jackson Pollock? *"I'm no art
  historian, but… postmodernism, abstract expressionism tend to actually get more money in the open
  market."* Conclusion: record-breaking work is priced on *"aesthetics or notoriety or specific
  technique or newness, novelty"* — not units of time or so-called quality. ★
- ★ **How to understand a client's business — "ask a question."** Mo asks how to assess a prospect's
  business and risk; Chris answers *"ask a question,"* then reveals it wasn't a joke: *"that's the
  answer. The answer is the question — you just need to know how to ask the question."* His sequence:
  **the outcome** (*"it's not what they want you to make, but what's the ultimate goal, what's driving
  this request"* — a website is a vehicle, not an objective), **the challenge** (*"there must be
  something that's getting in the way… have you tried this in the past? Why didn't it work?"*), and
  **the impact** (*"what impact will it have on your business if this problem were solved?"*). Then —
  and only then — *"we owe it to ourselves and to the client to say: based on what I just learned,
  there are two or three things that come to mind, each has a set of pros and cons, let's discuss,"*
  and let the client pick. ★
- **Be a detective.** *"We need to be a detective. We have to ask questions. We have to be diagnostic
  in our dialogue."*
- ★ **Research: a graduated scale, and a warning (his own practice).** *"If you're new in your journey
  you need to do a lot of research… if you're more senior you'll notice that you do less research, and
  there's a reason why."* For beginners: look them up, read the site, the social channels, Glassdoor
  reviews, Amazon reviews, benchmark the website — *"just so that you don't seem totally ill prepared."*
  But: *"don't start the party without the client."* And his own reason for doing less: *"when I learn
  too much it starts to cloud what it is I'm looking for. It's almost like I read the case file before
  arriving on the scene of the crime, and I'm starting to make a lot of assumptions."* He also
  explicitly corrects Mo's paraphrase — *"just for the record, if you play back the tape, I never said
  go into it with no information. You just made that part up."* ★
- **Cites Kevin Daley's *Socratic Selling*:** *"no amount of research that you can do will get you the
  kind of information that you can get if you just speak to the decision makers — they have the
  up-to-date stuff, they have the real stuff. You just have the publicly available information."*
- ★ **What makes a question "leading."** *"It presumes an outcome, it's very directed, there is a
  sense of judgment on it."* His example: *"'Is your website working for you?' — well, you've made a
  presumption that I want to talk about my website, and by inferring 'is it working for you' that you
  don't think it's working."* ★
- ★★ **Why he argues against the sale — the therapist frame (the role-play breakdown).** *"What I'm
  trying to do is what therapists do. The therapist is almost solely reliant on the person in front of
  them to tell them the truth — and the truth is a funny word to use, because true to them, true to
  life, true to reality can all be true and also be all untrue. So whenever they hear something and
  their spider sense goes off, they have to say: **where have you seen this to be true? What evidence
  do you have that supports your observation?**"* ★★
- ★★ **"Assume everything that's said and presented to you is untrue."** *"Not to be a jerk, but
  that's what it means to be a critical thinker — to be critical of your thoughts."* His stated
  reasoning, which is generous rather than cynical: *"clients are busy, they don't spend their time
  thinking about the things that I care a lot about, so they haven't formulated all their thoughts…
  **they ask for solutions they don't even know are connected to real results**, and they don't use
  precise enough language. That's why I suspect nothing that they say is true — **not that they're
  ill-intentioned, not that they're trying to lie to me, but they just haven't had time to think about
  it.** My job, my role, is to help them think about it **as an advocate for their business and their
  goals, not as an advocate for Chris Do.**"* ★★
- ★ **The critical-thinking move done politely.** He won't ask "what evidence do you have?" directly —
  *"it seems very challenging for me to ask it that way"* — so instead: *"based on your experiences
  with brands that are valuable, how many can you think of that have a great identity design system
  versus how many that don't?"* He knows the answer in advance: *"**the world is ugly. It's run by
  people who have no taste, who hire people [who] have no taste, to make things that are tasteless.**
  It's very rare… Apple is the easy reference as the gold standard, [and] if you look at the gold
  standard it falls off really quickly."* ★
- ★ **Don't enter new evidence into the case.** *"If they say something that's true, then I'm more
  convicted and confident that this is the right solution."* But *"me coming in saying, well, I went
  through the site and I've been watching these shows on the internet and this is what's [wrong] —
  that's me entering new evidence into the case. I don't want to do that."* ★
- ★ **A business lesson aimed at his own team (values/leadership).** He recounts a two-hour call with
  the entire content team where *"each and every single person presupposes they have the right
  solution, and the whole time in which I'm talking I think they're just mostly waiting so they can
  present their idea — versus, what is Chris really saying, what is it that he wants, and how do we
  check in with him to make sure this is true."* His verdict: *"everybody is just really waiting to
  say something, not really listening at all, and using their own unverified observations and opinions
  to counter my own opinions. **You're going to lose that war every single time** — but what you're
  also going to do is increase the friction that you have with your clients."* He names it directly:
  *"that's unfortunately just a sign of business immaturity."* Delivered with an explicit softener —
  "this is not an indictment on you and anybody else that was on that call." ★
- ★ **Arrogance, redefined.** *"What people assume to be arrogant is not what they do — it's like, no,
  that's what other people do. But if I come in ready to prescribe solutions to you, presuming I know
  a lot about your business and what your wants and needs are, and that everything I say is true —
  that's a pretty arrogant position."* The technique that avoids it: *"I ask certain questions thinking
  I know the answer, but I leave room for them to say something different. And if you frame it as an
  open-ended question they won't feel like you're leading them, so you have no pie on your face."*
  Illustrated with a courtroom analogy ("isn't it true you were there on the night of the fifth…"
  versus "so where were you on the night of the fifth?"). ★
- ★ **Neediness leaks into everything.** *"If you go in there feeling needy, like you have a lack
  mindset, it's going to skew your question, it's going to change your tone of voice, and you're going
  to sit there and think: what can I do to get Mo to sign on the dotted line."* And on impulsive
  discounting: *"well, it seems like Mo wasn't comfortable with 50, I'll drop it to 25 right now —
  this creates all kinds of strange signals to your prospect. It's like, wait a minute, is this all a
  game for you? This is my business. You just change prices and scope on the fly."* ★
- ★ **How you show people you care.** Mo recalls asking him this years earlier; Chris's answer:
  *"here's a dirty secret — cuz I genuinely care. It's that simple."* Paired with: *"I don't need to
  sell you today. In fact I prefer not to sell you. I got enough on my plate."* ★
- ★ **The gold-giveaway analogy for rejection.** Recalls a video of a man offering strangers an ounce
  of gold, who are suspicious and refuse. *"When you have something truly of value — you're literally
  handing out gold on the street and people don't want it — you don't feel rejected, you feel relieved.
  **But I don't think we internally believe that what we're doing is valuable.**"* ★
- **On giving it away free (channel/business).** *"I know all of you who are watching this are a little
  suspicious as to why I'm making content for free and laying all my cards on the table, unlike so
  many creators who are keeping the good stuff behind a gated paywall."* Followed by a transparent
  ask: *"my team has told me that less than 50% of the people who watch these videos actually
  subscribe… it's one metric that we care about and it's a measurement of value to you."*

  **I**mpact (what impact will this have on their business), **C**hallenge (what's getting in the way),
  **E**nd goal (what result do they want). Mo says so explicitly — *"I'm going to turn this question
  formula into a quick acronym"* — and predicts Chris will "remaster it." **Attribute ICE to Mo, not
  to Chris.**

  recap of Chris's prior teaching, not new material.
- Mo's reflection on the role-play — that Chris did "a lot of unselling yourself… clarifying my need,
  challenging my thinking, and then logic testing" — is a useful outside description of the method.
