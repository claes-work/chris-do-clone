# Leveling Up Your Business 2024 (Business Bootcamp pt. 1)

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do is the sole teaching voice.** A live session for alumni of The Futur's
> Business Bootcamp. Participants heard include **Abby**, **Ellen**, **Jason**, and **Igor** (a
> presentation designer; captions render "eigor"). Their contributions are context only.
> `attribution: sole-subject`.

## Summary

A "tune-up" session for people who have already taken the bootcamp — and, incidentally, the **most
candid and numerically specific account of the Brand Lab pivot anywhere in the corpus**.

The first third is a business-structure framework: every person in your company sits in **marketing
and sales**, **delivery/production**, or **other**; only the first two make money, and the third is
overhead to be minimised. He attaches a rule of thumb — **every dollar you pay someone should bring
back five** — and a diagnostic: are my people making me 5×?

He then distinguishes a **marketing problem** (not enough people knocking) from a **sales problem**
(can't close the ones who do), says he'd rather have the latter, and supplies a personal data point
that reframes his own coaching success: he never had a marketing problem — **two to three new-business
calls a week at $100–300K each** — which is why small tweaks from a coach compounded so fast. The
mechanism he names is **at-bats**: frequency creates patterns, and "if you were to go to the gym and
only work out once every six months, it's like you never worked out."

The heart of the session is **offer × market**, and the extended story he tells against himself: he
tried to launch a **$36,000-a-year mastermind** for creative entrepreneurs, couldn't write the sales
page for weeks, tried ChatGPT and got "a more generic version of this hot mess," and only solved it
when he spoke to a room of **mortgage brokers, loan officers and real-estate professionals** who came
up afterwards asking what they could buy. He describes having to let go of his attachment, knowing his
team and community would object — *"you turn your back on us, Chris is just chasing the money now"* —
and lands on the positioning line **"helping left-brainers think right."** He states his confidence in
the venture as **"a $3.6 million business venture at least."**

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-01-06

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **Three categories of people in a business — only two make money.** **(1) Marketing and sales**,
  **(2) delivery** (he prefers "production" but concedes "delivery" is "much nicer in the ear"),
  **(3) other** — "anything that's non-billable: legal, team admin, office manager, IT." *"Only these
  two make you money. This is an expense."* The advice, attributed to an unnamed coach who takes
  entrepreneurs "from seven to nine figures": reduce category three "down to as little as possible —
  do you really need that person full-time, can you do that on a fractional level, can you pay for a
  subscription service?" ★
- ★ **The 5× rule.** *"For every dollar you pay one of these people, they should help you make five.
  It's that simple. So if you pay someone $100,000 they should bring in half a million dollars in
  business."* His framing: "if you're on the payroll and you don't contribute to the way that the
  company makes money or profit, then you're an expense." And the ownership line he attaches: *"no
  matter what happens in your business, it doesn't matter if it's good or bad — it's your fault. It's
  your responsibility."* ★
- **Watch outflow against revenue, not revenue alone.** *"We have to look at our expenses relative to
  revenue all the time, cuz we don't want to just grow our revenue — we need to grow our profit."*
  Quotes a source rendered in captions as "Erol Garrison" (`attribution: uncertain`) to the effect
  that *if you don't manage your outflow, your inflow will be your downfall.*
- ★ **Marketing problem vs. sales problem — and why he prefers the latter.** *"Marketing is: I have a
  lot of people knocking at the door. A sales problem is: I don't know what to do with them when they
  come in and I can't close… Of the two I'd rather have a sales problem, because I can fix that."* His
  working presumption for most people: *"most of the time my presumption is you have a marketing
  problem, not a sales problem."* ★
- ★★ **Why coaching worked so well for him — the at-bats reframe (biography, self-reported, new
  numbers).** *"One of the reasons why I was very successful with my business coach was I didn't have
  a marketing problem — not at all. We had probably between two to three new-business calls every
  single week, and the average size of those was low six figures, between say $100,000 to $200,000,
  $300,000. Every single time somebody called us it was for a job of that size. And so what my
  business coach was able to do was just help me make small tweaks, because I knew in that week I was
  going to have another opportunity to take a swing of the bat and make an adjustment."* The general
  principle: *"when you're dealing with a frequency you start to develop patterns and habits… if you
  were to go to the gym and only work out once every six months, it's like you never worked out."* ★★
- ★ **Offer and market are inseparable — and the market comes first.** *"If I had a lot of gold to
  sell but you have no money and you're not interested in gold, it doesn't really matter — I don't
  care how good the offer is."* And the warning: *"we walk in with way too much attachment to what we
  think we have to do, [so] the opportunity will present itself and you will not see it."* The
  corrective: *"the clearer you can see who the market is **outside of what you want to offer**, the
  more likely you're going to succeed."* ★
- ★ **Sell the result, not the thing you make.** *"Most of us in our offer focus too much on what we
  do versus the result that we achieve. When you can shift your mindset away from what you do to the
  result that you can achieve for the client, it'll be much, much easier to sell."* And the market-fit
  version: *"what market finds this result to be valuable? You won't even have to sell it to them,
  because it'll be very apparent."* ★
- ★ **You cannot compete on the thing itself (dated 2024 example).** *"There's literally some people
  in India who cut your video for 12 bucks"* — corrected mid-sentence to seven — *"can you compete
  against $7? So if you just sell what you do, the video, you are screwed."* Yet others in the same
  market thrive: the difference is who they sell to and what result they name. ★
- ★★ **The Brand Lab / mastermind origin story — the fullest and most numerate version in the corpus
  (business/biography, self-reported).**
  - **The failed premise:** *"a little while ago I had this brilliant idea that somebody told me — to
    start a mastermind. I couldn't figure out who the person was… there must be enough creative
    entrepreneurs who want to do a mastermind with me and pay me **$36,000 a year**… And I was like,
    wait a minute, do I know enough entrepreneurs that are creative that do over a million dollars in
    business that want this from me? **Probably not.**"*
  - **His diagnosis of why creatives were the wrong market:** *"here's the problem with a lot of
    creative people: we suffer from some kind of poor relationship with money — that when we have it
    we don't want to spend it on anything… They don't want to hire anybody, they don't value coaching…
    they just want to rely on their craft, and that's why they get stuck in that million-dollar
    range."*
  - **The sales page that wouldn't come:** *"I'm struggling writing the sales page for weeks. The
    team's like, where's the sales page… if you try to write a long-form sales page to a person you
    don't understand, you're just throwing up garbage — and that's what I was doing, just straight hot
    garbage."* Then the AI attempt: *"I tried to help myself by using ChatGPT. I took all the copy I
    wrote and said rewrite this, and it just gave me a more generic version of this hot mess. **It
    didn't fix it, cuz it's built on a pile of garbage.**"*
  - **The accident:** speaking to a room of finance and real-estate people "who are not my tribe at
    all," who *"literally came up to me afterwards and said I want to buy something from you — what do
    you have for me? **I had nothing for them.**"* His read: *"the market told me this is what we want
    from you, and I had to let go of my attachment to what I thought I needed to do."*
  - **The market, described bluntly:** *"what they are are mortgage brokers, loan officers and
    real-estate professionals… they're super alpha broey types — even the women, they really all are…
    versus the meek timid creative person."* Their revenue: *"all these people do between a half
    million to $10 million a year — they can clearly afford it."*
  - **The internal cost he names in advance:** *"I also know that my community is like, you turn your
    back on us, Chris is just chasing the money now. But I have to make the tough decision."* And on
    his team: *"knowing that internally with my team they're not going to be happy about this."*
  - **The positioning line, and why it works:** *"finally we come up with a line — **helping
    left-brainers think right** — because I didn't realise this, but they all want to be creative."*
    The delivery insight: *"the market is hungry for a solution that's so easy for me to deliver on,
    because they experience this pain point — because they swim in the sea of same… For them, a little
    bit different is like two and a half degrees, and I know I can deliver that."* Contrasted with
    creatives: *"every single one of you are so divergent and different — you're like, screw the man, I
    don't want to be in a box."*
  - **The projection:** *"I'm super confident this is a **$3.6 million business venture at least**."*
    `[self-reported]`, forward-looking. ★★
- **A self-deprecating aside about teaching creatives (voice).** *"You fart a little font on them and
  they're like, oh my God that's genius, how'd you figure out that font, Chris? The colours amazing,
  the style super duper. I'm like — what am I doing wasting all my time with these creative people?"*
  Delivered as a joke against himself and his audience simultaneously.
- ★ **Sell to people who value their time — three worked examples (business).** (1) A man he coaches
  *"pays someone $3,000 a month to write a couple of tweets for him"* — probably one day's work,
  auto-scheduled — because *"I do not want to get on Twitter, but it's important to marketing my
  product that I be on Twitter."* (2) Someone editing one hour-long podcast a week with titling and
  posting, at *"$5,000 a month, $6,000 a month."* (3) **Alex Hormozi**'s content spend: *"every time I
  hear him say how much money he spends on content it blows my mind — and we're out here trying to get
  $4."* The lesson: *"it is not the thing that you do, it's solving the pain point of the person who's
  going to write you the check… **You just sell what you do to a different market**, because the
  result that they desire, they'll compensate you for it."* ★
- **On his own content spend.** *"I pay these guys to make videos for me, and if you start adding up
  all the people who work on our social content it's not that shocking — because I have a business
  that benefits by having content as the primary way of the top-of-funnel awareness."*
- ★ **The avatar exercise, with a deliberately uncomfortable instruction.** Five minutes, silently,
  create an avatar — *"and you have to divorce yourself from everything you knew up to this point,
  because what you're going to do is create an avatar of the exact same person you cannot sell to, who
  doesn't value what you do."* Then: *"you're going to tell me who you think it is, and all of us are
  going to argue with you."* ★
- ★ **His ChatGPT persona workflow, with an honest caveat (dated 2024).** *"You want to say: I want to
  build a buyer persona for a person who's looking for X in this market where they can afford to spend
  X, and then see what it gives you."* The realistic expectation: *"the cool thing about ChatGPT is
  it's kind of like grenades — you just got to get close… It'll give you hot garbage back. Okay, try
  again."* He credits **Daniel Priestley** for *"whatever ChatGPT tells you, just say: reflect on your
  answer and try to improve it"* — then immediately supplies the debunk from **his brother, a software
  engineer from Stanford**: *"it doesn't really think about it… what it's doing is it's re-rolling the
  variable again with adding some more data points. That's all it's doing. It's not really sentient."*
  > **New biographical detail:** SUBJECT.md records that he is the middle of three brothers; this is
  > the first source in the corpus to give a brother's profession — **a software engineer who studied
  > at Stanford**. Name not stated, and per SUBJECT.md family names are not public. ★
- **Programme note (business/biography).** The bootcamp has run *"seven to nine"* cycles over several
  years, usually three times a year, and *"the next cycle could be the very last boot camp we do,
  because it's just taken up too much of my time."* His stated intention for sessions like this one:
  *"I need to get you to where I am at mentally."*
- **On finding the right market as an alternative to selling.** *"If you can find your market then
  maybe you don't have to communicate your value, because your market understands the value… and if
  you're in the right market I believe they will value you and they will want to have a long-term
  meaningful relationship with you, so you're not just constantly chasing them down."*
