# How to Talk About Price or Budget Using Price Bracketing

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do is the sole teaching voice.** A short clip from a live workshop; an
> audience member, **Joe** (a developer), role-plays with him. Joe's lines are context.
> `attribution: sole-subject`.

## Summary

The most compact statement of **price bracketing** in the corpus — and it contains a **dated change of
practice** that matters.

The mechanics: say the price **verbally, before you show it**, using **big round numbers** and a
**range**, then follow with a question. The range is a sonar ping — *"you bounce the sound out, you see
how far the wall is"* — and you are listening for the reaction, not the answer.

The change: **he now says the bigger number first.** An audience member notices the reversal directly
("in the past you would say things like 50 to 100,000, but lately I've noticed you reversed the
number"), and he confirms it and gives the reason — anchoring. *"Say the bigger number first, pause,
let them absorb that number, and then say the second number."*

He also demonstrates the qualification he uses when he expects a mismatch (*"I'm going to say a number,
it's going to shock you — are you sitting down?"*), what to do when a bracket lands badly (address
resistance in the moment, **don't go build a bid**), and — in a flipped role-play where he plays the
vendor — how to reduce without collapsing, by re-scoping rather than discounting.

The closing section is about what happens after a no: helping someone you can't serve triggers
reciprocity, and *"that's why I have relationships that have lasted more than 20 years."*

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-02-05

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **Say it out loud before you put it in a document.** *"You've got to say a price before you show
  it. That's why I want to talk about it up front, verbally — and if it's not going to work, I want to
  know right now."* ★
- ★ **Big round numbers, in a bracket.** *"You want to say big round numbers and you want to give them
  a couple of numbers — it's called price bracketing… We don't want to be messed around. It could be
  10,000, it could be 50, it could be 200. I don't know, let's get into this."* And the purpose:
  *"you're listening for the reaction. It's like sonar — you bounce the sound out, you see how far the
  wall is. **When they don't fall out of their chair at 200, you know where your budget's coming in
  at.**"* ★
- ★★ **The reversal: bigger number FIRST (a dated change in his own practice).** Challenged on it by an
  audience member — *"in the past you would say things like 50 to 100,000, but lately I've noticed you
  reversed the number"* — he confirms: *"yes… it's due to the law of anchoring. When I say the bigger
  number first, they get stuck on the bigger number. It's weird, it's counterintuitive to say the
  bigger number first. **Say the bigger number first, pause, let them absorb that number, and then say
  the second number.**"* The effect: *"now when they want to pay you 150, they're going to think that's
  a bargain."* His promise: *"you will be surprised how quickly you can get your rate up. You will be
  smashing through ceilings that you didn't even know that you could get through."* ★★
- ★ **Anchor high — nowhere near your number.** Coaching Joe, who normally charges ~$10K and instinctively
  offered "10 to 20": *"if he's normally charging 10K, **do not start at 10 to 20. Start 30 to 60.**"*
  The rule: *"you want to anchor really high. **Don't anchor anywhere near your number** — that's how
  anchors work."* ★
- ★ **Qualify by shock when you expect a mismatch.** His actual script: *"I'm going to say a number,
  it's going to shock you — are you sitting down?… I know that you asked us to design a logo for
  $15,000; strategy for me starts at 30 to 50, and that doesn't even come with a logo. **Is that
  something you're going to be able to afford?**"* And when the answer is no: *"okay, all right — let
  me try and figure out something else for you that doesn't involve us."* ★
- ★ **A tell he reads before the call even starts.** *"I know a client who cannot afford me, cuz you
  know why? They're the owner and the founder. If the owner and the founder calls me, I know it's not
  going to work. I already know that — too small of a company."* A blunt, dated heuristic worth
  recording as-is. ★
- ★ **Address resistance in the moment — don't go away and build a bid.** *"You want to address any
  buyer resistance in the moment. **Don't build a bid.**"* Demonstrated: when the client says "that's a
  lot of money," Joe's instinct is to justify the value, and Chris stops him — *"how did Joe screw up
  already? **He sold.** You're not supposed to sell me. You cannot sell me."* ★
- ★★ **The flipped role-play — how to come down without collapsing.** Playing the vendor himself against
  a client who says $60–80K is out of budget: *"I'm sorry to hear that. **How much can you afford?**"*
  → "maybe 45." → *"You know what, maybe I overthought this. When we're talking I got really excited, I
  kind of thought about what's possible, and **maybe I'm building you something you don't need.** Let
  me think about this. So if I come back to you with a proposal for about 50K, are you okay with
  that?"* His own commentary: *"did I sell you anything? No. I just addressed what you said."* Note the
  move — he takes responsibility for over-scoping rather than discounting the same scope. ★★
- ★ **Helping people you can't serve pays back (the reciprocity/karma argument).** *"When you genuinely
  help people, you know what's happening — **the law of reciprocity**. You want to reciprocate. So you
  might know somebody who can afford us, and you'll say: I had this wonderful conversation with this
  robotic patient dude, you might want to give him a call, cuz he's a straight stand-up guy, he'll tell
  you the way it is. **That's why I have relationships that have lasted more than 20 years** — some of
  them come back. And here's the cool thing: this is like the **karma boomerang**. You throw it out,
  and sometimes when it comes back it comes back really big… That's why my network works for me."*
  Plus the aspiration he names: *"you ever have people that have worked with you before who are on the
  streets trying to sell for you, who talk about you in ways that would make you blush? That's what
  some of our clients do for us — and that's what you want."* ★
