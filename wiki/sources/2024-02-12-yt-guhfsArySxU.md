# 3 Steps To Get New Customers (FULL MASTERCLASS)

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do is the sole teaching voice.** An hour-long masterclass delivered to what
> is evidently a paying student audience (he references a **$27,000-a-semester** tuition in the room).
> Audience answers are context. `attribution: sole-subject`.

## Summary

A masterclass built on three moves: reframe what sales *is*, reframe what money *measures*, and then
the counter-intuitive one — **sell to whoever writes the cheque, not to whoever uses the thing.**

**Sales is change management.** *"You guys are helping someone to go from A to B"* — from a **baseline**
(where they are) to a **benchmark** (where they want to be, and how success will be measured). He names
the structural failure in design education directly: *"a lot of design institutions — **we define our
own benchmarks**, and so we don't know how to have a conversation about it with anybody else."*

**Money measures transformation.** Against the creative's discomfort — *"money feels gross to us
because it makes it feel transactional"* — he offers: *"**money is a measurement for the amount of
transformation you create.**"* Then proves it on the room's own tuition: you paid $27,000 because the
transformation you expect is worth more than the money.

**Sell to the payer.** The section that makes this source distinctive. Illustrated three ways: a
Japanese YouTuber teaching English who reportedly makes **$10 million a year** (parents pay, not the
students); Chris's own creative director asking him to buy a $3,000 course (*"when he says we, he means
me"*); and **Johnny Cupcakes** arming attendees with materials to *"convince your employer."*

And then the admission that gives the whole section its weight: **"We have a huge problem at The
Futur. We sell to the end buyer and they're all broke. That's why we're not doing $10 million a
year."**

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-02-12

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★★ **Sales is change management.** *"Sales is change management. You guys are helping someone to go
  from A to B… their current state, where they are now, to a desired future state, where they'd like to
  be."* The two anchors: *"we'll call this the **baseline** — where you are — and where would you like
  to be? It's the **benchmark**. How will we measure success? How will we know we got there?"* His claim
  for it: *"once you master these concepts, your conversations around sales will be much, much easier."*
  ★★
- ★★ **Why design school leaves you unable to have that conversation.** *"Unfortunately, a lot of design
  institutions — **we define our own benchmarks**, and so we don't know how to have a conversation about
  it with anybody else. That's really the challenge here… So many of you that go to school have been
  trained to do creative visual arts. **You don't learn this part, and so for the rest of your business
  life you're going to suffer.**"* ★★
- ★ **An AI-era warning, stated bluntly.** *"If you're in the business of **doing things and selling the
  doing of things**, you may be in trouble, and you may be threatened by what is coming — if it's not
  already here."* Consistent with the taste/discernment thesis elsewhere in the corpus, but framed here
  as a business-model risk rather than a craft one. ★
- ★ **Problems are opportunities, sized by the problem.** *"We're looking for big problems, where
  problems are opportunities to make money. **The bigger the problem, the more money you make.**"* ★
- ★★ **Money as a measurement of transformation.** *"Money feels gross to us because it makes it feel
  transactional — I make you art… for money. But I'll tell you the thing about money: **money is a
  measurement for the amount of transformation you create.**"* The proof he runs on the room itself:
  *"the reason why some people in this room have agreed to pay $27,000 a semester is because the kind of
  transformation they see is greater than the amount of money they're going to give up."* And the
  decision logic: *"if it's worth more [you buy]. If it's worth the same, the decision is harder. If
  it's worth less, you probably will not buy — unless you were pressured to."* With a wry aside about
  who is actually paying: *"some of us are very fortunate to have very wealthy parents. **I don't blame
  you — it's not your fault that you're rich.**"* ★★
- ★★ **Sell to the person who writes the cheque, not the person who uses it.** The principle: *"if you
  can market to the ultimate buyer of something that's not the end customer, **it's much easier to
  sell**."* Because *"we can be more generous for others than we can to ourselves."* Applied: *"if you
  sell massage packages, don't sell to the person — sell to their wives, their husbands, their parents,
  their siblings, their children. **Give the gift of healing** versus buy the gift of healing for
  yourself."* ★★
- ★★ **The admission that lands it (business/biography, self-reported).** *"**We have a huge problem at
  The Futur. We sell to the end buyer and they're all broke. That's why we're not doing $10 million a
  year.** I had to figure out how to solve that problem."* > This is the most direct statement in the
  corpus of the *commercial* logic behind the Brand Lab pivot — read alongside
  [[2024-01-06-yt-aSzZiCp9GWg]] and [[2024-02-09-yt-QH6b8mMEP2g]], where the same pivot is framed more
  in terms of market discovery and mission. Here he simply says the old market can't pay. ★★
- ★ **The Japanese English-teacher case (business, self-reported anecdote).** Met at a YouTube event —
  *"they seem so light, like they're walking on air… what I imagine what it's like to be Prince when
  you walk in a room."* He teaches English on YouTube and reportedly makes **$10 million a year**, has
  used it to **buy physical schools** and self-publish books that alone do *"a million dollars a year."*
  Chris's read: *"what an incredible empire he's building"* — a funnel from channel → courses →
  brick-and-mortar → required books. Average customer pays **~$10,000**, and *"what kid can pay that?
  No kid can pay that. **Freaking parents again.**"* ★
- ★ **His own example, told against himself.** A former creative director, **Matthew**, pitched him a
  $3,000 course-design programme: *"when he says **we**, he means **me**… I'm like, okay, three grand,
  write the cheque."* ★
- ★ **The leapfrog play, credited to Johnny Cupcakes.** For pricey workshops aimed at people without
  budget authority: *"**convince your employer.**"* Chris generalises it to creative services: *"the
  person who makes the decision is not the one who writes the cheque. It's the CEO who writes the
  cheque, the CFO — but the **chief marketing officer** is the person you need to convince. So you have
  to give them materials or an argument as to why we need to work with [you]."* ★
- ★ **The refund asymmetry (an honest, slightly uncomfortable observation).** *"They never ask for a
  refund, cuz there's a disconnect between the person who sold and the person who received the goods…
  it wasn't my money, I didn't even care — and they don't want to go back to the person who paid."*
  Then, notably, he tells it from the *payer's* side, as himself: *"as a boss, I sign cheques… sometimes
  when people ask me to enrol in a program, I always think: what did you learn? **You've changed
  nothing. The company has not made more money. And I can't get my money back.**"* ★
- **Money buys happiness when you give it away.** *"They've done studies on this. I give you 10 bucks,
  you go spend it, the happiness goes away really fast. But if I give you 10 bucks with the rule that
  you must give it to someone else, **you get way more joy**… every time you spend that 10 bucks to buy
  the same thing, your joy decreases over time. But every time you give 10 bucks, the joy either is the
  same or grows."*
- **A candid note on his own practice.** Of the sell-to-the-payer principle: *"**I currently do not sell
  this way.** It's an observation I've made recently. That's what's on my mind."* And on where it sits
  relative to a design education: *"probably nothing you've heard at ArtCenter."*
