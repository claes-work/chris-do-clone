# 5 Passive Income Ideas to Make $10,000 Per Month in 2024

## Summary

Despite the clickbait title, this is a systematic teach on **building revenue that isn't tied to your
hours** — and it is the single most numerically transparent source in the corpus about The Futur's own
YouTube economics.

The frame: assume you will lose ~25% of your business, and build a second curve to fill it. When the
alternative revenue reaches **50%** of the original, *"you have to make a big boy decision"* — invest
further or let it run in the background. Either way: **choice is powerful.**

The engine is a two-step. **Step one: buy back your time.** He walks the coachee through brutal
arithmetic — $2,000 for four weeks of editing is ~$100/day, ~$12.50/hour, *"less than you would make at
Starbucks"* — establishes the LA market day rate for a good editor (**~$500**), establishes what the
coachee would need to charge (**~$850**), and names the spread as the whole business model. **Step two:
become the agency** — the agent connecting **buyers** of creative services to **makers** — which
requires accepting risk, managing a talent gap, and shedding every non-essential role.

He then stress-tests his own advice with a long digression on **where the value really sits**: a
friend pushed out of a nine-year partnership took **100% of the clients** with him. *"Where's the
value? In the relationships."* Which is precisely the danger of hiring someone to own lead-gen and
sales.

The back half is the passive-income ladder: **AdSense** (with real CPM economics), **affiliate
marketing** (including the double-dip play of converting affiliate performance into sponsorship),
**speaking** (via the "stage effect"), **consulting and coaching**, then **productising** — book,
course, frameworks, tools — with value stacking and a refund allowance built into the price.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-02-15

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **Build the second curve before you need it.** *"This is something I warn everybody about and they
  never heed the warning, and they call me in a state of panic after the fact… clients go away,
  industries change, disruptive technologies emerge to take away your revenue source, and then you're
  caught holding the bag."* Design for a ~25% loss. And the 50% decision point: *"you can say to
  yourself, should I invest more resources into this alternative business, or just keep doing what I'm
  doing and let this thing chill in the background? **That's a great thing, because you have choice.
  Choice is powerful.**"* ★
- ★★ **Time for money vs. money for money.** *"Historically most of us exchange time for money… but
  what I found, especially in certain communities — let's just call them rich people — is that people
  **trade money for more money.**"* Examples worked through: stocks (with the volatility/risk-reward
  relationship), and rental property. His conclusion: *"your job right now is to make enough money that
  you have surplus money, that then you can use to invest in other vehicles."* ★★
- ★ **A self-deprecating investing confession (biography, self-reported).** On selling Apple stock:
  *"this brilliant genius sold his stock in 2008… my financial advisor says don't do it, I'm like, bro,
  get me out, the market's terrible… when it got back to where I thought, this is it, I'm out — Steve
  Jobs is going to die tomorrow, this company's going to [fail]. **I did not see the genius in Tim Cook
  at that time.** Don't do as I do, do as I say."* Also relays that when he asked **Gary Vaynerchuk**
  his best-ever investment, the answer was **Twitter** — *"like a 100× return."* ★
- ★★ **Phase one is buying back your time.** *"The first thing you do when you have more money is you
  buy back your time."* The arithmetic he forces on the coachee: $2,000 for four weeks ≈ $100/day ≈
  **$12.50/hour** — *"essentially right now you're making less than you would if you were working at
  Starbucks. **We have to have this healthy honest dialogue with ourselves about what we're really
  earning versus the fairy tale that we tell ourselves.**"* Then the market anchors: a good LA editor
  is **~$500/day**; the coachee would need **~$850/day** to be worth booking over the average. *"The
  spread between this number and this number is what is going to drive your decision as to whether you
  need to hire someone."* And the trap: *"I'm getting $850 a day, why would I give some [of it] away
  for 500? …So you hold on to the money and you give up your time."* ★★
- ★ **The identity shift, stated explicitly.** Prompted by Mo, he names it: the move is *"from a sole
  proprietorship, or solopreneur, **to an entrepreneur** — to run an agency where he's the agent for a
  lot of different talented people, and he's a connector."* You are no longer selling that *you* do the
  work; you are selling *the business that achieves the result*. He also declines a tangent honestly:
  *"I'm not going to get into the ethical, moral debates about whether or not you can profit from other
  people's labour — that's a video for another hour."* ★
- ★ **Why the margin is legitimate: you carry the risk.** *"Why is it risky to hire this person? What if
  they flake out? What if they don't do the work? …You can't go back to your client and say I hired the
  wrong person — **they're going to say, that's your problem, not my problem.**"* And a good instruction
  on not over-explaining: *"you don't even need to tell them [about the markup]. Telling them says I'm
  new in business and I don't know what I'm doing, or I'm going to patronise you and teach you how
  business is done. **Chances are, if they could afford to hire you, they're a better business person
  than you are.**"* ★
- ★ **The talent gap, and how to close it.** Talented people often lack opportunity, or don't want
  clients, management or risk. So *"you need to coach them up through art direction or creative
  direction."* His progression: shadow → a paid one-day test with review → repeated tests until
  *"it becomes abundantly clear this person is easy to work with, takes direction well, is prompt, has
  great communication skills, has a great freaking attitude"* → semi-permanent booking → full-time.
  Then promote the one showing leadership to manage the others. ★
- ★ **Shed every non-essential role.** *"Non-essential is a role that only you can do right now.
  Everybody's going to think, oh, it's got to be me to do all these things — **it doesn't.**"* What
  actually can't be delegated: *"lead gen, sales, strategy, maybe general vision for the company,
  mapping out where you're going to be in the next two to three years."* ★
- ★★ **But that's exactly where the danger is (the friend's story).** Asked whether you could instead
  hire *someone else* to do lead gen and sales, he answers with a caution: *"where is the value in the
  value chain — someone closer to the buyer or someone closer to the maker? **The buyer.** So if you
  hire people to do lead gen, sales, strategy and vision, **they have all the power**… soon you'll be
  working for them."* Then the case: a friend who spent **nine years** building a company he was
  promised a stake in raised the succession question, and was cut off — emails shut, no access to his
  own client relationships. He contacted his ~100 former clients through another channel. Chris polls
  the room (90%? 75%?) and reveals: *"**100%.**"* His conclusion: *"we think because there's a label on
  a company with a federal tax ID number that the value is there. **Where's the value? In the
  relationships.** In a service-based business that's almost always the case."* And the mitigation, with
  its limits named: NDAs, non-competes, non-poaching and non-solicit clauses — *"but you're dependent
  on some pieces of paper to make this work… **you're running a creative business, not a legal
  service.**"* ★★
- ★ **Build a bench, not a dependency.** Create a card per maker: skill rating, price tier, primary and
  secondary specialisation, availability, personality notes, link to work. *"You cannot rely on one or
  two people to do the work."*
- ★★ **The Futur's own YouTube economics (business/biography, self-reported — unusually specific).**
  On CPM: *"our channel does about **$28** or something like that."* On monthly AdSense: *"we do
  currently about **15K a month**"* (Mo supplies the figure) — *"so if you multiply that by 12… **180,000**."*
  And the peak: *"the most we've ever made in a single year was **360,000**. That's just AdSense."* The
  factors he names: audience wealth (North America/Europe), and topic — finance, investment and real
  estate audiences reach *"$36 CPM"* while video-game streaming might make *"$2 per thousand."* Hence:
  *"you need to have content that teaches people fundamentally business skills on how they can grow and
  make money."* ★★
- ★ **Affiliate marketing, and the double-dip.** How it works (Amazon ~4–5% rising with volume;
  **24-hour cookie**, so unrelated purchases count). A friend runs *"an entire business model built on
  affiliate marketing"* doing **$1 million/year** with six or seven overseas writers, top-tier on Adobe
  subscriptions and holding a direct relationship with Adobe. Then the move Chris says newly clicked
  for him: once your affiliate payouts prove you move the needle, **call the brand and ask them to
  sponsor you** — *"now you get a double dip… you started with an exchange of value first. If you're
  making 5K, you know that they're making 50K or 500K, and **it's proof of concept — you're literally
  giving them money.**"* Versus the usual approach: *"so many of us are too busy [asking] can you
  sponsor me — no, who are you again?"* With an ethical limit: *"do not get greedy here. **Only promote
  and link to products that you like and use.** This is critical… you will screw over the relationship
  with your consumers."* ★
- ★ **The blog/SEO loop.** The blog page *"is not really for readers, but it is for SEO, and it's also
  to warm your audience up for leads,"* with affiliate links and a CTA. AI transcription has made this
  nearly free: *"in the old days — I'm talking about six months ago — you'd have to pay a transcription
  service."* Goal: *"top 10 organic, which we rank for for many, many terms."* His self-assessment:
  *"**but we're stupid — we just want to work really hard for no money.**"* And the shape he's after:
  *"I try to design ecosystems that, once you figure it out, it's infinity."* ★
- ★ **Speaking, and "the stage effect."** Credited to a name rendered "Erica needs" (`attribution:
  uncertain`): *"the quality of your presentation **times** the size of your audience equals your level
  of attraction."* Hence *"this allure that speakers have."* A good near-term target: *"$10,000 to
  speak, plus travel and hotel."* The compounding: you stop chasing opportunities and they arrive —
  bookings, sponsorship, hiring, team training. ★
- ★ **Consulting vs. coaching, and the rate jump.** *"When you're a consultant you're going to charge
  $1,000 **an hour**, not a day."* He separates **one-to-one coaching** (may not command that rate),
  **group coaching**, and consulting. And on the honesty of the label: *"these are alternative ways to
  make money — **not all of these ideas are passive income.** This you still have to work for… but now
  you're exchanging less time for way more money."* ★
- ★ **Productising, and value stacking.** Document the patterns → white paper, ebook, or book → each
  builds authority and feeds the loop → frameworks and mental models → worksheets, tools, a course.
  Cites ***Rework*** (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson): *"when you create one thing you create
  many things in the process — we often forget the other things that we create and we only sell the one
  thing."* The offer maths, credited to **Hormozi** as *"value stacking"*: six modules at an hour each
  against a $1,000/hour consulting rate = "$6,000 of value," struck through, *"or you can get it for
  $499."* Plus a **guarantee** with the refunds priced in: *"if you assume 10% will refund, just build
  it into the price… smart business people account for these kinds of things."* ★
- ★ **The Charles S. Anderson story — assets as a retirement plan.** The designer repurposed
  out-of-copyright 1920s–30s advertising illustration, built the Fossil watch look and the French Paper
  Company promotions on it, then hired illustrators and turned the work into a **licensable stock
  catalogue** — which a Getty or Shutterstock might eventually buy outright. *"That is an awesome way
  for Charles to build a 401k plan based on assets he uses today."* His lesson: *"we're leaving almost
  literally all these valuable things on the table… really look into what you use to create — your
  desktop, your swipe files."* ★
- **Every asset is also a lead magnet (Mo's meta-point, endorsed).** Every document produced can become
  the sample or tool that pulls people back into the funnel — *"this constant sawdust example."*
- **On whether it's scripted.** Closing: *"was this all pre-scripted, or was this just — as my friend Mo
  likes to say — **off the dome**? This is how my mind works. I obsess over trends and connect dots
  that people just don't see."*
