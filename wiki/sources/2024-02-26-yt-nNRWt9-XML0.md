# 4 Ways To Get Your First 10 Customers (41mins Full Class)

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do is the teaching voice.** A hot-seat coaching session with **Chris
> Franklin** (agency: BIV), with **Mo** contributing. The coachee's lines are context.
> `attribution: sole-subject (teaching)`. Companion to [[2024-02-15-yt-HJ9IW1w2bDc]] and
> [[2024-02-19-yt-fIZD4KHxcow]], which coach the same person.

## Summary

A diagnosis-then-system session, and unusually candid because the coachee's numbers are on the table:
**$297K in 2022 → $151K in 2023**, a ~50% collapse driven by losing one client who represented **50%
of revenue**.

Chris's diagnosis is structural, not motivational. He maps the funnel — marketing → leads → prospects
→ sales → onboarding → delivery → customer service/follow-up — asks where the coachee's time actually
went (*"95… let's be real"* → delivery), and names it: **working *in* the business rather than *on*
it.** He then points at the cheapest available fix, which sits at the *end* of the funnel: asking
existing clients for referrals at the moment they say thank you.

The core teaching is **Hormozi's Core Four** — cold outreach, warm outreach, **content**, referrals —
with a long comic detour in which the coachee cannot guess "content" despite running a YouTube
channel (*"it's literally in your blind spot"*). Then the three levers that turn attention into leads:
**highlight the problem**, **give a sample**, **give a tool** — each grounded in a mechanism (Cialdini's
reciprocity, loss aversion, the freemium/Costco model).

The session's most valuable single idea arrives late and is credited to **Noah Kagan**: instead of
asking what the client wants, ask **what are they trying to avoid?** Chris then runs it live on the
event-photography market and produces the reframe that lands for the coachee — stop selling video,
start selling the avoidance of an in-house team, overhead, liability insurance and workers' comp.

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-02-26

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★ **Client concentration is the underlying risk.** One client at 50% of revenue: *"that's the danger
  zone — usually shouldn't be more than 10%."* And the market signal: *"some companies won't hire you
  if any one client represents more than 20%, because it's too unstable. Really big clients… ask to
  see your client list and what percentage of your revenue, **because you represent a financial risk
  for them.**"* He also removes the personalisation: clients vanish *"for a number of different reasons,
  nothing to do with you… there's a change in management, or they decide our priorities are going to be
  totally different and whole departments get wiped out."* ★
- ★ **In the business vs. on the business.** With ~95% of time in delivery, the top of the funnel
  starves. He includes himself: *"this is where a lot of us — myself included — I recognise the pain
  because **I've been a perpetrator of this to my own business.**"* ★
- ★★ **The referral ask, scripted (his most immediately usable material here).** Timing first: ask when
  the client says thank you, *"because they're in a state of gratitude"* — and the mechanism is
  reciprocity, *"we'd like to reciprocate."* The script, credited to **Phil Jones**'s *Exactly What to
  Say*: *"you **wouldn't** happen to know one or two people who could use our services… would you?"* —
  deliberately a **no-question**, because (crediting **Chris Voss**) *"when we say no we're in control,
  or we have the perception of control."* Then: *"don't worry, I'm not going to ask you for that
  information now — but if you were to think of something, who came to mind?"* Then: *"**would it be
  too much for me to ask** if you could mention my name and tell them your experience with us? And I'd
  love to follow up with you in a week."* And the follow-up opener, also from Jones: *"**I bet that you
  haven't made that call yet**"* — which reliably produces "you're right, I didn't… it's my bad, I gave
  you my word, I'm going to take care of it right now." His illustration of why timing matters: ask for
  a favour right after you've given someone a backrub, not three weeks later. ★★
- ★ **The Core Four, credited to Alex Hormozi.** **Cold** — advertising, purchased lists, cold call /
  email / DM (*"this is going to be the most difficult thing for creative people… this feels like
  door-to-door salesman time"*). **Warm** — some existing familiarity. **Content** — the one the coachee
  cannot name despite running a channel: *"it's literally in your blind spot."* Plus referrals. And
  Hormozi's definition of a lead, which Chris likes for its simplicity: *"somebody that you can get in
  touch with. That's it."* Hence the honest caveat about his own audience: *"all the people who watch
  our YouTube channel — we can't really get in touch with them, because I don't have their personal
  information."* ★
- ★★ **Three levers to convert attention into leads.**
  1. **Highlight the problem** — *"when somebody knows their problem it agitates the pain, they desire
     the solution more."* His worked example is bacteria in a kitchen drain: educate someone into a
     problem they didn't know they had, and they go looking for a fix.
  2. **Give a sample** — the **Costco model** / freemium. Underpinned by **Cialdini**'s *Influence* and
     the **law of reciprocity**, which he illustrates with a car wash that left free microfibre cloths
     in his car (*"I was like, I need to give a tip now"*) and with restaurants serving a complimentary
     sweet *"right before they give you the bill… this has proven to increase the tip amount by quite a
     bit."* His honest note: *"even though we know they're doing this thing, we can't help it, because
     this is how the fabric of our society is glued together."* Plus **loss aversion** — a trial that
     expires, or a paywall that lets you read part of the article until *"eventually you're like, fudge,
     I'll just pay you the 30 bucks… and chances are after you pay, you don't even read it at all."*
  3. **Give a tool** — digital and scalable: a calculator, a script generator, a diagnostic, or a quiz
     (he names **ScoreApp**, a disclosed partner, and the BuzzFeed "which character are you" era as the
     model). ★★
- ★★ **"What are they trying to avoid?" — credited to Noah Kagan (the session's best idea).** *"He gave
  me this really cool prompt: what kinds of things are they **avoiding**?"* Chris's gloss: *"there's
  already financial, emotional, professional, personal pain attached to things that we're trying to
  avoid."* He runs it live on event organisers and speakers — avoiding looking cheap, attendees having
  a bad time, low ticket sales, losing money, the technical setup nobody wants to touch — and then
  demonstrates the pivot: *"if you say **hey, I take photos, do you want to buy photos?** — no, nobody
  cares… because you're not tying your solution to a problem or a pain they feel."* Versus: *"how is
  your marketing going? Are you generating enough sales for your next event? Do you think having assets
  to share before, during and after would help you sell more tickets and create a more premium
  experience?"* ★★
- ★★ **Applied to his own buying behaviour (a rare first-person disclosure of why he outsources).**
  *"The reason why we contract out our social media content editing and production is because I'm
  trying to avoid **not** doing that… I'm trying to avoid managing a whole internal team who doesn't
  specialise in this, who isn't up on the latest trends. I'm trying to avoid **overhead, liability
  insurance, workers' compensation.**"* And the sales conversation that would actually work on him:
  *"do you produce lots of videos? How do you do this currently? What does that cost you on an annual
  basis? What about insurance, bonuses, vacation, sick leave, maternity leave? What happens when
  they're not up to speed — you have to pay for them to train. **What if I could take that away from
  you?**"* ★★
- ★ **The Tokyo→Kyoto metaphor.** *"If we had to go from Tokyo to Kyoto, you can take a speed train and
  I put you in a sensory deprivation chamber… you would arrive all the same, but you'd go bananas. What
  if instead I put you in a premium suite — you can recline, someone asks what you want to eat, you see
  Mount Fuji? **The destination changes for you. Your memories change.** If you just say I'm going to
  sell you a ticket from Tokyo to Kyoto, **you're selling the wrong thing.**"* ★
- ★ **Funnel arithmetic, and what counts as a prospect.** 100 leads → ~10 qualified prospects → ~50%
  close → 5 clients, so *"you know how many people you need to reach out to on a regular basis."* His
  qualification bar: a prospect *"has a problem that you can solve, has the money, and there's urgency
  behind the request."* And a blunt exclusion: *"someone is not a prospect to me if their timeline is so
  far out that it's three years later — **I might not even be here in three years.**"* Plus the close-rate
  difference: warm/content-sourced leads convert *"75%"* by the coachee's own estimate, cold outreach
  *"might drop down to 10%."* ★
- **Marketing vs. sales, distinguished.** *"Marketing is something that's scalable — that's the
  difference. Sales is finite: constraints in terms of time and day and people you can actually call."*
- **On content's role.** *"The content in this scenario is king or queen — is royalty."* And the
  efficient version: one piece can carry all three levers at once — a problem, a free sample, and a quiz
  to see if you're a good fit.
- **A meta-move he names on air.** Closing the session: *"to get super meta about it, this is a sample
  of things that we do all the time in the Futur Pro Group… I just gave you an example of how to
  generate leads by highlighting the problem, the sample and the tool"* — i.e. the episode is itself
  the demonstration of its own framework.
