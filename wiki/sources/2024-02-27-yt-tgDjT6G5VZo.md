# Brand Strategy For Designers: How to Get Started (Part 1)

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do is the sole teaching voice.** A live workshop in Miami; audience members
> answer prompts and one (**Liz**) is credited for a definition. Their contributions are context.
> `attribution: sole-subject`. Contains two sponsor reads (Teachable).

## Summary

The clearest account in the corpus of **how Chris actually learned brand strategy** — told as two
origin stories, then a case study, then definitions.

**Origin one: envy.** He watched **Yo Santosa** of **Ferroconcrete** — an ArtCenter graduate from the
same programme, years after him — do work he couldn't do. Her Pinkberry branding (built on an
insight drawn from high-end cosmetics photography: clean, white, minimal, luxurious) covered copy,
website, signage. His reaction, stated plainly: *"I was just really intrigued by it because… I was
just semi-intrigued and really just jealous — like, how do you do that? Did we go to the same school,
and did you have some secret sauce?"* He interviewed her to extract the method and she **couldn't
articulate it** — *"that's okay, cuz I figured it out."*

**Origin two: Jose.** **Jose Caballer** brought user-experience thinking from Silicon Valley and the
**CORE** framework, which Chris says he influenced the shaping of. The epiphany was the **user
profile**: *"throughout my years at ArtCenter and after ArtCenter, for almost 20 years at that point,
I didn't understand that we're designing for someone."* Their philosophical disagreement is recorded
too: Jose started with brand voice, tone and culture; Chris insisted on **user first** — *"it's much
easier for the brand to understand the needs of the users and create products for them than it is to
say we have a product, now let's go find a user."*

**The case study** is Tango, an IT expense-management company, and it is the most complete
brand-strategy walkthrough in the corpus: two insights (their language was impenetrable; they held
their customers **in disdain**), the reframe (**the IT person is the superhero**, not the necessary
evil), and everything that flowed from it — the logo sold on a single line, the copy, the Spider-Man
riff, the growth from under $100K to a quarter-million engagement.

Then the definitions section, where he credits **Marty Neumeier** properly and separates **brand**
(a result, a gut feeling) from **branding** (the act of shaping it).

## Key claims — Chris Do, dated 2024-02-27

_Paraphrased unless quoted._

- ★★ **How he got into brand strategy — envy, then reverse-engineering (biography, self-reported).**
  On Yo Santosa's Pinkberry work: *"it opened my mind as to what designers could do."* On his own
  limits at the time: *"for a very long time we're a design production company — we work with
  advertising agencies, so they did the strategy, they would write the copy, we would figure out
  creative ways to bring that to life in video and animation, **but we didn't do the strategic
  part.**"* His method: *"I'm very logical, I break things down… I started to study it and try to find
  the recipe for it. I even interviewed her and asked her all these questions… **but she struggled
  with telling me how she did what she did.**"* ★★
- ★ **The purist line on who actually does "branding."** *"For me, this is what graphic designers do:
  we design identity systems, not branding. I know it's a very popular phrase — 'I do branding.' **No
  you don't. If you don't shape the entire customer experience from point A to point B, then you're
  not really doing branding — you're using that as a buzzword because you think you can charge more
  money.**"* With a fair caveat: *"there's nothing really wrong with that, you can call yourself
  whatever you want — but if you're a purist… you're misrepresenting."* ★
- ★★ **The CORE framework and the user-profile epiphany (biography — dates a major gap in his own
  education).** Jose Caballer showed him CORE, which sits *"somewhere between the brand, the user and
  the business goals."* Chris's admission: *"throughout my years at ArtCenter and after ArtCenter for
  almost 20 years at that point, **I didn't understand that we're designing for someone.**"* The
  airline example he gives — you design not just for the end user but for the travel agent, the
  airline itself, and partners. *"When you design for someone, it really shapes how you think about
  the design… that was a big epiphany for me."* And the self-deprecating admission that follows:
  *"I'm semi-ashamed or guilty of designing websites as digital brochures. I've done this before and
  you should spank me for it later."* ★★
- ★ **Where he and Jose disagreed (a documented philosophical split).** *"Jose would start with
  defining the brand voice and the tone and the culture of the brand. [But] if we're doing user
  experience design, shouldn't we be focused on the user first? He and I would butt heads on this."*
  His reasoning stands: brands can learn users' needs and build for them more easily than they can
  retrofit a user to an existing product. ★
- **On learning in public.** *"I'm the kind of person who learns a little bit and I'm super dangerous,
  cuz I go telling people I'm going to do this now — and then you get hired for it, and then you
  panic and you break out in a cold sweat."*
- ★★ **The Tango case study — insight one: admit you don't understand (sales-clients/facilitation).**
  In a 6–8 hour executive workshop, hours in, drowning in acronyms: *"if you don't understand
  something you have to be **brave enough** to say: I do not understand what you're saying."* His
  actual words to the room: *"hey everybody, **you guys are talking inside baseball.** I do not know
  what you're talking about. You need to explain this to me like I'm your mom, or a fifth grader —
  cuz I'm the guy who has to write the copy and build the stuff that people use. **I suspect you're
  having problems with your website, with your identity, with your communication, because you're
  speaking very inside baseball.**"* The result: *"to my surprise and delight they all just laughed,
  and there was a big sigh in the room, as if I just let the pressure valve open."* ★★
- ★★ **Tango — insight two: they held their customers in disdain.** *"They considered them a necessary
  evil."* Chris connects it directly to his own audience: *"some of you think your clients get in the
  way of your ability to do good work. **That's a horrific, combative way of looking at the people who
  make what you do possible.**"* The reframe that unlocked the project: at the core of every
  21st-century business is the IT person, *"blamed for everything, they get no credit… **they're not
  the hero of every operation, they're the superhero.**"* And the observable effect: *"for the first
  time you can see the energy shift from disdain to deep empathy and gratitude."* ★★
- ★ **How the logo was actually sold.** He had the designer (**Emily**) cut the descender of a
  two-storey G flat, then presented it with one line: *"you guys are about the bottom line."* Response:
  *"show us no more logos, we're done."* The generalisation: *"sometimes a little customer insight
  turns into a design expression that is abstract but has meaning to them — and they can then take
  that and sell it all the way through, through marketing, through the CEO and every CFO."* ★
- ★ **He discovered he could write copy (biography, self-reported).** *"Something had happened to me
  when I was working on this project. I realised **I thought I got into design to escape words and
  copy** — but I was just like, I think I can write copy now. This is kind of fun."* His stated
  standard: *"single-syllable words, fifth-graders can understand — spend less, do more."* Examples
  from the project: *"IT expenses out of control, gain visibility of shadow IT"*; *"super official vs.
  super fiscal"*; and the Spider-Man inversion — *"**with great visibility comes great power**"* — plus
  *"the diligent dollar defender"* and *"titan of IT."* ★
- ★ **The engagement compounded.** Logo → identity → website → copywriting → video. *"The engagement I
  think started under $100,000 and quickly blew into a quarter-million-dollar [project]. They paid
  $100,000 just for the video itself."* The mechanism he names: *"once you get to know the client's
  needs better than they can articulate it, you're in real deep at this point."* ★
- ★ **The hug, and what it told him (biography — a genuine before/after).** The client at the first
  break: *"Chris, you've been able to do more in hours than we have been able to do in months."* And at
  the end: *"they'll stand up and hug you — privately, not all together… they just lean in and give you
  a hug."* His reflection: *"**I've been working in commercial production, working with ad agencies for
  years at this point — none of them ever gave me a hug**, because they saw me as a person who made
  things, not as a partner trying to help them facilitate their goals and solve a big problem."* ★
- ★ **Brand vs. branding, defined.** Credits **Marty Neumeier** — *"a professional friend, colleague,
  and now I think personal friend too"* — for *"a brand is a person's gut feeling about a product,
  service, organisation… **it's what you say it is, it's what they say it is**,"* plus the *Brand Flip*
  addition: *"when enough people share a gut feeling, then you have a brand."* Chris presses the
  obvious follow-up himself — how many is enough? — and answers *"more than half… 51%, that's your
  brand."* And then the distinction, credited on air to an audience member named **Liz**: *"if this is
  the brand, then **branding is how we influence or shape all that.**"* ★
- **Products vs. brands.** *"Products are made in factories, but brands are built in the hearts and
  minds of people."* Also cites **Jeff Bezos**: *"your brand is what people say when you're not in the
  room — it's your reputation."*
- ★ **You cannot fake a brand any more.** *"This is why you can't create a false brand, because people
  are going to see right through it almost immediately — because we live in a new time when companies
  no longer control our perceptions because of social media. Good news travels fast, bad news travels
  even faster. **We the consumer now are taking over the brand. We own the brand.**"* ★
- ★★ **The Ford Aerostar story — the fake-brand proof (biography, self-reported, new and vivid).**
  Setting up "quality is job one" as a false claim, he tells it against his own family: *"I'm an
  immigrant, a refugee from Vietnam. My parents, when they came here — we escaped communism — my
  parents were so grateful for America. My dad [would say] **buy American, buy American.**"* So they
  bought a first-year **Ford Aerostar** minivan. In high school, being dropped at a fancy French
  restaurant for a French Club dinner, mortified and asking his mother to park in the darkness:
  *"I flip the door open, the sliding door, and **the whole door falls off.** Quality is job number
  one. My face went totally red. I just started sweating immediately. My cousin [and I were] like,
  hold the door so it doesn't look so bad — like we're just saying a long goodbye — now help me put
  the door back on."* And the sequel: on a first road trip from **San Jose to Las Vegas** it broke down
  in the desert, and his father and older brother had to run across the highway to hitchhike to a gas
  station. *"To this day my dad's like, I guess we're done with Ford. He bought a Honda. It's never
  broken."* ★★
- ★ **Impression management = branding at a personal level.** Credits **Dr. Christine Lucer** (spelling
  uncertain) of "the Monera Institute/project" for the term. His own examples: dressing for a date,
  dressing for a job interview — *"dress for the job you want, not the one that you have… if they're
  very suit-and-tie three-piece suit, don't come in in shorts and flip-flops; conversely, if you work
  in the creative industry, do not show up in a suit, they'll make fun of you."* A family data point:
  *"my younger brother, when he first arrived in LA, showed up for a job interview and he wore an
  ill-fitted suit, and the guy said **never come back again like that — but we'll hire you.**"* The
  conclusion: *"if we are managing impressions, that is branding. We're trying to shape and influence
  it — **we can't control people, we can't control their feelings, but we can definitely influence
  it.**"* ★
- ★ **The logo is a container for meaning.** *"A logo is just a container for meaning. Everybody that's
  part of that company — from the marketing team down to customer service to the intern — **puts
  meaning in the container.** Then the container has to be beautiful, it has to be open, it has to be
  something that can withstand the rigours of different applications and time."* Attribution: he
  credits an interviewee whose name he cannot recall on air (`attribution: uncertain`). Paired with a
  self-aware admission: *"there are wonderful brands that I love that have a horrible logo that I just
  cannot buy, because **I'm a snob.** I shouldn't be caught up in that, but the aesthetics do matter to
  me."* ★
- **Why designers conflate logo with brand.** Nike has spent decades shaping impressions and *"we come
  to symbolise it in a mark"* — so designers conclude the mark carries the value. His test: Nike and
  Skechers both cover your feet and cushion them, *"but it's a totally different experience — the one
  that we tell ourselves."*
- **On HBR (a small, characteristic aside).** He hunted down a print copy at a Barnes & Noble in
  Glendale and reports: *"the article sucked. HBR is not all that it's cracked up to be… save your
  money."* But recommends the preceding article on measuring branding vs. marketing.
