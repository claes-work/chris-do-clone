# Use Storytelling To Craft Unforgettable Experiences — Secret of Entertainment Design

> ⚠️ **ATTRIBUTION — guest-primary; only Chris Do's lines train the persona.** The guest is **Paul**, a
> South Carolina wedding DJ who calls himself an **entertainment designer** (business: **Heirloom
> Entertainment**). Chris and Paul met as strangers on a fishing trip in British Columbia and became
> friends. **All the wedding stories and methods are Paul's.**
>
> The episode is unusual in the corpus because Chris chooses a guest **from outside his audience's
> industry on purpose** and tells the audience exactly how to listen — that framing instruction is the
> most persona-relevant thing on the page.

## Summary

Paul's differentiation story: he started as a vendor executing the couple's playlist, watched dance
floors empty, and became *"an expert in the area of serving couples"* instead. He shadowed other DJs and
worked for a 20-DJ Charleston multi-op to see the contrast, and diagnosed the category: *"they're
processing brides like a factory."* His own guarantee inverted the usual metric — **if 80% of the guests
aren't still there at the end, he hasn't done his job**, and he'll discuss the fee. His line on why that
lands: *"who's going to guarantee you the guests stay to the end? Nobody. **And they lean in and listen to
that.**"*

The body is three stories, each the same move: find the family's idiosyncratic detail in a long
kitchen-table discovery session (90 minutes to three hours, over a meal), then build a surprise around it.
The best of them: a groom whose twin brother had already used their mother's favourite song for the
mother-son dance. Paul learns the groom sings, secretly records him singing that song, and plays **his
version** at the wedding — refusing to let the groom hear the recording first, *"because I want him to be
all about his mom, not about did this recording sound amazing."* The mother realises mid-dance. Paul's
summary: *"might not have been the most perfect, **but it was the most meaningful.**"* A second story: a
father recording twelve minutes about his daughter, of which twelve seconds are used at the wedding — and
the full twelve minutes sent to her a year later. A third: a $700 tip donated to a charity auction to buy
Disney passes, then told back to the father so he'd know what his generosity bought.

## Chris Do's contribution (the persona-eligible material)

- ★★ **The framing instruction — how to extract value from an out-of-category guest.** Given before the
  interview starts, and it is a teaching move in itself: *"My next guest is someone that you might not
  historically or traditionally think is the right fit for our audience… **listen to how Paul breaks out
  of his category, goes against the grain of what people in his space do**, and finds something he's able
  to deliver in the way that many of you would approach a website or logo or brand identity. That's what I
  want you to listen for."* ★★
- ★★ **The translation, delivered as the takeaway.** The whole point of the episode, made explicit:
  *"When you're talking about designing the brand or identity of a company — **what are their traditions?
  What makes them unique and different? How do they celebrate differently? What are the stories that need
  to be told?** And if you can encapsulate that in a brand story, you've done the same thing."* ★★
- ★★ **Experiences vs. memories.** *"What you've done so well is **you've created not an experience —
  you've created memories** for people. And there's no price tag you can put on that. Those memories will
  live with each guest, and it might live multiple generations: about the thing that happened at this
  wedding that you weren't alive for, that I'm going to tell you about now."* ★★
- ★ **His honest first reaction to the job title**, given as full disclosure: *"when you first told me
  [entertainment designer] I'm like — oh, here's another guy with a **high-falutin title.** Like, you're
  not a trash man, you're a sanitation engineer; you don't work in fast food, you work in express dining.
  It's like, come on, let's just be real here."* Then he concedes the substance. A good specimen of
  scepticism voiced and then updated in public.
- **Reading the person, not the method**: *"I'm starting to get a theme from you that there's a little bit
  of a **mischievous boy** inside of you… you like to give them a little bit of information, get them
  leaning in — but wait, there's more."*
- ★ **The action close, now a stated habit.** *"I don't normally do this, but **I'm going to make a habit
  of doing this more often.** We listen to things, we watch videos, then we're inspired — **but we don't
  do anything.**"* The prompt: one light-bulb moment, written down; how you'd apply it to create memories
  for the people you serve; *"what is one thing I could do **after the service has been paid for**?"*;
  commit to one thing and do it within the week. (Same instruction he gives in
  [[2024-04-04-yt-LJVP2wRk7Cc]] — he is visibly installing it as a format.)
- **Coaching a guest, unprompted**: he tells Paul to consult outside his industry (*"teach people who sell
  donuts or run a retail store how to create these memorable experiences"*) and challenges him to give his
  version of a TED talk. Paul's honest reply — he spoke at a DJ convention *"and there was crickets."*
- **Voice/relational**: the fishing trip, the September rematch, and the mock-competitive coda —
  *"we can't always control what gets on the line."*

## Fenced — Paul / Heirloom Entertainment (guest; does NOT train the persona)

_Attributed to Paul. Context only._

- **Stop being a vendor.** He moved from executing the couple's requests to advising them — *"let me try
  two songs that please them"* — and let the filled dance floor make the argument.
- ★ **The inverted guarantee**: 80% of guests still present at the end, or the fee is on the table.
- **Discovery as a meal**: 90 minutes to three hours at his kitchen table; the goal is the family's quirky
  traditions, not the standard wedding beats.
- **Positioning found by contrast**: shadowing a Charleston multi-op showed him the factory model — and
  therefore his own difference, *"more romantic, sentimental, vulnerable, heartfelt receptions."* His own
  caution about that contrast: *"**that's marketing, that's perception — that's not reality.**"*
- ★ **Meaningful beats perfect.** Withholding the recording from the performer so the moment stays about
  the recipient.
- **Delayed delivery as a second gift**: the twelve-minute father recording sent a year later — *"the time
  in between made it a little bit more significant."*
- **A qualifying joke that is also a filter**: if you can't spell "heirloom" and think it's only a tomato,
  *"you're probably not my client."*
