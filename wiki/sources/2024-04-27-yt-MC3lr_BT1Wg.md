# How to Counter Low-ball Offers (Sales Roleplay)

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do primary.** A live Pro Group roleplay: Chris plays the vendor, a member (**Mo**)
> plays a shop owner with a $1,000 video budget. Other members (**Alec**, **Z**, and one unnamed) add
> commentary between rounds — their observations are context, not persona data. **Everything Chris says
> in and around the roleplay is persona-eligible**, including his in-character lines.
>
> Two complete negotiations are performed back to back, and the second exists **because a member imposed a
> constraint on him** — *"if you had set the parameters as: Chris, you can't say no, you can't refer them
> to Fiverr, you must book the job."* That framing is what makes this the most useful sales artifact in
> the recent corpus.

## Summary

**Round 1 — the walk-away.** The buyer opens at $1,000. Chris never counters on price. He asks what
problem it solves (awareness; nobody's coming into the store), then makes the buyer do the arithmetic:
2–3 extra customers a day × ~$100 average order = ~$250/day × 30 = **$7,500/month of new revenue** — for
$1,000. His question is not "is that too little for me" but *"**spending $1,000 seems appropriate for
$7,500 of revenue per month?**"* The buyer flinches into the standard defence (*"are you going to
guarantee me $7,500 a month?"*) and Chris declines to over-claim: *"I'm not saying that at all. I'm just
trying to measure effort versus result."*

Then the move that defines the round: he treats the small budget as **evidence the problem isn't real**.
*"When somebody comes to me and they say I don't have a lot of money to do something, **my first instinct
is to say let's not do it — it's not important. Wouldn't that be your instinct too?**"* When the buyer
says it's important but *"not $1,000 important,"* Chris audits their actual spending — the refrigeration,
the AC, the polished floors, **the M5 parked outside** — to establish the principle: *"**people tend to
spend money on what they value.**"* He also names the failure mode by name: **video envy** — *"businesses
like yours see other businesses produce video and then we create it just to say we're keeping up with the
Joneses."*

He closes by disqualifying **the engagement, not the client** — and does it generously: *"I'd encourage
you **not to spend that money with anybody**, not just me."* Then the Fiverr referral, delivered dryly.

**Round 2 — the risk-share.** Boxed in (must close, can't refer out), he inverts the deal: **do the video
free in exchange for a share of the measured result** — $50 per new customer through the door on a $100
average order. *"No risk to you at all. **I take all the risk and I rely on your honesty.**"* The buyer's
objection is the tell — *"I'm concerned that you're going to make a banger video"* — and Chris uses it:
*"**let me ask you something: if I can bring in a long line of clients for you, you're saying please don't
do that?**"* When the buyer asks for a cap, Chris refuses to blend the two structures and re-states them
cleanly: either **$4,000 flat and you keep all the upside**, or **free and I take 50% of new net revenue,
uncapped** (he offers a one-year limit purely as comfort). Peter Drucker supplies the principle: **all
profit comes from risk**. The buyer, watching the maths, takes the $4,000 — which is the point of the
exercise: the flat fee becomes the *safe* option only once the alternative is genuinely expensive.

Deal closed at **$4,000 with $2,000 up front**.

## Key claims (2024-04-27)

- ★★ **Don't negotiate price — establish proportion.** Convert the deliverable into the buyer's own
  revenue arithmetic and let them evaluate the ratio.
- ★★ **A small budget is data about importance, not about the buyer's wallet.** The correct first
  response is *"let's not do it, then."*
- ★★ **People spend money on what they value** — auditable against their fridge, their floors and their
  car.
- ★ **Disqualify the project, not the person**, and mean it: advise them not to spend the money with
  *anyone.*
- ★ **Video envy / keeping up with the Joneses** — named as a bad reason to buy, by the person being paid
  to sell.
- ★ **Effort must be proportionate too**: *"in order for me to do this properly I'd have to spend quite a
  bit of time… **it would seem disproportionate, the length of that conversation, to talk about a $1,000
  problem.**"*
- ★★ **The risk-share as the alternative to discounting.** Free work against a share of measured results;
  the vendor carries all the risk, so the vendor takes the upside. **Peter Drucker: all profit comes from
  risk.**
- ★★ **Refuse the hybrid.** A capped percentage on a free engagement is the buyer taking the upside *and*
  the safety; keep the two structures separate and let them choose.
- ★ **Surface the contradiction in the objection**: wanting a cap means hoping the work *doesn't* work too
  well.
- **Member additions, worth recording as context** (not Chris): *"everything is a negotiation — **if
  they're not meeting you at your price, you don't have to meet them at their deliverable**"* (change the
  scope, e.g. a 30-second video for $1,000); and Mo's own practice of quoting a **$1,000 minimum per
  video** and cheerfully pointing people to the $80 alternative — *"if that video is good, let me know,
  'cause then I'll hire him too."*
- **Chris's answer to "but $1,000 is a lot to turn down"**: ★ *"**You didn't have that $1,000 before they
  called you. You were going to make life work regardless.** Don't fall into that mindset — like, now just
  because they called, I have to close the job. That puts you in a horrible, disadvantageous position."*

## Verbatim quotes

- ★★ *"When somebody comes to me and they say I don't have a lot of money to do something, **my first
  instinct is to say: let's not do it. It's not important.** Wouldn't that be your instinct too?"*
- ★★ *"I find in my life and my experience that **people tend to spend money on what they value.**"*
- ★★ *"I don't want to take your money to do something that's not important. **Is it important, or isn't
  it?**"*
- ★ *"Then you shouldn't. **But I'm probably not the person to do that for you then — and I would
  encourage you not to spend that money with anybody. Not just me.**"*
- ★★ *"I'll do the video for you for free — **if we get the results that you want, I'd like a percentage
  of the results.** … No risk to you at all. **I take all the risk, and I rely on your honesty.**"*
- ★★ *"You know who Peter Drucker is? **All profit comes from risk.** I'm taking all the risk — **or you
  can just pay me what I'm worth and you keep all the profit.**"*
- ★ *"Let me ask you something: **if I can bring in a long line of clients for you, you're saying please
  don't do that?**"*
- ★ *"**You didn't have that $1,000 before they called you.** You were going to make life work regardless."*
- ★ *"I know what happens a lot of times: businesses like yours see other businesses produce video, and
  then we have **video envy**, and then we create it just to create it — to say, yeah, we're keeping up
  with the Joneses."*
- On being interrupted mid-quote: *"So it feels a little disproportionate, doesn't it?"* — the buyer cuts
  off his Jim Rohn quote and he simply absorbs it and continues. A good specimen of composure under
  interruption.
- Closing the round: *"You're a wise businessman. I can see you're doing the calculations in your eyes
  right now… **so you're probably thinking it's a better and more prudent decision just to pay me the
  4K.**"*
- The dry referral: *"Can you recommend anybody? — Yeah, I hear there's a site called **F-I-V-E-R-R**,
  could do it for five bucks."*
