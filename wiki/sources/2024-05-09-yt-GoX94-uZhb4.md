# How to Negotiate When Clients Can't Afford Your Prices (Whiteboard)

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do primary.** A whiteboard teach-back with **Diego ("dgo"/"D-go")**, a Futur team
> member whose own business is used as the live case study. Diego's numbers are his; the teaching,
> frameworks and arithmetic are Chris's and are persona-eligible.
>
> ★★ **This is the direct companion to [[2024-04-27-yt-MC3lr_BT1Wg]]** — the same low-ball roleplay, now
> unpacked economically and defended against the comment section. It is where the deal structure gets its
> justification. **The two must be promoted together.**

## Summary

Three movements: dispel the "don't pay until it works" objection; teach the arithmetic that makes a
risk-share rational; then use the arithmetic on Diego's actual business and prescribe a new floor.

**1. The framing correction, and an unusually blunt disclaimer.** He opens by conceding what the
roleplay was: *"granted, **this was the kind of client I don't want to work with**… very foolish with how
they spend their money and their time, they're not risk takers, they're risk averse, and they're actually
not really good business people."* The constraint was artificial — *"context: you can't say no, you must
accept a deal."* Then he disposes of the loudest comment (*"you shouldn't be paid anything until it
works"*) by asking Diego to name a business where you consume first and decide whether to pay later. The
restaurant doesn't count — *"the social contract is such that by the time you're done eating you will pay,
even if you don't like the meal."* Only donation-based models qualify. *"That doesn't fit the logic test.
We have special rules that don't seem to apply to any other use case."*

**2. The arithmetic.** The chain he builds on the board:

- **The two B's — baseline and benchmark** (his own coinage). *"We have to know where you are and where
  you'd like to be."* Current state → desired future state.
- **Lifetime customer value** = gross revenue ÷ number of customers. With a refinement he calls **Olympic
  scoring** — drop the top two and bottom two clients, because outliers skew it.
- **Gross vs. net**, and why net is the number that matters: after vendors, **after paying yourself a
  fair salary for someone doing your work** (*"if you had to replace yourself, what would you have to pay
  them?"*), after overhead, subscriptions, licences, insurance. His own past error, admitted: *"I thought
  this at one point too — 250,000 is how much I profit. **It's not.** Even if you're a solo operation."*
- **Cost per acquisition.** The governing rule: ★★ *"as long as you can **spend less to acquire a new
  customer than what your net margins are**, you should be happy to do that deal all day long."* Taught
  via a headphone example where the margin math leaves only $5 — and the point is that **$5 scales**:
  *"you have basically an ATM machine that just spits out $5, and all you have to do is spend more money
  in advertising."* Reinforced with Wall Street microtransactions (buy at 99¢, sell at $1, at volume).
  *"We have to start thinking like real business people. **We don't have to get emotional about how much
  effort it is.**"*

**3. Who is actually taking the risk** — the heart of the video, and his answer to the comment section.
He draws "Joe the sales rep" and shows that **Joe doesn't eat until you eat**, and eats the smallest slice
of the pie. The rep makes the calls, does the meetings, wines and dines, presents repeatedly, and can be
burned twice — once by wasted opportunities, once by reputational damage if the vendor delivers badly:
*"that client comes back and says *your people are not good* — **what does that do to my reputation?**"*
Hence: *"to work for free in hopes that you will get a new client, **that is risk.**"* With Drucker as the
principle again: **all profit comes from risk**, so whoever carries it earns more. Illustrated with
crypto, mutual funds and index funds.

**4. The Diego diagnosis** — the practical payoff (below).

## Key claims (2024-05-09)

- ★★ **The two B's — baseline and benchmark.** Establish the client's current number before any
  performance deal, because otherwise the deal is unmeasurable. He works the failure modes honestly: if
  the baseline is 30 and a bad month brings 27, the video's three new customers earn the vendor **nothing**;
  if 33 of the client's *existing* customers show up and the video sends none, **the vendor gets paid
  anyway.** *"You see how complicated this is."*
- ★★ **Lifetime customer value, net margin, and CPA** as the three numbers that turn a "can't afford it"
  into a structurable deal.
- ★ **Olympic scoring** — drop the top and bottom two clients before averaging.
- ★★ **Sales-rep economics, from his own company.** *"When we were working with sales reps I think we were
  doing anywhere between **7 to 12% of gross revenue** to pay them, depending on who they were, how hot
  they were, and how desperate we were to get them. **We were happy to pay this, and we built a
  multi-million dollar business using this exact model.**"* Blind ran **five reps** — West Coast, Midwest,
  East Coast, Europe, Asia. Comparison: **Amazon pays affiliates ~4%**, rising with volume.
- ★★ **Blind's revenue, self-reported**: *"on an average year my client-service company called Blind would
  do about **$5 million in revenue a year**"* — with 7–12% of that going off the top to reps.
- **The negotiation was an opening salvo, and he says so.** 50% in perpetuity was *"a negotiation point"*
  — *"I'm not going to negotiate myself to 10% right away, then he'll say 3%, and this is where we're
  going to get stuck."* The realistic landing zone he offers: a **collapsing tier** — 50% on the first
  $5K, 40% on the next, stepping down until he's whole.
- ★★ **The whole structure is an argument for just paying the fee.** *"The whole point of setting up this
  deal was to let them know that **$1,000 is really cheap. Just pay the $1,000 already**… you don't have
  to deal with any of this stuff."* And: *"**if you don't pay upfront, you'll pay some way.**"*
- **What the vendor actually signs up for** beyond the video: channel selection, SEO-friendly titling,
  thumbnail, descriptions, a campaign to drive views, supporting social assets, and ongoing analytics
  optimisation. *"It could take months of work."*
- **Protection, if you do the deal**: an attorney-drafted **contract**, a right to **audit the books**,
  and an agreed **dispute path** (arbitration or small claims). With the caveat that matters more:
  *"**if we have a funny feeling, I don't care how good the contract is — it's not going to save you.**"*
  (He also plugs The Futur's legal kit, and repeats his standing disclaimer: *"I'm **not** an attorney,
  consult a legal professional."* Note the caption renders this as *"because I'm an attorney"* — a
  transcription error; the surrounding sentence makes the intent unambiguous.)
- ★ **Creative freedom follows risk.** If the vendor carries the outcome, *"short of them doing something
  misogynistic, homophobic, transphobic, racist [or] sexist, **they should be allowed to do whatever they
  need to do to make the video, because their butt is on the line.**"*
- ★★ **Guarantees are priced, not given.** The car analogy: a 5-year warranty covers **manufacturer
  defects only**; a pothole that destroys a rim ($5,000, 24 hours after buying the car) is yours. That is
  why insurance and extended warranties exist — *"you're buying **peace of mind.**"* So: *"if you want a
  guarantee, what should we charge? **A lot more money** — and it can go up exponentially."*
- **Why 50% is defensible in some categories**: insurance, real estate, telecom and credit-card companies
  have high margins and low hard costs, so they spend enormously on acquisition. *"You'd be shocked at how
  much they're willing to spend."*
- ★★ **The Diego prescription — minimum level of engagement.** Diego's numbers (self-reported): **$250K
  gross, 36 clients, ~$50K net = 20% margin**, so **~$7,000 LTV** and **~$1,400 net per customer**, with
  **$0 spent on acquisition**. His channels: SEO (~25 clients at ~$5K, mostly one-and-done), social media
  (5 clients, ~$3K, more recurring, ~$12K LTV), word of mouth (~$7K LTV). Chris's diagnosis: the SEO
  clients are **below his own average**, which drags everything down. The prescription: **$7K becomes the
  floor, then push the minimum level of engagement to $15K** — *"and you need to be clear about that, and
  you need to be positioned in the market to attract these kinds of customers."* The arithmetic: 15K × 36
  = **$540K in 2024**, then **$1M in 2025**. *"We take small steps toward our goal."*
- **A recurring-revenue aside, credited**: *"this is why **Ronald J. Baker** talks about getting
  subscription models as the ultimate way we should all price professional creative services — because you
  don't have to worry about acquiring new customers. All you have to worry about is **giving and creating
  value.**"*
