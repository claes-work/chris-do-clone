# How To Respond To The 3 Most Common Client Objections in Sales

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do primary**, lecturing to a student audience (*"when you walk out the door and
> you have your first client"*). Persona-eligible. **Blair Enns** is credited by name for
> *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* and **retreat-and-follow**; Cialdini's **consistency principle** is
> referenced without naming him.

## Summary

He calls it **"the art of the argument"** — *"because that's how it sounds like in our head."* The
question behind it: *"how do you convince the client to go in a particular creative direction?"* And his
first move is to reject the verb: *"the word **convince** implies that you're moving them from their point
to your point, and **it's a lot of friction.**"*

**The three objections**, which he says he has heard *"many, many times"*: **you're too expensive**, **you
don't have enough experience**, **you don't know enough about [X]**.

★★ **Why you cannot win them head-on — the rock-paper-scissors trap.** You are the medium-sized player.
The big firm wins *"we're worth it"* (better known, more established, 500 people, researchers, MBAs — and
**less risky**); the one-person shop wins *"we're cheaper."* *"So we're in the middle, and **the middle is
a hard place to win.**"* Worse, *"anytime you reduce scope **you actually give in to the price** — and you
cannot go as low as a low-cost bidder. You just can't."* His image for the position: *"balancing on a
chair on top of a table on top of another table on top of a ledge, where **one wrong move, the wind
changes, you die.**"*

★★ **Why charging in fails psychologically.** *"When clients raise an objection **it's like red meat to a
dog** — the problem is inside your head. What they're doing is trying to invalidate you, and **you feel
invalidated, so it's a stab at your own ego. We're eager to prove them wrong.**"* And then the
**consistency principle**: people dig into positions they know are wrong rather than concede — *"if you
have a girlfriend or boyfriend you do this all the time; even when you're wrong you're like *no, that's
the way the world works*, and **you find some cockamamie reason to explain it.**"* So pushing hardens them.

★★ **The alternative: judo, not karate.** *"Karate is a hard martial art — it's about power, breaking
boards, bricks, heads. **Judo is leverage. You use the other person's momentum against them.** They charge
in, and what do we do? **We embrace them, and you pivot, and you throw them to the side.** You exert very
little power, and it can be done by anybody."*

Operationally: **own the objection, then pivot.** *"The thing that I found to work best for me is that
**I just own it.** You're right — we are too expensive, we don't have enough experience. **The trick is
how do you pivot out of that.**"* With the sequencing warning: *"I need to change the conversation, but
**without acknowledging what the client said first, you're just changing the subject — and you're
slippery, you're distrustful.** You have to acknowledge it first, because they're smart people. **The
reason they hire you is because they're smarter than you in business, and you're not going to outsmart
them.**"*

★★ **Then Blair Enns's retreat-and-follow**, and the specific line that inverts the power dynamic.

## Key claims (dated to the talk, ~2015–2017; published 2024-05-28)

- **Don't "convince."** The word encodes friction — moving them from their position to yours.
- ★★ **Rock-paper-scissors**: you can beat one competitor type but not all; the middle is structurally the
  worst place to argue from.
- ★ **Reducing scope is conceding on price.** And you can never out-cheap the cheapest bidder.
- ★★ **Objections feel like invalidation, which is why the instinct is wrong.** Go against it.
- **The consistency principle** makes pushing counterproductive — people defend positions to stay
  consistent, not because they're right.
- ★★ **Judo over karate**: acknowledge → validate → let them commit → pivot.
- ★★ **Raise the objection first** (Blair Enns): *"as artists and creative types our tendency is to avoid
  areas of potential objection. **What you want to do is the opposite — raise the objections first and lay
  them on the table for the client to address.**"* Because if the client says *"you're too expensive"*
  first, *"you're going to have to explain yourself, you're going to have to be defensive. **It's not a
  power position.**"*
- ★★ **The power line.** To a million-dollar company while charging $80/hour: *"**I'm not sure a company of
  your size can afford us.**"* Why it works: *"they're already thinking that anyway. **So now they have to
  tell you why they think they can afford you — they're proving themselves to you. It changes the entire
  client dynamic.**"*
- ★ **Whisper, don't shout.** *"**If I whisper, they lean in to hear me. If you shout, you push people
  away.** Selling, convincing, all that kind of stuff — that's shouting."*
- ★ **The buyer's real risk is personal.** *"Somebody who hires you — they're usually not the owner. **They
  make the wrong decision, what happens to them? They get fired.** That's the reality of it. … Somebody is
  going to have to find a new job. **I know you don't think about that, because you're not there yet.**"*
  This is why the big firm's "less risky" argument wins so often.
- **The differentiation line he ends on**, delivered as his own script: *"I help businesses grow and be
  more innovative by helping them understand their customers… **so if you don't need help in this way, save
  your money, hire the other guys. We'll just let you know we'll be here in six months when you need
  help.** I know it's cocky. I'm confident."*

- ★ *"**You don't need to prove you're right if you can just find the hole in the other person's logic.**"*

  and you have your first client, you're 20 years smarter than you would have been.**"*
