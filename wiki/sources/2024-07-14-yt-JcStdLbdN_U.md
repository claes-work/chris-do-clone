# How To Handle Client Feedback As A Creative

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do primary**, teaching to a room (with a Zoom audience of Pro Group members).
> Persona-eligible.

## Summary

The question he opens on is the real one: *"**how do we know if work is good or bad?** Do we have an
internal compass that guides us to be able to interpret feedback as **just one point of data** — or do we
just throw ourselves at the whim of the opinions of other people?"*

★★ **The Blind-era story, and the move he made.** His executive producer came to him after losing a
pitch on a big account: *"Chris, **I'm not sure the team is giving it their best effort.**"* Chris reads
the motive generously and precisely — *"he's saying this because he thinks it will trigger a response from
me and **get me involved in the project**"* — and explains why he wouldn't: *"I have very capable creative
directors **who I've trained myself, who I brought up the ranks** — and to pull me in **would undermine
what they're trying to do and would be a blow to her self-confidence and self-esteem.**"* He also states
the stakes fairly: *"this is not just any other person off the internet. This is **a client who's got the
keys to whether you're going to proceed with a six-figure job — $200,000, $400,000.**"*

★★ **What he did instead — the accountability flip.** *"You see all the work before we submit it, right?
… **Do you have an opinion of the work? Do you know if it's good or bad before you submit it?**"* — the
producer says yes — *"okay, this is great. So here's what I want you to do: the next time we go after a
new piece of business, **make sure you have ample time to check in the work… and you tell me if the work
is good or not before you show the client.**"* And Chris names the trap he has just set: *"**he knew then
that I kind of boxed him into a corner**, because now he would have to take **responsibility for
determining, prior to someone else telling him, that the work is good or not.**"* Both directions now
carry accountability — if they lose, he had a view; if they win, he owned it too. *"**And we did bring in
that project.**"*

**The generalisation, which is the point of the video**: *"many of us **do not have an internal compass or
north star** telling us that the work is good — so we're constantly seeking the opinions of other people,
and therefore we **put our emotional well-being, our happiness, our joy and our sadness in the hands of
strangers**, or people who don't actually really care about us at all."*

Then a clean teach on **intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation**, using his own son and a messy room as the
worked example (allowance vs. no games vs. *"my tone, my mood, my temper will change for the worse"*), and
— importantly — refusing the purist position: *"sometimes it can actually be motivated by both, and
**that's totally okay too.**"* His own example: pride in the work *and* the new business that pays
salaries.

## Key claims (2024-07-14)

- ★★ **Feedback is one data point, not a verdict** — but only if you have an internal standard to weigh it
  against. *"**The feedback that you get can wreck you if you have no internal sense of what is good and
  what is bad.**"*
- ★★ **The accountability flip**: when someone brings you a judgement about quality, ask whether they had
  a view *before* the outcome — and make that their job going forward. It converts second-guessing into
  ownership.
- ★★ **Refusing to be pulled in is a leadership decision, not aloofness.** Stepping in over trained
  creative directors would undermine them; he names the cost to their confidence explicitly.
- **Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation**, taught with the in/ex root and a domestic example — and with the
  both-at-once case allowed rather than moralised.
- ★ **The consequence of outsourcing the standard**: your emotional state ends up in the hands of *"people
  who don't actually really care about us at all."*
