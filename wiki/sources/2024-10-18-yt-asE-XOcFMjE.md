## Summary — what is Chris's

★★ **He submits his own product to the guest for critique, and the guest takes the shot.** Chris:
*"very selfish question here for me — **to get people to join the Futur Pro Group, which is $250 a month,
minimum three-month commitment.** We run events like this but we teach them something, but **our audience
is so broad that it'll get filled with people who are not qualified to be there**, so our conversion
percentage is not so good. **What do I got to do to tweak that?**"*

Priestley's answer is a direct hit on the price: *"**$250 a month is expensive to the wrong person and too
cheap for the right person** — and that's something that kind of concerns me. Realistically for the right
person **5 to 10 grand actually signals that there's value**… so potentially this should be starting at
**$500 or $1,000 a month** — and it's a lot less people, but they're **the right people**… and for anyone
who doesn't want to spend a grand a month, **go look at the channel.**"* Chris's response is one line:
★★ *"**I can hear that.**"*

★★ **And the deeper diagnosis, which Chris applies to himself out loud.** Priestley's frame is that an
introduction event must **create hunger, not satisfy it** — *"if you sat down at a restaurant and they
brought out ten samples of all the meals… **you'd say: well, I'm full now.**"* Chris's answer is the most
useful self-criticism in the episode: ★★ *"that's something new for me, because **I really think of myself
as an educator. I just want to teach**, and so I'm writing new webinars all the time — I'm like: let me
teach you this thing. **I'm definitely feeding them. I'm not creating hunger**, because the conclusion is
*let me go work on this before I join*. So we're not getting the conversion we want. **So I'm messing up
here.**"*

Priestley's paired data point lands the same way: an AI analysis of his team's last 30 intro presentations
found the **only strong correlation with conversion was the number of calls to action** — lowest converting
had **three**, mid **five**, highest **seven** (in 90 minutes). *"Who was the most guilty of the three
calls to action? **Me** … because **I love fancying myself as an educator** as opposed to doing the calls
to action — *oh, they'll come through when they're ready.* **No, they don't.**"* His fix is a drawing of
**seven circles** ticked off live, and *"my conversions are nearly double."* When he tells Chris *"you did
it twice"*, Chris agrees: *"**yeah, barely.**"*

> ★★ **This is a rare thing in the corpus: Chris's teacher-identity named as a commercial liability, by
> him.** Elsewhere the educator instinct is presented as the whole point. Both are true and both are dated
> — **hold them together, don't resolve one into the other.** See also his own give-it-away doctrine.

★★ **His own coinage, offered mid-episode: the three A's.** *"There's a need for us to **reflect on the
things that we did** — not just always looking forward… and we'll call this the **tri-A**: because when you
look back at your history you can gain an **awareness** that you didn't have before… and then you start to
**align** the things that excite you, that fill you with passion and joy, **so that you can be alive.**"*
(**Awareness → Alignment → Alive.** *"You got a AAA battery — you plugged in."* Priestley: *"then you had to
one-up it there."*) ⚠️ Caption renders the coinage as *"the triaa"*; the three A-words themselves are clear.

★★ **The direct address to the audience, delivered as an interruption of his own interview** — the clearest
statement in the corpus of what he thinks the failure mode of consuming his content is: *"a lot of times
you're going to listen to guests that we bring on, you get really excited about something, and then the
next new exciting thing comes along, and **a month, two months, six months later you're going to totally
forget about this thing until you hear it from another person again.** … **I don't think you need more
information. You need more action.** You have to go and apply this stuff — **otherwise it's not magic, it's
not going to work by itself.** … **You don't even need to listen to the rest of this** — make this
commitment today, because **the next time you present yourself could be the time that everything
changes.**"*

★★ **He names the gap between his own biography and his audience's, unprompted.** *"I've had the good
fortune, the foresight, to get into what I got into and **win awards and do work at the very highest levels
in a highly competitive market** — and then I realised **there are not that many people that fit those
descriptions.** There are more people out there who **didn't find their path**, didn't go to school for it,
kind of figured it out on their own, so **they have massive amounts of impostor syndrome**. They weren't
raised by entrepreneurial or forward-minded parents — in fact **they were raised by parents who kept
telling them: you can't do this, who do you think you are, you're nothing special.** So they're working
against a lot of limiting beliefs. **I've come to the conclusion that sometimes the things I say are really
hard for people to process.**"* He even voices the objection in their words: *"you went to a great design
school, you graduated top of your class, then opportunities just dropped in your lap — **so you didn't have
to grind it the way we did.**"*

★★ **The one place he pushes back on the guest.** Priestley: *"it really doesn't matter what you believe
about yourself, **it matters more what your clients believe about you**… we don't need self-belief, we need
others to believe in us. We get self-belief after the fact."* Chris agrees in part and then dissents:
*"I also find that **if you don't believe, how can they believe?** Because — I have this idea that **your
mouth and the words that you use, they betray you, because they reveal the real view.** So when someone
comes up and gives you their first pitch, **it's revealing their own self-belief** until you help them
reframe it."*

★ **The safer version of the guest's hard tactic — his own contribution to it.** Priestley's instruction is
to declare yourself closed. Chris makes it survivable: *"at the end of the year **we don't get a lot of work
done anyways**… you can wait until late October and then announce you're not going to work until January —
**and you're risking nothing, because you weren't planning to work anyway.** … Or if you're planning a
vacation or a sabbatical, **what a great true and honest way to say I'm not available.**"* With the joke
that carries the argument: *"a lot of us are very hopeful that **December 25th some new client's going to
come in the door and say: we need you now, you're the only person who can do this — save me, Obi-Wan.**
And that doesn't really happen."*

★★ **And the reframe that makes the whole tactic land for his audience specifically** — a permission
structure rather than a growth hack: *"**creative people have a hard time saying no.** But if you were to
put on the internet that **I'm not taking any new clients**, when someone who's a poor fit calls up
randomly… you can just say: you know what, **I'm actually not doing this**, I've communicated to the world
that I'm shutting down for four months. **That's a great way to filter out bad clients — and you don't
have to feel like a bad human being for doing that.**"*

★ **The dry-cleaner bit**, on why a logo can genuinely not matter: designers *"walk up to a local business
like — well, **your logo is not consistent and it doesn't follow the Fibonacci grid system** — and they're
like: **huh? I'm a dry cleaner.** … **You see this line of customers I've had for the last 40 years?
They've never once said the logo bothered them.**"*

★ **Location/biography**: recorded in **Santa Monica**, *"right above the Third Street Promenade"* — which
he calls ★ *"**my old stomping grounds.**"*

## ⚠️ FENCED — Priestley's frameworks, credited to him

- ★★ **The three buckets** people sort you into on hearing you speak: **newbie** (people offer you
  *experience*), **worker bee** (people ask your day rate), **key person of influence** (in demand, charges
  what they like). *"**If you position yourself as a newbie, you'll get treated like a newbie.**"* And on
  being offered exposure: *"**that's your fault** — the way you communicated led them to believe you needed
  the experience."*
- ★★ **The magic sentence**: *"**I did something special for a certain type of client and we got a
  remarkable result — let me tell you about it.**"* Applied year by year across a timeline to mine stories.
- ★★ **The functional layer vs. the vital layer** — the bread and butter everyone must do, versus *"the
  icing on the cake… **that changes the course of the future.**"* And the KPI self-definition:
  *"**they know their job in the world is bending reality.**"*
- ★★ **The financial-planner rewrite** (a 23-year generalist → *"I help rural families that own farms plan
  for the next two generations"*), which the room valued at *"$500 a day versus $5,000 a day"* — **his
  worked example**, ★★ *"and this was **not inventing stuff** — this is actually what she'd been doing for
  23 years. **She just wasn't talking about it.**"*
- ★★ **Name, same, fame, aim, game** — his 30-second pitch framework.
- ★★ **The waiting list**, and the reason given for it: *"**I protect my ability to do great work, which is
  why I slowly onboard clients.**"* The tactical version: declare yourself closed, target **40 names**
  before accepting anyone, then re-open. And ★★ *"**nothing kills neediness faster than having a list.**"*
- ★★ **The Cinderella principle**: throw the ball (a low-commitment event), then apply a **rigid criterion**
  — the glass slipper — to identify the one. *"It's a complete reversal of the typical way people go about
  winning clients."*
- ★★ **The self-assessment**, with the 1920s toothpaste "squeaky clean" campaign as the origin story, and
  the doctrine: *"**it's so much easier to sell someone on a self-assessment than to sell them on the
  product**… have an **ambivalent persona** until they've taken the assessment."*
- ★★ **Introduction events** — *"the person who runs a workshop is a key person of influence **almost by
  definition**"* — and the stacking of all three (event → waiting list → assessment).
- ★★ **Clients who throw money at problems, not time.** *"The businesses that really become profitable…
  solve a problem for a client who **likes to throw money at their problems, not time at their problems.**"*
  With the warning aimed at designers who prefer nonprofits and unfunded startups: *"**all they've got is
  time**… and then they're super micro."*
- ★★ **High-end clients are less demanding, not more** — *"if you could make Lewis Hamilton drive one second
  faster around a racetrack that's worth millions; **if you could teach me to drive one second faster around
  my neighbourhood, that's not worth anything.** Making good go to great is worth a fortune; **broken needs
  fixing is not necessarily worth a lot of money.**"*
- ★★ *"**We get what we pitch for. We're always pitching.**"* — used to dismantle the *"easy for you"*
  comment: *"**that's a pitch. You just pitched it.** … Come back to me when you want to change that
  pitch."* (Chris calls it *"a great sentence."*)
- ★ **Borrow confidence before you have it** — the motorcycle analogy: *"**park to the side the fact that
  you don't feel confident. Borrow the confidence of someone who's already ahead of you**… we're not going
  to feel like a key person of influence the first time we pitch ourselves a certain way. **If we're
  waiting for that feeling, it's never going to start.**"*
- ★ **His own background as the counter-credential**: *"you and I are complete opposites… **I went to a
  terrible school, I went a few months of university and dropped out, I got no qualifications other than a
  driving licence**… I built a **$10 million business** without winning any awards, without a single piece
  of work for a household-name company."* Plus employee number three at a startup that went **3 → 60
  people**, McDonald's at 15 (*"**at the time I couldn't clean my room, but I could run a multi-million
  dollar restaurant**"*), and Pizza Hut delivery. The **Stone Soup** story as the entrepreneur's method.
- ★ **His business numbers, dated August 2024**: **ScoreApp** at **$29 / $59 / $99 per month** plus roughly
  **$1,000/month** enterprise, **6,500 customers** across **150 countries**; **Dent's KPI accelerator**
  capped at **~550 people/year** from *"5,000 to 7,000 who engage meaningfully"*, at about **$5,000/year**
  (startup) or **$10,000/year** (established), 15 years running, fortnightly 3-hour live sessions, 12-month
  minimum. Intro-event funnel: ~**240 register → ~110 attend → 25–30 interview**.
- ★ **Arithmetic for the episode's original question** (*how do I get 10 high-end clients?*): *"the top 5 to
  10% of people who are already filtered — **so if you want 10 clients you're going to need 100 to
  200**"*, run quarterly.
