## Summary

The frame is Peter Drucker's, stated twice — once cold-open, once as the payoff: ★★ *"your primary job is
to **consolidate the largest amount of discretionary time** to do and think of the only things that you can
do and no one else can"* (from ***The Effective Executive***), because ★★ *"**time as it turns out is the
most valuable non-renewable perishable resource.** You can make more money but you can't make more time."*
Everything in myths 1–3 is downstream of that.

**Myth 1 — "you have to do it all yourself."** The correction arrives as a client's mild irritation. Running
the agency, he felt *compelled* to tell a fast-growing tech client he could do all the work personally:
★★ *"he smiled and said — **Chris, I don't need to know how you do the work. I expect you to hire people to
do it.**"* — *"**he was almost bothered that I was telling him this.** After all, he was an entrepreneur
himself, and he didn't do all the work."*

★ **And the correction to the fear underneath it** (that the client will cut you out and go straight to your
team): *"it's **not** because they like their work better, or because it's cheaper to work with them
directly. **It's usually because you failed in keeping the relationship** — they feel neglected. So if
you're not doing the work **and** you're not maintaining the relationship, then yeah, they might go
elsewhere."*

★★ **The evidence he offers is a story about being on the receiving end of it.** The real-estate broker who
sold him his Pacific Palisades house left a voicemail at his office during the pandemic — *"hey Chris, it's
Robert, just checking in. **Fun fact: it's been 16 years on this very day that we helped you to buy your
house.**"* Two months later he and his wife decided to move to Pasadena: *"who else were we going to call?
**It didn't even occur to us to call anybody else.**"* His four rules follow from it — don't take the
relationship for granted, check in periodically, celebrate milestones and anniversaries, and be personal
(keep the details).

**Myth 2 — "you can't afford to hire, just work harder."** Named as a thinking error: *"this is what I refer
to as **worst-case-scenario thinking**. It's like Doomsday — instead of imagining all the positive outcomes
you obsess over how everything can go wrong."* Against it, Drucker again: *"**in business all profit comes
from risk.**"* The employee/owner contrast is unusually plain: an employee *"goes home each night relatively
confident that the next day will be similar… **the same paycheck every two weeks, regardless if they hit
home runs or strike out**"*, where entrepreneurship *"is inherently risky — you take on projects with no
certainty of outcomes, expenses, or even profit margins. **Sometimes you can work really hard to make no
money.**"* Which is why *"you build this into your pricing, your payment terms, contracts and overhead."*

**Myth 3 — "I'm a creative, not a manager."** ★★ **The best-in-the-world test, which converts a quality
objection into a pricing diagnosis:** *"do you believe that no one else is as good as you? Are you the best
in town, best in state, best in country, maybe even best in the world? Okay — well, probably not… **so
there are other people who are at least as good as you, if not better** — and by hiring them their work
would be better. **The problem is you can't afford to hire them and to make money. That's usually a sign
that you're undercharging.**"*

★★ **The test, stated as arithmetic**: *"ask yourself **how much would it cost to pay someone else to do
comparable work.** There should still be enough money to pay them **and** you, and have **30% profit
margins** after you've paid for everything."*

★★ **The true-cost stack**, built up one layer at a time: bid at **production cost (COGS)** only and *"you
would break even **if you do it well** — and often what happens is you don't run it well, so you actually
spend more time producing the work, so you're going to lose money. **Do this consistently enough and you'll
go broke.**"* Missing layers: **management** (overseeing the work), **overhead** (rent, utilities, software
licences), and then **profit** — *"without profit you can't account for uncertainty, or build a better
future for yourself and for your loved ones."* Only that total is the true cost, *"and this is how you
should be bidding."*

★★ **The delegation math, credited to his business coach.** The objection is that a hire performs at
*"50, 60, 70%"* of you, so you lose productivity managing them. *"**And that's true if you hire one
person.** But something my business coach helped me to understand is: when you hire **three** people, the
total productivity you're getting is **150% of the capacity of what you're able to do.**"*

★★ **The EHR rule** — the most transferable single idea here. At an effective hourly rate of **$100/hr**
(his illustration), doing your own bookkeeping at a bookkeeper's **$25/hr** costs the company **$75/hr**;
doing your own production art against a **$30/hr** production artist costs **$70/hr**. *"**Anytime you can
pay someone else less money to do something that you don't need to do — for less money than you're worth —
you should hire them.** Otherwise you're losing money for the company and **being a bad steward of the
company.**"* With the aside: *"besides, bookkeepers do a much better job of managing books than you do, if
you're anything like me."*

## Key claims (2024-10-24)

- ★★ **Time is the constraint, not money** — Drucker's discretionary-time mandate as the job description of
  an owner.
- ★★ **A client who expects you to hire is not being cheated** — the belief that doing it yourself is more
  honest is the founder's projection, not the client's standard.
- ★★ **Clients defect because of neglected relationships, not better/cheaper alternatives.**
- ★★ **"You can't afford good people" is a pricing diagnosis, not a market fact.**
- ★★ **A correct bid = production + management + overhead + profit**, with a **30% margin** target after
  paying comparable talent.
- ★★ **Three hires ≈ 150% of your own capacity**, even though each is 50–70% of you individually.
- ★★ **Doing sub-EHR work is a stewardship failure**, not thrift.
- ★ **Worst-case-scenario thinking** named as the specific cognitive habit blocking the first hire.
