# Want YOUR Logo To STAND OUT? Watch This Now

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do solo, to camera.** Fully persona-eligible. Short (12KB) and unusually
> craft-focused: he spends most of the corpus arguing that aesthetics are *not* the point, and opens this
> one by suspending that position on purpose.

## Summary

★★ **The setup is a deliberate exception to his own doctrine**: *"whenever there's a new logo or rebrand
refresh the internet goes crazy, especially designers who talk about the aesthetics of it — **you know how
I feel about that. But I'm actually going to talk about aesthetics on this episode.**"*

**The case he picks is Under Armour**, and the critique is structural rather than a matter of preference:
the mark is *"two half ellipses that overlap and intersect… flipped upside down so you can see the X in
it"* — *"a U and an A overlapping."* ★★ **His diagnosis is a failure mode of the pitch room, not of the
designer's eye**: *"I can imagine being in the board of directors… where the branding design firm presents
this thing: **there's a U, there's an A, and there's an X in it — it's the X-factor of Under Armour, isn't
that cool.** And everybody applauds and says let's just adopt that. **This is one of those moments where
the idea could be so strong that you approve it beyond the aesthetics.**"* His verdict on the form is blunt
— *"the aesthetics of this mark is **horrific**"*, *"the form is just, I hate to say it, **it's like ass**"*
— hedged only by *"I know I'm being a bit of a design snob here, and **I'm not saying I have better taste or
aesthetic level than you.**"*

★ **Why he thinks it matters commercially** (i.e. why this isn't just taste): *"whenever we go out in the
world and we're going to wear something where the logo is very prominent, **it's part of the tribe that we
join — it's the symbol we can rally behind. It's got to look good.**"* And the plain statement of purpose
underneath: *"**what is the purpose of any marketing or design effort? It's to bring in new customers and to
keep the existing customers really happy.**"*

### ★★ The tests he actually applies

- **Mock it everywhere first**: *"put it on a uniform, put it on a truck, a plane, put it as wrapping paper
  or tape for a box you're going to ship out — **find as many applications as possible.**"*
- **Then check**: is it still recognisable; is it iconic; is it appropriately sized for its use. *"Some
  things look really good small but when scaled up look terrible — so sometimes you have to design a **more
  responsive logo**, so it can have multiple configurations or slight modifications and look good at all
  sizes."*
- ★ **The fatigue test**, which he calls out as rarely asked: *"**does it feel like it's going to get tiring
  to look at?** Are you going to get **visual fatigue** from it? Does it inspire you?"*
- ★★ **The taste test, and his admission about taste itself**: *"this is the hardest test of them all —
  does someone who has a good sense of taste and aesthetic (**which we're not all born with, and some of us
  never can find**) … you need to **bring in the people who have good taste** and say: do you like this,
  and tell us what you like or don't like about it."*
- ★★ **The mood-board test** — the most portable idea in the episode: *"build a mood board with all the
  brands you love **that have withstood the test of time** — nothing younger than 10 years old, **preferably
  30, 50 years old**, because now it's survived a couple of different shifts in design and trends. Put your
  logo and your mockup next to that board. **If it looks like it belongs there, you've done a great job.**"*
- ★ **Disagreement among tastemakers is itself the signal**: *"if you have a bunch of tastemakers and you're
  **not getting consistent feedback across the board**, you're running the risk here — and **unless you're a
  really forward, bleeding-edge kind of company, it's not a risk you want to take.**"*

### ★★ On changing a logo at all

*"Ideally when you create a logo **it begins a story that you don't want to change.** It can evolve, but you
don't really want to change it — because changing it means **all the brand equity, all the recognition
that's built into it… you're going to have to start over.**"*

**The F1 rebrand as the worked example, with the honest ending**: the fans, the automotive enthusiasts and
*"the logo geeks came out of the woodwork to say this is a terrible logo. **But five, six years later it's
quieted down massively and now no one complains about it.**"* The cost is still real though: *"you run the
risk of alienating a lot of people and having people talk about your company and your logo in not such a
good way. **That's not good PR.**"*

### ★★ Trend vs. fad

*"**When people use the word trend, they misuse it. What they mean is fad.**"* His split: **trends** are
things like *"equality, inclusion — those are not fads"*; **fads** are *"certain elliptical shapes that were
on trend for a hot minute and then went out of trend just as quickly"*, or frozen yogurt, *"which seems to
come in cycles every 10, 15 years"*, or *"**oh, those bell-bottom pants don't look so good on me now.**"*

★★ **And the rule he derives, which is the line most worth keeping**: *"the people who figured out design —
the masters, in **Switzerland and Germany**, decades ago — figured out what works… **golden principles of
ratios, harmonies, certain geometric shapes that just work**, and when you look back at those marks they
withstand the test of time. Everybody's trying to reinvent the rules. **I don't think you need to reinvent
them — you just have to find the right application.** So rather than be so weird and unique in **breaking**
all the rules, **be weird and unique in your application of the rules.**"*

With the identity argument behind it: *"**the worst thing you want to do is to do what everyone is doing.
The whole point of identity is to be unique, and to be different, and to be slightly weird if anything
else.**"*

### ★★ How to hire the firm (advice aimed at the buyer, not the designer)

- ★★ **Read the portfolio as a prediction, not a capability list.** *"Look at their body of work **to see if
  you see your future in there.** We tend to think that **if you can design something, you can design me
  anything — and that's the problem. I've made this mistake before**: to look at gaps in the portfolio and
  think *of course they can figure this stuff out.* But **what I usually wind up getting is more of what
  they've already done.**"* Hence: *"if they have a long track record of doing something you like, there's
  a high probability they're going to do that thing again for you. **So if you don't see it, use
  caution.**"*
- ★★ **The one interview question he recommends**: *"**tell me what the ideal client working relationship
  looks like to you. What happens, where does it go wrong, and how does this work in your mind?**"* — and
  then *"**just let them talk themselves in or out of the job.** Often times they reveal that **they have no
  process**, they're approaching every single problem as a brand new thing — **and that's not a good thing
  for you.**"*

### ★★ On generating a logo with AI

*"If you're thinking about using ChatGPT or one of these generative AI engines to make you a logo, **I would
ask you to reconsider what you're getting.** … **You're not just buying the mark.** If you showed me an
award-winning logo and asked any capable designer *can you draw this logo* — **of course they can. That's
not the point.**"* What is being bought instead: *"large agencies and design firms will go and do research,
competitive analysis, they'll go into the history of your company… **unless you can prompt an engine to do
this, it's not going to happen.** So **it's not about the formal qualities — what it looks like — it's all
the research they do to arrive at a conclusion**, so that you can feel confident it's worthwhile to now
spend hundreds of thousands, if not millions, replicating this logo across multiple touchpoints."*

★ **His own company as the small-scale evidence**: *"**we're a pretty small company** — every time we change
one thing, the colour palette or a secondary typeface, **it has massive implications** across the few
applications we have. And just to think if a company like ours was **a thousand times bigger**, what kind of
headache that would be."*

## Key claims (2024-11-20)

- ★★ **A strong idea can get a bad form approved** — the Under Armour mark as his example of the pitch-room
  failure mode.
- ★★ **A logo must be mocked into many applications before judgement**, and pass recognisability, scale and
  **visual-fatigue** tests.
- ★★ **The mood-board test**: place it beside brands 30–50 years old; belonging is the pass condition.
- ★★ **Inconsistent tastemaker feedback is a risk signal**, not a tie to be broken.
- ★★ **Redesign costs brand equity** — a logo *"begins a story you don't want to change"*; F1 recovered, but
  paid in PR.
- ★★ **Trend ≠ fad** — chase neither by default; *"the worst thing you want to do is what everyone is
  doing."*
- ★★ **Be weird in the application of the rules, not in breaking them** — the Swiss/German canon still holds.
- ★★ **A portfolio predicts what you'll get, not what a firm could do** — and he names having made the
  opposite mistake himself.
- ★★ **What clients pay tens or hundreds of thousands for is the research, not the mark** — which is
  precisely what a generative engine does not supply.
