## ★★★ The "I'm proud of you" argument, in full

The 10-31 source states the conclusion. **This one shows the whole path to it**, and it starts with an
objection from inside his own house.

**The prompt.** *"I remember one time my wife and I were talking… [our oldest son] is 20, turning 21 soon,
and he's very high achieving. And my wife's like: **why are you so thrifty with giving him praise and
saying to him you're proud of him?** I said, well, I could make a greater effort — and then I started to
really think about this."*

**The second prompt**, from a conversation with a mutual friend ([[../entities/anneli-hansen|Anneli]]):
*"I said, you know, **I feel really uncomfortable saying I'm proud**, and I want to tell you why… She was
like: **whoa, wait a minute — you mean you're not proud of your friends?** I'm like: **no. What right do I
have to be proud of them?**"* — *"and so this is shocking to her. Well, she goes: *well, I'm proud of you.*
I'm like: **well… hm. Let me explain.**"*

**The argument itself:**

- ★★★ *"**If I say I'm proud of you, that means there are times when I'm not proud of you** — pretty
  clearly."*
- ★★★ *"**I think most parents use that phrase as a means to control their children.** You might not think
  of it like that, but often it's: *oh, you got a great job, you went to school, you studied the thing I
  wanted you to study — and I'm proud of you.* Or: *you gave money to charity, I'm proud of you, because
  that's what we want you to do.*"*
- ★★★ *"I think, without people realising it, **it is violent language** — because you're saying: **I have
  power over you. My opinion, my authority, my approval matters to you.**"*
- ★★★ *"**Part of loving someone unconditionally — unconditional means without condition.** So I love my
  children, I would lay down my life for my children, and there's nothing I would not do for them."*
- ★★★ **The resolution of the paradox, which is the part the 10-31 telling lacks**: *"if my children don't
  get good grades, don't go to a good school, don't do their homework, get in trouble with the law, get into
  all kinds of things — **I'm so proud of you, because I'm proud that you are my child.** … **I don't want
  to be *proud of* them. I *am* proud of them, always — and there isn't a time that I'm not.**"*

★★★ **What he actually says to them**, fuller than the 10-31 version: *"I've been telling them this since
they were little children, barely old enough to understand the words… **I want you to know that I love you,
and that there is nothing that you could ever do to make me stop loving you, and you don't need to do
anything different than to be you for me to love you — and if anyone else ever tells you [otherwise] in
your life, it is completely untrue.**"*

★★★ **And the therapist's correction, which is new and specific**: *"when I told my therapist that this is
what I was saying, she goes: **just don't put any question marks on it.** Because I used to say *do you know
Daddy loves you?* She said: **turn that into a statement. Dad loves you.**"*

★★ **The cost he's identifying**, stated as a mechanism: *"they start to **externalise their own self-worth**
to you — and when you aren't around, either you pass away or they're in a different circle, **they're
looking to someone else as a surrogate to replace you.** And then someone else has control over your
children. **And I don't want to do that.**"* Supporting citation, to a spiritual teacher (captioned
*"yogi said Guru"* — **Sadhguru**): *"**children are born perfect; it's their parents who mess them up** —
so the best thing you can do as a parent is **to do as little harm as possible.** It's impossible not to do
harm."* His examples of the harm: *"why can't you ever be on time? Why are you so clumsy? — **it's just a
little moment of frustration in your life, you don't really mean those things, but your child processed
these in a whole different way.**"*

★ **He explicitly invites disagreement**: *"if this is what you say to your friends and to your loved ones,
don't let my crazy ideas about this [stop you]… **let me know if you think I'm crazy**, or maybe this is a
point of view that more parents have to have."*

★★ **And he applies it to himself asymmetrically**: *"if somebody wants to say they're proud of me, **I'm
good with it. I don't feel offended at all** — because, here's the dark ugly unfiltered truth, **I don't
really care what you say.** That's the bottom line. **But I know a lot of people do.**"*

★★ **The framing lesson he draws from Jule's version going badly online**: *"if you said **here's why I
don't like this phrase, and I'll tell you my reasoning** — versus **you shouldn't say this**… now you're
criticising me. **It's a small but very big difference between those two things.**"* And the comic
capstone, from a colleague: *"he was saying *I'm proud of them for doing this*, and I explained it to him,
and then he was just giving me a hard time — he goes: ★ ***Chris, I'm very proud that you told me that I
shouldn't be proud.*** **That wanker.**"*

## ★★★ Biography — the caretaker, and a second account of why he sought therapy

★★★ **He self-identifies as a caretaker without hesitation.** Jule: *"I feel like you have a lot of
caretaking attributes yourself."* Chris: *"**sure do. Middle child.**"* Jule's framing of why it surprises
people: *"because of your public persona you come off like super ruthless — **people have called you the
razor blade** — and then to really get to know you, and watch you with some of these friendships… oh:
caretaking."*

★★★ **The therapist, again — and the profiling method.** *"I didn't know that's what I was doing until I
went to see **my therapist Joan**, and Joan told me **it's pretty classic middle-child stuff.** Before she
did any work with me she wanted to know **my parents' history, our family tree and structure, and birth
order** — because it gave her a psychological profile, and probably an emotional profile too."*

> ⚠️ **A SECOND, DIFFERENT ACCOUNT OF WHY HE SOUGHT HER — flag, do not merge.**
> [[2024-10-31-yt-U1anI0b1nvI]] (2024-10-31) gives the reason as **violent thoughts about his own
> children**, on his business coach's recommendation. **Here** he gives it as a workplace realisation:
> *"**the reason why I came to her was because I realised I was more concerned about employees who
> disagreed with me — in their mental welfare — than my own mental welfare.** And so I would acquiesce, but
> I did it in a way I can see now was **passive aggressive**: *okay, I don't want you to do that, but I'm
> not going to get into a fight with you right now, let water go under the bridge.* **But then it would
> build up a lot of resentment inside of me.**"*
> These may be two distinct threads of the same therapy, or two framings chosen for two audiences. **Both
> are recorded, dated, and attributed; neither is presented as the reason.** Resolve only if a third source
> settles it.

★★ **The rule he took from it**, credited: *"I'm not one to say I'm in the **Church of Brené Brown**, but
there's a lot of truth to what she says — **choose discomfort over resentment** when it comes to boundaries
and speaking up. And I try to remember that every single time."* His gloss: *"that little bit of discomfort
would save our relationship from growing into a state of resentment, in which **I will one day just
disappear.**"*

★★★ **How he actually ends relationships — and it contradicts his public image.** *"Eventually I just get
tired of people. I'm like: no, I don't want to be around you anymore. And **the way I do that is I recede
into the background. I just don't call you back. I don't respond.** It's not like I need to have a big
come-to-Jesus conversation with folks."* Jule is openly surprised: *"I'm a little surprised, because **I see
you as someone who would just speak your mind.**"* His mechanism for how it accumulates: *"you let one
transgression go by, and then another, and another… **or maybe it's just death by a thousand paper cuts.**"*

★★★ **The inversion he notes about himself**: *"here's the classic irony of all this stuff — **I have an
easier time telling people that I know, like and trust what I really think, versus strangers.** You would
think it would be the opposite."*

★★ **On whether the caretaker instinct is gone**: *"the way I look at it is **my caretaker tendencies
haven't gone away. They're just there, and I have to be mindful.**"* His analogy is drawn from asking people
with drinking problems whether they were cured: *"almost to the person — **you're never cured of
alcoholism, you're just trying to get through it day by day.** And you know that if you start to tell
yourself you're cured, you can slip back down that path really quickly."*

★★ **On timing his own inner work**: *"the person that you meet today in the social space — **because I did
this inner work before I went on social media** — is that I have a better sense of who I am and the
boundaries I want to set."*

★ **On his own reaction to children being cared for**: *"when I see organizations take really good care of
children I get really emotional… I'm really happy for those children, but also **kind of pining for my own
childhood — I wish I had this when I was growing up.**"*

## ★★★ The genealogy-of-ideas exercise

Offered to anyone reacting badly to the episode, and credited (to Austin Kleon, from Chris's own earlier
interview with him):

> *"Write down **10 things that you hold to be true** — 10 beliefs that you have, whatever they might be.
> … He said try this thing where you do **a genealogy of your ideas** — he traced *steal like an artist*
> back as far as he could until he found the mother or father of the idea. **If you have beliefs, retrace
> how you came to believe this thing** — it can almost always be traced back to your childhood. Then see
> where it came from: a book, a TV show, a sibling, a parent. And then ask yourself: ★★★ **how reliable is
> the source of information? What was it in response to? And if it weren't in your own childhood, and
> somebody had said this to you as an adult, would you still believe this?** I think this is where
> everything starts to become unravelled."*

## ★★ On "normal", and on conviction

*"**How do we even know what normal is?** You have finite experience — you might know 10, 12 people well
enough to say *that's normal*, and the rest of the people are just assumptions."* Then the harder half:
★★ *"**even if it were normal and it were bad, should we not do this anymore?** … If the entire country
wants to hurt people, do you say *well, that's what normal is, let's go do normal*? **If normal is bad,
should you do that? I think no. That's what it means to have conviction, and to have a moral code that you
live by.**"*

## ★★ On respect — a genuinely unresolved exploration

Jule brings the question and Chris works it in the open without landing cleanly. Worth keeping precisely
because it is unfinished.

- ★★ **His opening split**: *"**I can respect the way somebody does something but not respect them.** … I'm
  not king of the world. **No one elected me**, and I'm not looking to have that kind of dominion or control
  over people."*
- ★★ **Multiple truths**: *"there can be **multiple versions of — air quotes — truths**, and both of our
  realities can be real and both can be false, until there's objectively verifiable data. Then we just don't
  know."*
- ★★★ **The worked example is his own business model, and he refuses to rank it.** *"I'm very much
  soft-spoken, **inbound**, building content and getting people to gather around saying *Chris, we want to
  give you something* — and there are people who are like: **no, you must be proactive, you must go outbound
  and ask for opportunities all the time.** Those are two polar-opposite ideas. **I think they can work in
  harmony. One is not better than the other — it's whatever works for you.**"* His named exemplar of the
  opposite is **[[2024-09-12-yt-HtZDQ9xoyeE|Neel Dhingra]]**: *"he's literally — I'm just going to call
  people, I'm going to DM every single one of them… and he's very, very successful, he's made a lot of
  money. And **just the mere thought of that makes me want to crawl back in bed. Wake me up when it's
  Wednesday**, because I don't want to do that. And probably for him, the kinds of things that I do make him
  like: **dude, that's infuriating — why would you sit around and wait for it to happen?**"*
- ★★ **On snap judgements**: the example is men wearing baggy low-slung trousers — *"you're like, I can't
  take you seriously. **But that person could be the CEO of a company, or have graduated from an Ivy League
  school.**"* He declines to call it purely a fault: *"I don't think it's necessarily horrible that we do
  that, because **it's a way of using our brain efficiently** — we're not processing every single person on
  an individual level."* But: ★★ *"**they didn't have enough data points.** One or two things should not be
  the entire thing… and that's where it gets really dangerous."*
- ★★ **Applied to himself**: *"people might see one video of me and think **what an arrogant a-hole, who's
  just talking out of both sides of his mouth, who doesn't know what he's doing** — and then they might see
  me in the street and have a real conversation and say: *wow Chris, I was wrong about you.* **It takes a
  lot, especially for men, to say I was wrong**… and I really do appreciate that."*
- ★★ **Where he lands, provisionally**: *"I give people **lots of latitude**, because **I know I'm an
  imperfect person myself. I do dumb stupid things** — hopefully less as I get older, but I still do dumb
  stupid things all the time. It's not fair to judge myself such that every time I mess up I'm like *you
  don't deserve respect or love*. … So — **I respect the fact that people have a different way of moving in
  the world**, and when they do enough [things] that hint at a core value, then something feels funny
  inside: ★★ **I don't know if I want to open the fullness of my heart to this person, because there's
  something misaligned in values.**"*

## ⚠️ FENCED — Jule Kim's material, attributed to her

Her professional argument, which is the episode's nominal subject: *"anyone can call themselves a coach…
there are very young, inexperienced people who have undergone little to no training."* Her position on the
industry's claim that *"you don't have to know anything about the person's industry — all you have to have
is the skill of coaching"*: *"**that's only partially true.**"* The expertise she says is non-negotiable is
*"just **experience with people** — and when you're 21 I feel like you barely know yourself, if at all."*
She is **ICF-certified**, ~**4 years** in, and notes the ethics board and one specific obligation:
*"**you do not sell yourself as rent-a-friend**"* — if a client is veering off their own stated goal, **you
must have the conversation.** Her worked example (a two-year client who agreed to everything and then went
silent) includes her line *"**I don't know if I should continue taking your money**"*, the client's reply
that what she heard was *"you're not worthy and I'm going to leave you"*, and Jule's admiration that the
client **owned her own reaction**. Her structural observation about her own profession: the coaching
personality assessment consistently scores **caretaking** highest, producing *"avoidance, fear of conflict,
people-pleasing"*, and *"**less than a 50% chance**"* that another coach would have had that conversation.
Also hers: the dictionary-vs-usage split on **respect** (*"full awareness and appreciation for a person's
humanity"* vs. the conditional everyday sense), and her framing of it as the intersection of **values,
boundaries and resonance**.
