# How to land YOUR DREAM client in 2025

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do, live to a small room**, taking questions and referring to people present.
> Fully persona-eligible. Framed explicitly as *"some business 101 for 2024 moving to 2025."*
>
> ★★★ **A landmark. Two named frameworks that are new to the corpus** — the **9-1-1 content ratio** and
> **"all strategies are autobiographical"** — plus the best statement of his niching argument and a
> genuinely new sales idea (**what clients want to avoid**).

## ★★★ "All strategies are autobiographical" — the caveat he opens with

Before saying anything, he disclaims the whole talk: *"a friend of mine said something like — **all
strategies are autobiographical.** **I'm only going to tell you to do things that I've done, that I like and
think work.** But that means **you have to be aware of bias**, and of the certain factors that contributed
to the decisions I made — and **make sure you map it to what you do.**"*

★★★ **And the instruction for disagreement**, which is the useful part: *"often times if I say something and
it doesn't quite map with what you're doing, **don't fight the whole idea. Just understand the idea, and
then later think to yourself: how might I use those ideas and tailor it for me.** … **We don't want to throw
the baby out with the bathwater** — some parts we keep, some parts we can toss out."*

> ★★ **Pairs directly with** *"I'm borrowing concepts that I barely understand, but I package them in a way
> that might help you apply it"* from [[2024-12-31-yt-5_RMSS8Mf30]] a week earlier. **Two independent
> epistemic-humility markers in eight days** — this is a stable feature of how he frames advice, not a
> one-off.

## ★★★ The 9-1-1 ratio

His answer to the obvious objection (*"well Chris, we're going to go poor, because you're not allowing us to
advertise ever — that's a reasonable conclusion to what I'm saying"*):

> ★★★ *"In America, when there's an emergency, we dial **911**… and right now I'm just going to guess **your
> social media content needs a lot of help.** No one cares, no one's reading it, **engagement is dead.** So
> you need to resuscitate — we call 911."*

| | What | His constraint |
|---|---|---|
| **9** | **pieces of pure value-based content** | *"all value. **Do not ask for anything.** Don't be sneaky about some offer — just give me some insights."* |
| **1** | **personal post** | *"**not about your cat or your dog**, but something unusual about you that makes you a little bit different… you like to hike, you like to go fishing — write about that."* |
| **1** | **hardcore ad** | *"buy my stuff — **and you won't need to apologise for it.**"* |

★★★ **Why the personal post is in there, with himself named as the offender**: *"a lot of people, **myself
included, hide behind all-value educational content** — and then people are like: **who the hell are you?
You're putting out faceless tips. They're helpful, but I don't know who you are** — and **I need to know who
you are to connect with you on an emotional level.**"*

★★★ **And the principle underneath the whole ratio**: *"the reason we don't do hardcore ads — *hire me*, or
whatever it is — is because **we haven't earned the right to ask for it.** That's all it is."* The payoff he
describes: *"if you give so much value, eventually they're going to say **what do you make? I need your
help** — and they'll ask for the call, or **slip into your DMs**, or check out your profile. **That's the
time to follow up.**"*

## ★★★ Niching, argued three ways

**1. As a choice about suffering.** *"I think you have to just **make an adult choice** here, which is:
★★★ **how long do you want to suffer for? How long do you want to work in relative anonymity, and compete
at the bottom level, and get paid almost nothing for what you do?** **That** would determine if you should
niche or not."*

★★ **Who is exempt**: *"in very few instances will it work in your favour to be a generalist. Usually
**executives like CEOs** are better off as generalists, **managers** are better off as generalists,
sometimes **salespeople**. But **everybody who has their hand in the work — it's almost always better that
you do something repeatedly.**"*

**2. ★★★ As cognitive load on the buyer.** On the multi-slash introduction (*"I'm a photographer, an
illustrator, a graphic designer, I build websites and I do development work"* — *"people really say this to
me, and they even throw in **I'm an artist as well, and I sing and dance**"*):

> ★★★ *"**You're doing a cognitive overload to the other person's brain.** You're asking them: *I'm really
> important — please stop thinking about your own problems, and use all of your brain power to remember what
> it is I do and find the connective tissue between all these things.* What will ultimately happen is
> **you'll short-circuit their brain. They won't remember anything, or they'll remember the wrong thing.**"*

**3. ★★★ As an inversion of the competition logic** — the sharpest of the three. Buyers *"don't want
mediocre, they don't want middle of the road — **they'd like to hire the best that they could afford**"*
(*"you don't want to take your family out to eat an Italian dinner that's at the bottom of the barrel"*).
So listing five services means: *"you compete with all the photographers who are at the top of their game,
**and then** all the illustrators, **and then** all the graphic designers. So **what you've inadvertently
done is invited MORE competition, not less** — and ★★★ **the goal of positioning and marketing is to reduce
or eliminate competition.**"*

★★ **The calibration, credited to David Baker** (*The Business of Expertise*): **one** competitor is *too
few* — *"the market is not big enough to support you"*; **10,000** is *"way too broad and too generic"*;
the target is roughly **10–50 competitors**. Plus the line he clearly enjoys: *"**if you think you've niched
enough — he says you haven't.** Everybody thinks they've niched, and then when I ask them what they do or
who they serve, **it's just too broad.**"*

★★ **The shorthand**: ★★ **niche = market + passion.** *"Who you serve, plus what you're passionate about…
**we don't have to make this so complicated.**"* His worked example: *"I help certain types of entrepreneurs,
and what I'm passionate about is people who want to **start gyms for women trying to recover from
pregnancy.**"* And the reasoning: *"**why would you want to build a career doing something you're not
passionate about?**"*

> ⚠️ **MISATTRIBUTION — flagged, not propagated.** He credits *Book Yourself Solid* to *"**Michael P
> Porter**"*, hesitating audibly (*"Is it Michael Porter? Has anybody read that book?"*). ***Book Yourself
> Solid* is by Michael Port.** Michael E. Porter is the Harvard competitive-strategy academic — **a
> different person.** He hedges, so this is a misremembering rather than an assertion. **Record the idea,
> attribute it to Michael Port, and do not carry the error into any topic or persona page.** (Fidelity
> rule 5.)

## ★★★ Low variability — and being *safe* to buy

★★★ *"Here's another line I want to burn into your brain: **low variability in process equals low
variability in outcome.** **If your process is different every single time, there's a strong chance
[the outcome] is going to be different every single time.**"* — credited to **Blair Enns**.

★★★ **Why it is a sales argument and not an ops one**: *"**predictable outcomes are valuable because it
communicates low risk.** … If you have consistent outcomes **you look less risky — that means you're safe.
You want to be safe in the eyes of the buyer.**"* His analogy is a car plant: a mass-produced Mercedes or
BMW works a specific way *"because they keep manufacturing the car the same way — **if they open a new
plant, hire a new plant manager, there's a new assembly process, the cars might break down more often.**"*

★★ **So publish the process**: *"if you have a defined process, **you should publish said process in as many
places as possible**… **you'll be a sought-after keynote speaker**, because a lot of people in the industry
are going to want to learn how you keep getting these great consistent results."*

★★ **And teachability is the proof of it.** Pointing at a couple in the room who restore furniture:
*"**they can teach other people how to do this**, and have great confidence that relatively new people can
get into it and have similar outcomes. **We know they've done this many times, because they can teach it —
and their students are able to achieve similar results.**"*

> ✅ **Connects to** [[2024-12-26-yt-MIyLNmejcVY]] (*"being an entrepreneur is synonymous with being an
> educator"*) and [[2024-12-31-yt-5_RMSS8Mf30]] (*"if you can teach what you're doing while you're doing it,
> you've advanced to the next level"*). **Three statements in three weeks** that teachability is the test of
> mastery. Promote as one idea.

★★ **The consistency argument in pop terms**: *"**Taylor Swift — maybe not the best artist in the world —
is a billionaire**… because she knows how to make a hit after hit, and **she knows how to produce a show
that's very consistent.** The Beatles know how to consistently put out hits. And then there's **the one-hit
wonders, like The Verve** — one great song, *Bittersweet Symphony*, and that was kind of it. ★★ **Do you
want to be the one-hit wonder, or do you want to be the Beatles?**"*

## ★★★ Two more genuinely new ideas

**1. ★★★ Social media as the new résumé.** From a summit *"amongst really influential people"*: *"has your
social media following become **the new resume**?"* His own turn on it: ★★★ *"**when was the last time you
looked at someone's résumé and believed it?** Everybody fudges — **I see people who write *Stanford* but
they went to school for one semester and dropped out.**"* Whereas *"**it's harder to fake a highly engaged
social media account with pieces of thought leadership where you're speaking and sharing what you know
in the voice that's yours.**"* And the tell for the fakes: *"people **regurgitate other people's content** —
those people are not building thought leadership… **it sounds generic. They never put their voice or their
signature on it. You can just tell.**"*

**2. ★★★ What clients want to *avoid*.** *"The problem with most creative people: **we spend so much time
thinking about what we do that we have so little time to understand what the clients hate.**"* The
correction: *"there's **both** things they want **and** things they want to avoid — ★★★ **sometimes we think
too much about what they want, and not the things they want to avoid**, and we need to address that."* His
concrete example, which is the kind of detail the corpus lacked: *"**most corporate people are worried
about… that they're not going to look good on camera.**"*

★★★ **The quick win, and "the journey before the journey."** He takes it from a personal experience —
★ *"I recently went on a **week-long tour of Ireland** with some of my professional friends, and the event
organiser sent me **a book that describes the things we're going to see**. So ★★★ **he's created the journey
before the journey.**"*

Applied to the camera-shy client: *"produce a video and share it with **every single client**, because now
you have a process. *Thank you for bringing us on — here's a video that's going to take you through how this
is going to work, how you can be your most present, charming self on camera.* … Something that takes you a
little time to do **that you can repurpose forever.** … **You're giving me value, and you're also
communicating: this ain't my first rodeo.**"* Keep it *"under five minutes."*

★★ **And the training instruction**: *"**train your mind: whenever you go to buy something, whenever you
experience something — what are they doing to give you a quick win?** Sometimes you go into a fancy
restaurant, you sit down, you're still looking at the menu, and they say: *oh, a gift from the chef.* That's
a quick win. **It's being done all around you by very smart business people.**"*
