# How to Get Clients to Value YOU — (Whiteboard AdobeMAX)

> **ATTRIBUTION — Chris Do solo, live at Adobe MAX**, working a whiteboard with named participants from the
> floor. Fully persona-eligible. Participants' own situations are context, not persona data.
>
> ⚠️ **DATING TRAP.** Published **2025-01-16**, but Adobe MAX is an **autumn** conference — this is almost
> certainly an **October 2024** recording. Date the ideas to the talk, not the upload.
>
> ★★★ **A landmark.** Two frameworks the corpus does not have (**radical responsibility**, **the
> Belief→Action→Result reset**), the fullest telling of **"you are the prize"** and of **guarantees as
> priced risk transfer**, and a **self-implicating financial admission** about the Blind years.

## ★★★ "I like to create unsafe space"

His stated teaching philosophy, offered as the frame for the whole session:

> ★★★ *"Everybody talks about creating **safe space** to learn. I like to say **I like to create unsafe
> space** — because **in the friction is where the real learning happens.** It's the push and pull, the give
> and take between us, that I think gets us to some maybe **more informed truth.** **I'm not saying absolute
> truth** — but more informed."*

★ And on why he prefers a room to a camera: *"a lot of you might see me on a little screen — those things I
hate to do, because **I'm talking to myself most of the time. It's not very exciting.**"*

## ★★★ Radical responsibility

*"The first thing I want to talk about is an idea I learned from **a business coach**: it's called **radical
responsibility**, or **being 100% accountable for everything that happens in your life.** ★★★ **Even things
that are not your fault are your fault** — because we have to make a decision: **how do we get ourselves in
that position or situation such that those things happen to us?**"*

## ★★★ Friendship vs. business — the airport test

A participant is being *"boxed in"* by an agency of close friends: scope with *"very little context"* and
*"no talk of financials."* Chris's sequence is the useful part.

★★ **He tests the premise first**: *"when [they're] your friend — are they **really** your friend? … They
care about your wellbeing? … They want you to do well?"* — and hears the hesitation: *"**there's a lot of
pauses between those yeses.**"*

★★★ **The definition he offers**: *"if I'm sitting at home, I've got nothing to do, but I've got to pick you
up at the airport — and you're like *hey bro, can you pick me up?* — and I'm like: **not at my expense.**
**Am I a friend? No.**"*

★★★ **And where the fault actually lies** — with the participant, not the friends: *"there's a difference
between friendship and business… **he just described a whole scenario where we're business, but he's like:
for the sake of the friendship. He's the one blurring the lines.**"* The conclusion: *"if we don't have
healthy boundaries working with our friends, then what should we do? **Not work with them — or figure out
how to talk about it.**"*

## ★★★ "You are the prize"

Credited carefully: *"there's something that **Blair Enns** says — that **he referred to someone else who
said it**, but I can't quote the original person."*

> ★★★ *"**In the client buying-selling cycle, you are the prize.** … The reason why is because **the client
> has fewer options to do what you do than you have for sources of money.** **What they represent to you is
> money. What you represent to them is a highly honed skill or craft.**"*

★★★ **The individuation argument that carries it**: *"there are a lot of illustrators here — throw a rock,
hit one. A lot of photographers — throw a rock in this booth and you hit one. **But the way that you frame
it, the way you see it, the way you tell your story, your humanity, your compassion — what you bring to it
is uniquely you. They can get something similar, but they can't get you.** … **You're one in eight billion.
Those are good odds.** But **they're just another person with money.**"*

## ★★★ The Belief → Action → Result reset (his "mind reset")

★★★ **The insight is the direction of travel.** *"What people try to do is ask you to **change your belief**
— which is what I initially tried to do… [and he says] *bro, three years, man. It's not going to happen.*
**If we fast forward to the result that he wants, it's much easier for us to create a road map.**"* And the
mechanism: ★★★ *"**the action will change our belief system**, which then will reinforce this part — and now
we're going to make the money that we want."*

**The exercise**, as he gives it: write your **current belief**, the **action** you take, and the **result**
you get. *"Then take a pause, then go back to it and say: **well, what is the result I'd like to have — the
life I want to live? And then what's the action I have to take?**"* ★★ *"**We always start with the
result.**"*

★★ **Desired future state.** *"There's a **current state** — one where my friendship feels like they're
taking advantage of me, I'm feeling resentful, I'm not making any money, I'm questioning my life decisions,
**I'm proving my parents right about making bad decisions in my life.** … And then: what is the **desired
future state**? Well, I'm working less, I'm making more money, I'm working with good people who value me…
**so I can save up money for my kids' tuition.**"*

★ Asked whether it works better alone or with someone: *"**yes. Yes. The answer is yes.**"*

## ★★★ "I am good" — the correction he makes on one word

A participant states her new belief as *"my work is good."*

> ★★★ *"Stop there. **My work is good.** I've noticed now twice that she has used the word **work**. Can we
> switch the word *work* with something else? … **I am good.** … For some reason she keeps using the word
> *work* — **separating herself.**"*
>
> *"**Is it uncomfortable for you to say that?**"* — *"It is, a little bit."* — *"**How does it make you
> feel?**"* — *"A little tender."* — *"**You are good.** … **You are valuable.** You believe that?"*

**Recorded as a coaching pattern, not an anecdote**: he intervenes on a single pronoun-level word choice and
treats it as the actual problem — the same instinct as *"use his language, don't introduce a new word"* and
the *comfortable/makes sense* catch elsewhere in the corpus.

## ★★★ Risk, guarantees, and the project he bought

★★ **Drucker again** (now attested repeatedly): *"**in business, all profit comes from risk.**"* Illustrated
with the risk/return ladder — *"the one with the biggest payout is… tech companies, crypto companies, pretty
much scams… **because you know going in: I might lose all of my money.** That's why they say don't buy
stocks with money you can't afford to lose."* Against a government-backed bond, *"**not risky at all.**"*

★★★ **The self-implicating admission**, which is new to the corpus: *"have you ever been in a project where
**you paid for the project**? **I have — and I think I'm pretty smart.** We have literally spent more money
than what we got, so **I bought the project.**"* And the category: ★★★ *"**if you ever do music videos, you
pay for the project.** So all those music videos you might see on our demo reel? **Lost money. Lost money.
Lost money.**"*

★★ **Why undefined scope is the mechanism**: *"if you don't define the scope clearly, the client's like:
*hey, you didn't say I couldn't do that.* Guess what — **now you're doing it. They have more lawyers than
you. They have more power than you.**"*

★★★ **Guarantees decoded as risk transfer, and then priced.** *"When they ask for a guarantee, what are they
saying to you? … **They don't want any risk.** Therefore… **they have all the ideas and you're assuming all
the risk** — therefore **you have the greatest [chance] to either earn or fall out.**"* The insurance
analogy: *"total collision insurance, total home protection, total health insurance — **complete coverage,
you've got to charge a lot more.**"*

★★★ **The script**, which is the fullest version in the corpus: *"I get this question a lot — *Chris, I
can't guarantee results.* I'm like: **happy to give you a guarantee. Really happy to. It's just going to
cost four times as much.** — *Oh, I don't really want a guarantee like that.* **I see you get out of it.**"*

★ **His framing of the whole segment**: *"I'm trying to build a foundation of economic and financial
knowledge for you… **I promise you this: if you understand this and you execute on this, you'll make more
money than [from] anything you attend today.**"*

## ★★ Niching, the foundation, and the serial killers

★★ **The metaphor**: *"the most important thing [in building a house] is **get the foundation right**… if
it's not level, if it's not square or plumb, you've got problems, **because all the walls are built off that
foundation.** **So your foundation is your core skill** — and a lot of times we tie it to our identity."*

★★ **The joke that lands the point**, on multi-slash introductions: *"**how many people here are serial
killers?** Slashers — like a photographer **slash** model **slash** artist? **You're a serial killer, then.
On the slasher films.**"*

★★ **The line**: *"**be a mile deep and an inch wide.** **Most generalists are a mile wide and an inch
deep** — a little bit of knowledge about a lot of things, but very little depth."* And: *"**the more niched
you are, the more repetition you get doing the same problem.**"*

★★ **The one exception he accepts**, live and on the spot. Asked where a generalist's confidence comes from,
a participant answers *"the ability to bring those deep people together"* — Chris: *"this is excellent…
**it gave me the one answer I can accept. She hires people.**"* Generalised: *"**CEOs and executives are
generalists** — they need to know a little about a lot because they put teams together. They know **just
enough to separate the good people from the BSers. That's all you need to know.**"*

## ★★ Word salad, and communication as a durable skill

★★ *"When we're unclear in our thinking, **we use lots of words** — I think it's called **word salad**…
think about a salad: you toss things around and — what did we eat? **Iceberg lettuce. Zero nutritional
value.**"* And the cause: *"**when you're nervous you speak a lot.** When you have the mic and the camera on
your face, **you will speak a lot.**"*

★★ **The instruction**: *"**keep it simple, stupid.** You use too many words because **words sound nice, but
you're not actually precise** — you're not at that level yet. So just **pretend you're talking to a
five-year-old. I don't even say fifth grader — a five-year-old needs to understand it.**"*

★★ **A dated AI use-case he endorses**: *"we'll teach you how to say it in the most complicated way, and
then **have AI coach you how to say it simply** — you can use **ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity** to help you
learn to speak more simply."*

★★★ **And why it matters now**: *"communication is what they call a **durable skill** — a transferable skill
of the 21st century. **Those who know how to communicate will be leaders. They'll charge more. They'll be
sought after** — **especially in this world now where almost everybody can be replaced by some form of AI.
We need to learn to be better thinkers, critically thinking, and to articulate our ideas.**"*
