## ★★★ Problem solver → problem seeker, and it is **Brian Collins's**

> ★★★ *"You've all been taught to be great problem solvers, but **when I was having a conversation with
> Brian Collins, he says designers need to move from being problem solvers to problem seekers** — and
> there's a big difference there."*

★★★ **The clinical framing of the client that goes with it**: *"when a client comes to you… **they will come
self-diagnosed and self-prescribed.** They'll say *I need a new logo* or *I need a new fill-in-the-blank* —
and this is where your instincts will be to say **okay, I can solve that problem.**"*

★★ **The order-taker image, which IS his and recurs across the corpus**: *"this is us being more of an

★★ **The worked logo dialogue**, and note that it is a **two-move** sequence, not one question: (1) *"I
would love to make you an incredible logo, **but before we do this, what is driving this request?**"* →
client discloses embarrassment (*"my cousin Vinnie designed this"*) and an upcoming launch; (2) *"**will
changing your logo impact the business, and how?**"* → *"I'll feel better… and we're actually going to print
**100,000 pieces of a package**, I don't want to get the wrong logo."*

> ★★★ **What the two moves are actually doing**: *"you're **contrasting the positive points** about looking
> more prestigious… **and you're also looking at some of the negative outcomes in terms of getting it
> wrong.** **What's at stake if we do it well, if we do it poorly — and between that balance is where you're
> going to come in as a designer and thread that needle.**"*

★ **A scope caveat he volunteers**: *"**if you don't do anything other than logo design, this may not be
applicable to you.** I'm talking to designers who are capable of thinking bigger."*

## ★★★ The three why-questions — Jonathan Stark's, with Blair Enns underneath and Chris Voss on top

★★★ Credited: *"this is where I'm going to reference **Jonathan Stark** — **he has the three why
questions.**"* Then, notably, he **teaches tone as a separate skill from the script**: *"first I'll tell you
what the questions are, **then I want to talk about tone and delivery, because that matters a lot.**"*

1. ★★ **Why this** — *"why is it you want to do this, **and why not do something else?**"* Sometimes the
   answer is *"I don't know, that's a darn good question — well, what other options are there?"*, which is
   ★★ *"**an invitation for you to contribute to the conversation.**"*
2. ★★★ **Why now** — *"**why not do nothing? Why not just stop and wait six months?** Because **if we can
   save you money by not doing anything, why proceed?**"* ★★★ **And he names the mechanism and its source**:
   *"in the book **Win Without Pitching Manifesto**, **Blair Enns** says in a buy cycle **we want to retreat
   and see if the client will follow us.**"* Result: *"**you no longer have to convince them they need to
   move forward — now they convince themselves.**"*
3. ★★★ **Why me** — *"**the hardest question to ask, but as important as the others, maybe even more
   important.** … *of all the people you could possibly work with, **why did you choose today to give up
   your very valuable time to speak to me?*** … **This also saves you from them asking you *why should I
   hire you?* — well, they just told you. So you no longer have to play defensive.**"*

> ★★★ **The tone instruction, which is the part most people would skip.** *"If you deliver this in an
> aggressive, accusatory way it's not going to end well — *why would you ever hire me?* — they're like
> **well, really good question, I have no idea why I'd hire you. Hang up the phone.**"* The fix, credited to
> **Chris Voss** in *Never Split the Difference*: *"he describes it as the **late-night FM DJ voice.** I know
> that's a lot of words to remember — **just remember cool jazz, coasting as you're driving. Slow, deep,
> methodical.**"* ★★★ And the grammatical tell: *"**end it on an up note, to insert a question. It's not a
> statement.** *Why me?* — **see the difference?**"*

## ★★★ Portfolio: fewer, deeper, extended, and the perceived-value multiplier

- ★★★ **Snap judgement is the premise**: *"we make a snap judgement as to whether somebody is worth it…
  **and the way we do that is we make decisions visually.** Whatever you can do to package your work in the
  highest possible way — the best mockups, the best lighting — **it communicates on an emotional level that
  this is expensive and this person knows what they're doing.**"*
- ★★★ **Three to five, not breadth**: *"it's best just to put together **three to five of your best work**
  and put all your energy and love into making those amazing case studies… ★★★ **it's better to show fewer
  high-quality pieces than to show a lot of work that doesn't stand up to that same metric of quality.**"*
  Why breadth hurts: *"it **lacks focus**… and **not all the work is at the same level of quality.**"*
- ★★★ **Show the process, not just the artefact**: *"clients look at something really finished and think
  *oh this is wonderful*, **but they have no insight into how you created this work**… show it broken down
  in parts and pieces — **this is the logo, these are the colours, this is the typography** — **in doing so
  you make a small project look more comprehensive, more thought out**, and therefore increase the perceived
  value."*
- ★★★ **The ladder**: take a logo, add *"a texture and a colour palette and start to build out a system —
  **this will help you transform from being a logo designer to an identity designer to a brand
  designer.**"* ★★★ With the number: *"**you can make a project go from $100 to $100,000 in its perceived
  value.**"* ★★ And the guard against doing it lazily: *"**I don't mean just take the logo and slap it on a
  bunch of different applications — that's not showing that you know how to design systems.** Crop the logo,
  put it on a shopping bag, turn it into a pattern."*
- ★★★ **The sales claim**: *"when clients look at your work and **they can see themselves working with you,
  then you've done 50% of the sales work just by your website.**"*
- ★★★ **Copy is half the job**, credited: *"I read something really clever by **Seth Godin** — he said
  **no one wants to read email, they don't care about what you do, they don't care about your education,
  your services. All they care about is themselves. What they want to read is *me-mail*.**"* Hence: *"talk
  about **the problem, the solution and the impact**… **focus on business impact** — increased time on
  website, reduced bounce rate, higher conversion ratio, customer satisfaction."*

★★★ **The early-career trade-up rule, stated with an explicit caution**: *"**I want to say this with some
caution** — in the very early parts of developing your career **it is okay to over-deliver and over-produce,
as long as you meet the client's business objectives.** … **You can take a small project and trade up — but
the only way you can trade up is if you put all of your heart and soul into building the very best you're
capable of.**"*

★★ **And the spec-work story, retold as its own instance of that rule** — wanting work his portfolio didn't
demonstrate: *"I thought **foolishly** I could ask the clients to take that risk, to take a chance on me…
**and guess what — no surprise to anybody — no one ever did and no one ever believed we could do it.**"* So
they self-financed a full 30-second **Mini Cooper** spot, *"the exact same process we would for any client —
write a script, create storyboards, do look frames,"* built with *"sweat equity."* Outcome: *"**that one TV
spot got us a whole bunch of other jobs that looked just like it**, and it even won awards."*
✅ **Consistent with the fuller telling in [[2024-02-09-yt-QH6b8mMEP2g]]** — which carries the ethical bound
(*"you can present anything, just don't lie… spec project, spec project"*) that **this course version omits**.
⚠️ **When promoting, promote the bounded version.**

## ★★★ Customer service — the story he tells against himself

★★★ *"One thing I want to share is **something I've not been very good at myself, admittedly.** I thought
when clients hired us **we were paid to do the best design work we could do, and that was the beginning and
the end of the relationship.** I never really thought about customer service beyond that."* And the
correction: *"**that's just what you paid for.**"*

★★★ **The voicemail.** During the pandemic, from the real-estate broker who sold him his house: *"**it's
been 14 years since we helped you buy your home. I hope everything is going well with you and your kids, and
if you ever need anything let me know.**"* Chris's read is the useful part — he assumes it is systematised,
not sentimental: *"**he's built the system into his routine**… ★★ **I assume I'm not so special that I'm the
only person getting this call.**"*

★★★ **The payoff, years later**: *"my wife and I are going to sell our house and she asked **who should we
call?** — and **without hesitation I said absolutely** [him]. Of all the brokers I've worked with in my
life, **he's the only one who's ever called to touch base.**"*

> ★★★ **And the instruction, delivered against himself**: *"**don't do like what I've done — do as I say.**"*
> The concrete version: *"after you deliver a project, **put into your calendar a follow-up call** to see if
> the clients have any issues… **this keeps the relationship warm versus going cold.**"*

## ★★★ AIDCA — the customer journey, mapped end to end

★★ **Awareness** → *"who are my clients, what are their wants and needs, behaviours, values, habits… **and
where might I show up that is most relevant to where they're going to be?**"* ★★ *"**Be at the right time at
the right place and generate your own luck.**"*
★★ **Interest** → a post plus a gated extra: *"**I just finished an additional seven points — comment below
with a special phrase like *tell me more*.** ★★ **That's permission from them to tell them more**, so you
can drop into their DMs."*
★★ **Desire** → the resource earns trust; on the resulting call *"you're diagnosing the problem, you're
listening carefully, you're trying to surface the deeper motivations."*
★★ **Conversion** → *"**professionalism, being prompt, and a price matched against value.**"*
★★★ **Advocacy — the stage he says everyone drops**: *"a lot of people stop here. **The last part is
advocacy. What are you doing to deliver delight** so that this prospect becomes your client and then becomes
**your biggest salesperson**… **they become an unpaid salesperson for you.**"*

★★ **Delight, with examples**: Amazon (*"glad to renew my Prime membership every single year because of
this"*), Zappos surprise expedited shipping, and **Johnny Cupcakes** — *"sometimes he puts in a little gag
gift, or even money, inside the box."*

★★★ **The instruction that makes it operational rather than a poster**: map it, *"and then **write what
changes you need to make on your website, in your communication, your branding, your design, your
copywriting and your overall brand experience** to ensure that this is the likely outcome."*

## ★★★ Process, speed, and two money mechanics

- ★★★ **Transparency as risk reduction**, credited (caption reconstruction — see notes): *"**[Blair Enns]
  said this: when there's low variance in process, there's low variance in outcome. The greater the variance
  in outcome, the higher the risk you seem to be.**"*
- ★★★ **Anticipating the post-delivery problem as a sales move** — the website-maintenance example: telling
  a client up front that *"what they don't often think about is what happens afterwards… **therefore I've
  anticipated this and built in an affordable maintenance program.**"* What it signals: *"**this is
  obviously not your first rodeo… you're so thorough in your process that you can even anticipate what's
  going to happen after you deliver.**"*
- ★★★ **Speed is worth money — a direct contradiction of the effort heuristic**: *"**most creatives falsely
  assume that clients want you to spend more time working on something** and therefore are working harder
  and earning your money — **when in fact what they care about is saving time.** **They will actually pay
  more to get something done in less time.**"* With the everyday proof: *"**if you want to avoid the line at
  Disney there's a special pass**… if you want to board first on an airplane **it's called first class.**"*
  ★★ And an example from the room he is literally sitting in: *"right now I'm delivering this course — **I
  want drapery so I can control the light in this room** — and if the vendor could deliver to me faster it
  would be worth more to me."*
- ★★ **The efficiency tip, credited to [[../entities/aaron-draplin]]**: *"anytime you watch Aaron work
  you'll see **he has mockups ready to go built into his palettes in Illustrator** — a t-shirt, a cap, an
  apron… **he's not searching on his hard drive for the file.**"*
- ★★★ **Insider mechanic 1 — the discretionary-budget threshold**: *"some clients have a **discretionary
  budget** where **if they spend less than this amount, no one else has to approve it.** So **if you know
  this amount** and you have this kind of rapport, **you probably want to submit the bid just underneath
  that number.**"*
- ★★★ **Insider mechanic 2 — the phased engagement, as a response to sticker shock**: *"**everybody agrees
  the value is there, but they look at the price and *we can't do this now*.** … **break up one big number
  into multiple payments across time**… if a client is working with a quarterly budget you can break it
  across a three-month payment cycle. **You're still going to get paid — you're just giving the clients more
  flexible payment terms.**"*

## ★★★ Verticalize before you widen

★★ **The two-designers thought experiment**: designer one spans bakery, CPG, technology and femtech and is
asked for relevant work — *"**they're going to scramble, and it's the ice dance — you know, when you stand
on ice you're slipping the entire time.**"* Designer two works only in men's health-care products.
★★★ **Why it wins, and it is not mainly about the work**: *"**when two strangers get together we're looking
for points of commonality**… when they see you've worked with someone they know — *oh my gosh, you worked
with that company, say hi to Bob for me* — **instantly rapport is built and you already have the inside
track.**"* ★★★ Plus the real asset: *"**you understand things about their industry that they might not even
yet know, because they're so deep inside the jar.**"*

★★ **Research discipline**, with a caution attached: talk to the client's team (*"boots on the ground"*) but
*"**cross-reference this against social media posts, because sometimes people are a little self-delusional
in terms of how great their product is** and how much their customers love them."* ★★ And on delivering
what you find: *"**it is important HOW you bring this to their attention, and not just throw it in their
face**, because it can come across as insulting and you being a know-it-all."*

★★ **On differentiation**: *"what makes them different — **and we want to celebrate the difference and not
hide from [it].**"* ★★ On why context speeds him up: *"**when I don't know enough about the problem I could
stare at a blank screen and be stuck for a really long time. Conversely, when I know the challenges, the
parameters and the guide rails, it's super quick for me to come up with ideas.**"*

## ★★ Two closing pro tips

1. ★★★ **The recap email**: *"follow up with an email to summarise the key points… **this seems so obvious
   and so simple, yet so few people do it that when you do it it's going to be like a breath of fresh
   air.**"* Selfish half: *"if you're talking to five, ten different clients a month **you're going to
   forget what's important.**"* ★★★ And the one line to add: *"**did I miss anything, or would you like to
   clarify anything?** — **that opens up the invitation.**"* ★★ With the underlying signal: *"**the fact
   that you took notes shows that you value their time** — and **the more time they invest in you, the more
   likely they are to proceed.**"*
2. ★★ **The capabilities deck** — for the *"let me review it with my team"* moment, instead of *"a Dropbox
   link with a bunch of loose files."* His own is **a master deck he curates down**: *"I go in and curate
   and select specific ones relevant to the client — **I'll probably pick three.**"* Contents: relevant
   work as **problem / solution / impact**, team bios, services, sometimes *"a price sheet… in terms of
   broad ranges"*, a handful of testimonials, and a **logo quilt**. Refresh cadence: *"**every three to six
   months.**"* ★★ And when to build it: *"**especially when it's slow and you have nothing to do, this is a
   great thing to work on.**"*
