## ★★★ Why corporate-brand behaviour kills a personal brand

**He builds it as an exercise, not an assertion.** First he has the room list what a corporate brand's
social account is *for*: **leads, awareness, conversions, engagement, recruitment, mission, PR** (product
launches, *"damage control"*). Then he tests each against a personal account — *"are you posting on your
personal account to recruit people? …to do PR?"* — *"**you shouldn't. You can if you want, but I advise you
not to.**"* What survives is **engagement**, and that is where the argument lands:

> ★★★ *"**Corporate brands do not get a lot of engagement.** Corporate brands are usually very generic
> content. It's like a team has a strategy, marketing department, there's copywriters, there's HR, there's
> legal — ★★★ **that's where everything goes to die in terms of creativity. Send it to a committee and all
> creativity dies.** It has **too many masters to make happy**, and then they **water it down until
> everyone's happy — which means no one's happy.** There's a quote: **a good compromise is when no one's
> happy.**"*

> ★★★ **The conclusion, and it is the title of the talk**: *"**the problem here is: if we start behaving
> like a corporate brand, we will get the engagement of corporate brands** — **which is, no one gives an F.
> No one cares.**"*

★★★ **The distinction in one line**: the corporate brand is *"**the voice of many** — because it's the
committee that decides"*; the personal brand is *"**the voice of one. It's your voice, your beliefs, your
mission, your attitude, your style.**"*

★★★ **And the asymmetry that makes it winnable**: *"**all the time corporate brands are trying to be more
human.** The good news is: ★★★ **you're already human. There's only one of you. So do you — it will
work.**"*

★★ **The follower evidence**, restated with examples: *"who typically has more followers, the corporate
brand or the CEO/founder? **By a wide margin** … Tesla and Elon Musk — not even close. **Apple and Tim
Cook, who's not even the founder — Tim Cook has way more followers than Apple.** Virgin versus Richard
Branson."* With the qualifier: *"**it seems to hold true if it's an interesting CEO.**"*
✅ **Reinforces the personal-brand-outperforms-company-brand framework already in the branding hub, with the
same three examples.**

★★ **The subscription-brand aside** that sets it up: *"**who follows Netflix on Instagram? …Apple TV+?
Disney+? Hulu?** … **nobody follows them, period.**"*

★★★ **The differentiation rule underneath it all**: *"**in creative culture we tend to emulate what other
people do** — somebody uses a colour, we all use the same colour; somebody uses a new trendy font, we all
use the same font. **What we should be doing is doing the opposite. That's how you stand out — and it's very
easy to stand out, because you just have to be more of yourself.**"*

## ★★★ Cultural currency — and the three stories

✅ **The corpus already has *origin story as cultural currency* as a branding framework; this is the fullest
teaching of it**, worked live off the conference's own keynote speakers.

*"Why do people tell stories about where they're born and where they're from? **It's called cultural
currency.** … **Where you're from is a shorthand for you to connect with people** — your language, your
customs, your culture, the food that you eat. **It says a lot about you.**"* Demonstrated with the room:
*"who's here from New York? — **then they all [go] *we're from New York, we know each other already.***"*

★★★ **The mechanism he names, using the earlier speaker's story**: a man from the South Bronx who *"didn't
know how to do art, who got in trouble and got arrested, and didn't want to disappoint his grandma."* Chris:
*"**it's going to stick with you long after today**… **we would have all cheered for him because we are
[him] and [he] is us.** We all want to achieve something, we all want to make somebody we care about happy,
**we don't want to disappoint those people.** There's a lot of connection happening **without you even
knowing what's happening.**"*

★★ **A craft note on delivery**: *"**if you add dialogue or different voices in telling the story** — which
he did, he started speaking a little bit in Spanish — and as soon as he did, what did the audience do?
**I didn't understand a word, but I knew that they were feeling something.**"*

> ★★★ **The line to keep**: *"**we all don't think our immigrant story or our origin story matters — until
> you tell it.**"*

★★★ **And the instruction for people who say they have nothing to post**: *"all of you sitting there like
*I don't know what to say* — **start there. Tell your origin story, your backstory: where you were born, how
many brothers and sisters, only child, firstborn, last born; your parents' story — the first one to go to
college, the first one to go to high school. That matters.**"* → *"**everybody who shares that idea or that
emotion — even if they don't share your exact story — is going to feel like they're with you, and you are
them. You represent them.**"*

★★★ **Why a company structurally cannot do this**: *"**corporate brands can't do this. They were not really
born — they were created.** They don't have that same cultural currency **because there is none.**"* The
nearest substitutes he names: *"Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs in a garage in Palo Alto — **the mythical
Silicon Valley garage startup that turns into a three-trillion-dollar company. That's as close as they
get**"* — plus **Colonel Sanders** and **Dave Thomas of Wendy's**.

### ★★★ The three stories of a personal brand (new to the corpus as a named set)

He corrects his own ordering mid-list, which is worth preserving as delivered:

1. ★★★ **The inciting incident** — *"there's the old world or the ordinary, and the new world. **Look in
   your timeline for when everything changes.**"* Via **Loki**: *"whatever the **sacred timeline** was — that
   was you. **Something happened in your life, it forked, you picked one new path, and that becomes a new
   sacred timeline. Your life is totally different. It's never the same again.**"* The test he gives:
   *"when did you discover you wanted to be a photographer? **The first time you figured it out — not *I
   think I might*, but when you KNOW**, so that the world is never the same again."*
2. ★★★ **The origin story** — the beginning, above.
3. ★★★ **The defining moment** — *"**when it totally crystallizes**… somewhere along the story it becomes
   real."* (**The inciting incident is a clue; the defining moment is the crystallisation.**)

## ★★★ Retainer ≠ subscription — and the Ron Baker credit, on the record

A wedding-stationery designer asks how to get recurring revenue from a **bespoke, once-in-a-lifetime**
service — *"oh, that's tricky, man."* Chris pivots to a designer in the room (**Alex**, of a studio doing
*"logos, packaging, websites"*) who says she solved it, and **interrogates her model live**: **$2,500/month
for 20 hours**, *"no rollover, they're dead"*, and if they run over, *"**I will just do it for them** —
you'll just eat it."*

> ★★★ **The reclassification, which is the whole point**: *"**Alex actually does not have a subscription
> model. She has a RETAINER model.** There's a difference — and **it doesn't seem like there's a big
> difference until I explain it to you.**"*

★★★ **THE CREDIT, in his own mouth**: *"**the only reason why I know this is because I asked Ron Baker**,
[who] **wrote the book *Implementing Value Pricing*.** He's **a wizard with economics and accounting**, and
he explained it to me. I'm like: **whoa, now I know the difference.**"*

- ★★★ **Retainer** — *"**you're selling a prepackaged set amount of hours that they buy in advance for a
  slight discount.** So **what you're really selling is time** — you're still selling time, but **it's
  prepackaged in bulk**, and whenever you buy in bulk it's cheaper. **You have some predictability in terms
  of revenue** — you don't have to worry, you can hire people."*
- ★★★ **Subscription** — *"**you don't sell time at all. You don't talk about time. You just talk about
  results.**"*
