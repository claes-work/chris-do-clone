## 1. ★★★ Dollarize the value — i.e. sell money

*"**You don't want to sell services. You don't even want to sell benefits. You want to sell money back to
the buyer.**"* ✅ Same doctrine as the whiteboard session ([[2025-03-31-yt-aQYbXVZb88k]]), here credited to
the book. *"For every $100 you spend, **here's what you're going to get in terms of real value versus soft
touchy-feely things.**"*

★★★ **The diagnosis of the creative failure mode**: *"the mistake we make is **we sell services, we sell
deliverables — how many logos, how many iterations** — when we should be figuring out **how does this
advance the business forward? Will it save them money? Will it make them money? Will it improve customer
experience?**"* → *"**once you're able to dollarize the value, the price won't seem as much given the
relative value created.**"* And the charge: *"**most creatives don't even speak this language**… they're
**too stuck on creating this premium feeling and focus almost always on aesthetic value — which to a
non-discerning buyer doesn't actually mean that much.**"*

★★ **The analogy, and it lands on his own father**: *"there are people in your family who **just don't care
what they wear**… a comedian jokes that **these are the types of people who like to buy their clothes where
they buy their groceries.** Trying to sell **the Costco customer** Gucci or Prada is a wasted cause. **So
instead of speaking to them in YOUR language, speak to them in THEIRS.** ★★ **For my dad it could just be
convenience, or durability, or the fact that it's made in the USA.**"*

## 2. ★★★ Open-ended questions — and the order-of-operations error

✅ *"**When you're saying it, you're selling. When they say it, you're closing.**"* (existing doctrine,
restated).

★★★ **The error he names precisely**: *"most sales presentations go — **the salesperson does a presentation,
a pitch, a capabilities deck, then talks about their company and their products, and THEN starts asking
questions.** ★★★ **This is exactly the wrong way**, because **in the mind of the buyer, all you've been
doing is talking about yourself the entire time.** Not once do they feel: *do you understand my problem?*
**They're not feeling seen, heard, nor understood.** … **So they start to tune out.**"*

> ★★★ **THE SOCRATIC OPENER — quoted from the book, and new to the corpus**:
> *"**Mr Jones, I prepared to talk to you about brand identity design, which we discussed on the phone. Now
> if you could give me your perspective on that, we can focus the meeting on what interests you.**"*

★★ **His own expansion of it**: *"**I prepared a full presentation for you today — but before I get into it,
I'd like to find out what's important to you.** What are you looking to accomplish? What challenges are you
facing? **Where are you today, and where would you like to be? What's the reason motivating this
conversation today?**"* ★★★ **And what the move signals**: *"**it shows that I'm prepared — I have lots of
things I can show you — but what's more important than me giving you a presentation is finding out what's
really important to you.**"*

★★ **Why the client credits you for their own talking**: *"**although they do most of the talking, they
assign most of that value to you** — because what you did was **create space for them to think and
articulate what was important to them.**"*

## 3. ★★★ Full-value listening — and the anti-eye-contact heresy

*"You're communicating explicitly and implicitly that **everything they say is important.**"*

> ★★★ **The heresy, stated as one**: *"I know this is a slap in the face of everyone who's ever thought
> this — **when talking to a prospect, it's LESS important that you make eye contact with them, and MORE
> important that you actually take notes and show them that you care.**"*

★★★ **The reason**: *"when someone is telling you all their problems and you're just **looking them in the
eye**, they're thinking: ★★★ **is any of this being retained? Do you remember all this detail?**"*
★★★ **And the power framing**: *"**every person in a position of power wants to feel powerful. And people
who are in charge — when they speak, people take notes.** **That's what you should do.**"*

★★★ **The domestic example, told against himself**: *"**my wife will often give me very complicated
instructions** on how she wants something done, and **I'm listening intently** — I'm doing the full-value
listening thing. **But because I'm not [writing], she starts to wonder: does he have all the steps?** And
then it creates tension. ★★★ **And she'll say: hey, are you listening to me? — I'm like: of course I am.**"*
⚠️ **His wife is referenced and not named.**

★★ **How to take them**: *"not a courtroom reporter recording every single thing — **write down key bullet
points, facts, colours, information, challenges and pain points.** Then **circle back and ask about things
that were unclear.**"* ★★ **And *suitcase words***: *"**words that have multiple meanings — you literally
have to unpack it**… **be careful of hearing those words, because it can send you down a different
direction.**"*

> ★★★ **The line he quotes from the book**: *"**A tape recorder remembers everything but understands
> nothing.**"* → *"listen, record — **but then PLAY BACK, showing that you understand what is being
> said.**"*

## 4. ★★★ All sales is about the past

> ★★★ *"We are so eager to get to the future — **we close a deal, we do the work, we get paid** — that we
> often **run right over the most important things.** ★★★ **Somewhere in the client's past, a problem
> existed that warranted this conversation today.** When that problem hits a boiling point, they take
> action — this is often called ★★★ **a compelling event.** **So before we can go to the future, we must go
> to the past.**"*

★★ **The question**: *"**what has happened in the past that's caused this conversation to happen today?
What warrants this conversation?**"* — and what it surfaces: *"**what's important, why they reached out to
you, what kind of stress and pressure they're under.**"*

★★★ **Then — put your finger on the pain**: *"**you want them to RELIVE the pain**… you ask lots of
questions so that **they can emotionally go through it with you.** ★★★ **And when they feel that pain, they
want to buy medicine to relieve it. It's called selling pain relief.**"* ✅ **Same term as the whiteboard
session.** With the stakes made explicit — lost revenue, *"professional embarrassment, key members being
fired"* — *"**and now you know what's at stake, and THEY know what's at stake, which is more important.** So
when you offer a price of $100,000, **it's relatively small given the pain they'd suffer.**"*

## 5. ★★★ The conditional (hypothetical) close

★★ **The epigraph he pulls**, credited to **W. B. Yeats**: *"**In dreams begin responsibility.**"*

★★★ **Why it works**: *"**if you ask the client to buy RIGHT NOW, they have to make a really hard yes-or-no
decision — and that creates a lot of stress.**"* So instead:

> ★★★ *"**If you saw a proposal that solved ABC problem, on XYZ timeline, for Z price — would you be willing
> to move forward?**"*

★★★ **The two principles inside it**, and the second is the subtle one:
1. *"**We're used to hypothetical questions — they're very safe, they don't create a lot of stress.**
   *If you won the lottery, what would you do? If you could not fail, what would you try?*"*
2. ★★★ *"**You're not putting yourself in there.** You're saying ***IF YOU SAW a proposal*** — **this is
   third party, neutral and objective** — **versus *if I sent you a proposal***, [where] **the client has to
   think: if I say yes to this, it means I'm agreeing to YOU.** ★★★ **We don't take the client's power away
   from them by taking away their decision-making ability. We must let them make all the choices.**"*

★★ **The negative form**: *"**would there be anything holding you back? Is there anything still bothering
you? Anything left unresolved?**"* ★★ **And the ladder out**: *"I'll send you a proposal by end of business
today — **and if there's nothing else getting in the way, do you foresee yourself being able to make a
decision in the next 24 hours?**"* → *"**they've made several small commitments that should allow them to
say yes at this point.**"*

> ★★★ *"If you've followed any sales guru in the last 10, 15 years… **it's really aggressive. It's about
> manipulation. It's trying to force your will on another person. ★★★ And it's no wonder a lot of us hate
> sales.** When you think of salespeople, what comes to mind? **An image of a snake**, somebody really in it
> for themselves, **maybe a used-car salesman.**"*

*"The reason this book is such a fresh perspective — **even though it was published in 1996** — is that
**it's a 180 on this whole thing.** It's built around **asking questions, understanding the client's
problems, showing them that they have the power, and giving them respect.**"* ★★★ *"**If you're an introvert
like myself and hate sales, this is your key.**"* ★ *"It's under 200 pages, so **you could probably read it
in less than a day.**"*
