# Why Being Obsessed is YOUR Secret Weapon! (Daily Dose: Vlog 003) — 2025-05-25

> **Attribution:** vlog format. Chris is on-camera throughout; the footage is from the
> **UPS Store Small Business Challenge** in Austin, where he is coaching contestants
> ("Season 3. It's my third time"). Contestants, staff, and a product demoer appear

## Summary

Three threads braided into a ten-minute vlog: **obsession as an asset** (with the Star
Wars continuity-hire story), **foresight as the thing people mistake for luck** — which
produces the fullest recorded telling of why he moved [[../entities/blind|Blind]] out of
commercials, ending in an explicit AI parallel — and a run of **presentation coaching**
delivered live to contestants, including three pitch rules and an unusually specific
passage on speaking in a second language.

## Key claims (2025-05-25)

### Obsession

- The frame he opens with: *"Why did I tell you yesterday about being slightly obsessed?
  **Most people think that's a bad thing.**"*
- His defence is by comparison, not assertion: *"The alternative is to remain
  superficially interested in things and never really understand much beyond that.
  **That's good if you're trying to play Jeopardy or Trivial Pursuit**, but for most
  things in life, I think the world rewards people who have deep, deep knowledge or deep
  expertise."*
- **The Star Wars continuity hire** (recounted from an article he read): a superfan built
  a database and timeline of every canon event and where it happened, met George Lucas,
  and *"ultimately he got a job as the continuity guy to make sure the canon was
  maintained. Isn't that incredible? Like you could just be a big Star Wars nerd and just
  **own that space**."*
- Framed as personally validated: *"I just really get into stuff, and I think in my life
  it's proven to work out really well."*

### "It's not by luck" — foresight, and the Blind pivot

The setup is someone else's observation, reported by Chris: a friend told him a story
about a photographer for whom everything went wrong, and when Chris asked what the point
was, *"he turned to me like, well, **you're kind of like that. Things seem to keep
working out for you.**"*

Chris's answer, told to his wife at home that night:

> *"**It's not by luck. It's because you see where things are going. You go there before
> you run out of options.**"*

Then the worked example — the clearest account in the corpus of the reasoning behind the
commercial-advertising exit (cf. [[../entities/blind|Blind]]):

1. **The observation:** *"I used to work in commercial advertising and I just saw — okay,
   when's the last time I saw a commercial? It's been a while."*
2. **The check:** *"I look at all the young people and I ask them, 'Who here watches TV?'
   **Nobody watches TV. They stream everything.**"*
3. **The inference:** *"the demand for commercials is moving away, and we're in the
   business of making commercials. And this is not good."*
4. **The attempt:** *"I would go into the office, call my managers together and say, hey,
   **we need to figure out another business because this business is not going to be
   there.**"*
5. **The refusal:** *"And all they could see was **the food in front of them, not the
   winter that was coming.** So like, 'No, you're crazy — it will die, but it's going to
   take very long.'"*
6. **The confirmation:** *"and then we move out of that, we get into content — and of
   course our friends are going out of business and our clients are going out of
   business. **That's when you know it's really bad.**"*

**The AI parallel, stated immediately after:**

> *"**AI is going to disrupt everything. It's already disrupting everything. And if
> you're not trying to disrupt yourself, you're going to find yourself up a creek without
> a paddle.**"*

> **Note for synthesis:** the winter/food image reuses the seasonal metaphor recorded on
> [[2025-03-31-yt-aQYbXVZb88k|2025-03-31]] (Jim Rohn's "winter always comes"), but to a
> different end — there it argues for *preparation*, here for *foresight and
> self-disruption*. Worth recording as one image doing two jobs, not two ideas.

### Presentation coaching (delivered live to contestants)

- **Rooted, not pacing:** *"If you're nervous on stage, there's going to be a tendency for
  you to pace. **I'd rather have you just be rooted like a tree and not move.**"*
  ✅ Second attestation of the **be-the-tree** image twelve days after
  [[2025-05-13-yt-Anr8YYbt2ws|2025-05-13]], where it was an *internal* survival technique
  at a party. Here it is *external* stage direction — the same image reused as craft
  advice.
- **Eye contact:** *"pick three points in the room. It doesn't matter who you look at,
  but try to hit those points throughout."*
- **Hands:** *"when you're rehearsing, I need you to figure out what you're doing with
  your hands. You need to use your hands to tell part of the story, because **it's not
  just speaking. It's a performance.**"*
- **Hit the reaction — his sharpest craft note here:** on a contestant who mentioned
  making 1,600 deliveries during the pandemic shutdown, *"I heard Jared go, 'Ooh, wah,
  that's crazy.' **But you just steamroll right over that** by going to the next point.
  Those are the ones where you need to **hit and hit hard**, say it with some passion, and
  then **pause for a second**, and then move on."*
- *"**You have to pause to let certain ideas land.**"*
- **On his own restraint with feedback:** *"I'm just always nervous to give people too
  many notes and then they just totally have a meltdown."* — notable against his
  reputation for bluntness; the calibration is audience-dependent, not absent.

### The three pitch rules (stated as a numbered list)

1. **They don't know anything about you** — *"so you need to be super clear in your
   storytelling. Work on your writing or phraseology — those short little bite-size
   phrases that you can say that somebody's like, 'Oh, that's a bar.' It needs to be
   memorable. You need to have a repeatable story."*
2. **Say less** — *"avoid the temptation of cramming in too much information. It gets the
   opposite effect: **instead of them remembering you, they can't remember anything.** So
   say less so that you can have time to breathe and to feel the emotions of the words
   themselves, because that's what people ultimately connect on — the emotions and not
   necessarily the facts."* With the maxim: *"**stories sell, facts tell.**"*
3. **Choose words you can feel** — the second-language passage, below.

### Speaking in a second language

> *"When English is your second language, you have to overcome so many different things:
> the translation of it in your mind, understanding and feeling the words that you say. So
> when you're choosing words, **choose words that you can feel** and that you know and
> understand, and stay away from those complicated words."*

- **Complexity is relative, and the speaker's judgment governs:** *"a complicated word is
  contextual, because the person who's saying it might feel it as complicated where the
  audience might not. **If those are one of your words, just don't say it.**"*
- **The worked example:** a contestant said he had trouble saying *heritage*. Chris:
  *"That's not a complicated word for me"* — and then substituted anyway: *"use the word
  that you know — **family, tradition, culture, values** — whichever one works better for
  you, use that word instead."*
- **Why it matters:** *"those words become stumbling blocks. They're going to cause you to
  stutter and to overthink that moment. And then you're going to say it, **but you're not
  going to feel anything.**"*
