## Key claims (2025-06-15)

### ★★★★ The culture went sideways — a self-critical company account

> *"**The last two years I kind of took my eye off managing the company** and we had my **chief
> operating officer** hire and fire people. **Somehow the culture got sideways and it was really
> painful for me.** I'm like — **why are these people here? They don't even want to do what we want to
> do.** And it felt like **everything was like pulling teeth.**"*

The correction: *"with the audience and the community that we have, **we should only hire from the
community. So they believe in it** — and by the way, they get paid too, versus they're just showing
up."* The result: *"we've gone through the process of **a little bloodletting**, and now we have **a
really tight core team.** I think they're all bought in and we do incredible work together."*

The doctrine he draws: *"**the culture that you create will be the criteria for which people will
choose to work with you** and help you build on your mission… so **it's more important for you to
communicate what that culture or vision is**, so that people say *I want to be a part of that* — and
you're going to attract much higher quality people."* Credited in passing to **Simon Sinek**: *"not
everybody has a vision, not everybody needs one — but what we want to do is support a vision that is
in alignment with who we are as people."*

> ⚠️ **Self-reported**, and it names a role — **chief operating officer** — not a person. **No name is
> recorded.** ✅ **Reads as the backstory to the 2025-06-03 spin-off**: same period, same direction
> (smaller, aligned, content-led). ⚠️ But note the roles differ (**COO** here, **CEO** there); they are
> **not** assumed to be the same person.

**The worked example of hiring from the community** — his travel collaborator: *"he started out as a
guy who went to an event, then he went to another event, and eventually we travel the world together.
He's like, 'Where are you going to be? I'll meet you there.' And then, '**How can I help you? How can
I serve you?**' Now he's one of the contractors I work with to make content."* (Singapore, Toronto,
Miami, New York; Japan and Australia planned.)

### ★★★ The three-phase delegation framework (the "shadow" method)

> *"No matter what level you're at, if you're needing help with creative, production, management,
> strategy — the best thing I can tell you to do is **just have people shadow you.** … **Shadow me.
> Don't say anything. Just take notes and we'll talk at the end of the day. And depending on the
> quality of the questions and the notes you have, I know there's a potential for you here or not.**"*

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1 — Observe** | *"Don't say anything. Just watch, observe, take lots of notes, ask questions, document everything."* |
| **2 — Supervised** | *"Heavy supervision, but I'm giving you some control, and I'm checking out how you're navigating this."* |
| **3 — Autonomous** | *"Work on your own, be self-directed, and I'm going to periodically check in — and **the period between each check-in is going to get increasingly longer until I don't even check in any more.**"* |

**The compounding payoff:** *"the person who is the first hire for that role **has a playbook that
then they train their replacement.** So now you're not doing this over and over again."*

**The sober warning attached to phase one** — and it is the sharpest part:

> *"You'll know really early on in phase one, and you have to make a really hard decision. **As
> creative people, we're very optimistic and we see too much potential — when the potential is: that
> was it. They hit their ceiling**, and you were thinking there's more to this, and **there's no
> more.** So **you have to be sober.** You have to say: you know what, I don't know this is right for
> you. **And you're not a bad person.** It's just this isn't going to work. I can already see that."*

✅ He later confirms he used exactly this method to hand off new-business calls: *"I train my creative
directors — **it's the shadow technique.** They sit there, they listen, they follow… they do the
calls. **I don't even need to be in the room any more.**"*

### ★★★★ "We are historically the worst judge of our own value"

> *"**We are historically the worst judge of our own value. So you are just the worst person to ask —
> is this valuable to anybody? And you will not know until you put this out into the world and say
> whatever happens happens.**"*

**His own evidence:** *"if you ask me in **2014**, when **Jose and I** are making videos together, I'm
like — **no one's going to tune into this.** We're going to teach a very niche audience, designers, in
a very specific industry. **Five people are going to see this.**"* And now: *"there are people who
approach me on the street who are literally **hedge fund managers, real estate professionals.** I'm
like — **how did we get here?**"*

**The tile-guy story**, told against his own fear: driving in Santa Monica with his son, a white truck
pulls up aggressively. *"I pull my arm across my son saying — hey, I don't know what's going to
happen, but I can see it happening."* He braces (*"son, **I'm going to take a bullet for you** quite
literally right now"*). The driver rolls down the window: *"**I love your channel.**"* Chris:
*"What do you do? Are you a designer?"* — *"**No, I do tile work.**"*

### ★★★★ The message in a bottle (he chokes up telling it)

> *"The story I tell people — **trying to get choked up here** — is you put this message in this
> bottle… **all your wisdom, your heart, your soul, everything you've learned in this life, maybe for
> your future children** — and you throw it into the ocean. **The thing is, now you have no control
> where that goes.** It could go right back, no one sees it. Or it can go across the ocean… and
> somebody picks it up and reads it and they have this emotional connection with you — **and you still
> don't know.** Years later they put two and two together. **You run into each other in the street
> somewhere in Paris** and they're like, 'Hey, **I was in a really dark place in my life** and I think
> you're that person who sent me this, and it was meant to be.' **And that's the coolest thing
> ever.**"*

With the part that gives it weight: *"**I don't even make content to do that. Most of it is around
business.** And for some reason somebody sees something that's **beyond the intent.** And I think
that's **one of the coolest consequences of creating content.**"*

**The practice he draws from it:** *"**treat it as a form of public journaling — do not worry about
the results. Put in your reps.** And every day just ask yourself one basic question: **what did I do
well? What can I do better?** Acknowledge what was good and allow yourself room to keep evolving."*

### ★★★ Personal brand vs. company brand — and why committees can't have one

- *"There are **two words in personal brand**. **If you take the person out, then you're back to being
  a brand** — and that's corporations trying to be like people."*
- ★★★★ The line that reframes the reluctance: *"**the friction that you feel about putting yourself
  out there is the thing that prevents other people from doing the same. So it's just a question of
  who's going to blink first.**"*
- **Why a group can't hold a personal brand:** *"it's **groupthink**. If we were to say, 'what do we
  all think?', it would take us a long time to say something we all agree on — and **it becomes almost
  nothing.** **That's why good ideas die in committees.**"*
- **The Blind evidence:** *"when I used to write for Blind as the voice, there were **four directors,
  including myself**… from the oldest to the youngest person is **25 years difference**. We don't even
  use the same language. Their sensitivity to issues is way different than mine… **You're trying to
  write for the group, and no one's going to connect with that.**"*
- **The warning about deputising an employee as the face:** *"You could be, if you want to be — **but
  the value will be with her then.** You do understand that? You're like: **let me spend all my time
  and money and make you the valuable one.**"*

### ★★★ The new-paradigm income argument, with real numbers

> *"I used to work in commercial production. A mid-size project for us, about **$400,000**. It's
> expensive to produce, so **we might net 30%** — we spend a lot in production, post, **lost pitches,
> downtime, overhead.** So 30% of 400 is **$120K**. … **I can get a sponsor deal — and I've done this
> — where I work for like a day and a half. I'm talking about 12 hours total from beginning to end,
> and they're going to pay me $120K.** Maybe it's not something I love, but **I love that clean
> profit.** And **I literally do everything on my phone.**"*

Generalised: *"**you'll make more money from commanding an audience — a loyal and niche audience —
than you will from actual service work. And you get to do what you want. That's the best part.**"*
With the lawn-mowing creators as the proof case (free mow → dramatic before/after → hundreds of
thousands of views → the equipment and the business follow).

**And the spec-work solution for creatives trapped in approvals:** *"**You just do it for you.** 'Hey,
I was walking by, I looked at this bank, they have some ugly-ass design and their voice is
inconsistent. **I want to redo it. In this next three-part series, I'll show you how I'm going to do
it.**' So **you get to design exactly what you want, for no one except for you** — and **that's the
video that makes you money**, and you get to exercise those creative muscles."*

With the trade stated plainly: *"**if you put the caveat 'I need to do it for a client,' then you
accept the terms — which is: the clients will tell you what to do.**"*

### ★★★ Selling bold creative work — acclimation, and the commitment trap

- **Mild / medium / spicy** — three options, *"slowly ramping them into this."*
- ★★★★ **The commitment trap, stated openly:** *"We first get them to **fall in love with the big
  idea**, and we get them to **commit to the bold statement they're about to make** — because what
  they don't realise is **I'm going to use that against them in a little bit.** If you say you're
  recklessly, wildly crazy, then **we cannot look at any boring concepts** — because **you can't say
  one thing out of your mouth and point with a different finger.** … **Are you committed to this
  before I do?**"*
- **Acclimation is the whole method:** loose mood boards and sketchy ideas *"because **I want them to
  acclimate to the new aesthetic. It takes time.**"* Three analogies, all his:
  - **Fashion** — *"there might be things you don't like today, but given repeated exposure you start
    to warm up to it, and next thing you know you're the champion for that style."*
  - **Interior designers** — mood board, swatches, sketches, everything movable — *"because if you
    were just to show up one day and your home were totally different, **you would probably freak
    out. Even though objectively speaking it's superior.**"*
  - **Sushi** — *"imagine giving them not a California roll but **sashimi, tuna, blood red.** Welcome
    to your first plate of sushi ever."* → *"**instead of thinking of it as a one-course meal, think
    of it like a six-course meal.** Everything's going to prepare your palate."*
- **Paula Scher's logo book** — *"she would hand a giant book of the world's best logos to her new
  clients and say, '**Pick three. I'll be back later.**' You can't go wrong, because they're the
  world's best logos — and **it's a taste test.** No matter what you pick, we'll have a discussion.
  It's almost like **reading tea leaves.**"*
- ⚠️ **The caveat on the decoy**: some clients demand the spicy immediately, and *"every time we do
  something that we think there's no way they're going to buy this — **that's the one they want.**"*
  Hence: *"**you've got to be okay with your lure**, because there's a good chance they'll pick that
  one too. **So make sure that's good.**"*

### ★★★★ Taste is the new leverage — the most optimistic face of his AI position

> *"No matter what happens on the technology and design side, **good solid writing never changes.**
> … Conversational storytelling, **creating desire for the outcome, building tension and releasing
> tension, creating unexpected moments, and threading ideas together** — that is **a timeless skill**
> that's been around since we learned how to form words. **The translation of those words is where the
> technology comes in.**"*

> *"I was having a conversation with a futurist earlier today and I said — **it's the return of people
> who have taste, of people who understand history, and of people who have good language skills.
> These people pretty much were broke five years ago, but now they're going to be the new power
> brokers and the shapers of tomorrow. Because how do we tell the machine what we want if we don't
> know what's come before?**"*

With: *"**now is the time for us to be editors and taste makers** — because that's what we have that
the machines don't… **taste is going to be the new thing. And not everybody can have taste — just look
at all the rich people. You can buy that; you can't have style.**"* And the fidelity argument: the
nuance with which you can describe a **Rembrandt** determines the fidelity of what you can generate.

### ★★★ AI agents — and a trust admission

> *"The idea of an assistant or a second-in-command that does these things **will not be as necessary
> as it is today.**"*

**Daniel Priestley's travel agent**, recounted: it knows your airlines, aisle-or-window, hotel rates,
floors, near-or-far from the elevator; you give it parameters, *"it connects all your payment portals
and it books it for you"*, then emails for approval.

**The self-disclosure attached to it** — unusually candid:

> *"**I've never really had an assistant before**, mostly because — I'm going to read my own messages,
> I don't need you in there. But then it's also like, **I know it's a limiting belief, and there's
> some trust issues probably. But now I have a robot. The robot's not going to read what it's not
> supposed to. It's not going to disclose what it's not supposed to.** And so I really am looking
> forward to that."*

Plus: *"you can now **code your own basic app** to do a lot of things you use other apps for, **in
minutes.**"*

### ★★★★ Todd McFarlane — the full source of the "bilingual" self-concept

Asked what clicked for him as a designer-turned-business-person, he answers with a *Complex* interview
with **Todd McFarlane**, and this is the fullest telling in the corpus:

> *"He's asked, 'What's your secret to success?' And he goes: '**Most artists speak the language of
> creativity. I speak the language of business and creativity. I'm bilingual.** So I can walk into
> those business meetings and talk about the most creative ideas, but **it was always rooted in a
> sense of what the business person was concerned about.**'"*

With the supporting résumé Chris recites: the ornate Spider-Man webs, self-taught, **Todd Toys →
McFarlane Toys**, licensing *Halo* and other major IP, **Spawn** (film, one of the longest-running
Image books, an HBO show). And the anecdote he keeps: McFarlane paying **$1M for Mark McGwire's
home-run baseball** — *"was it worth it? **It was well worth it, because of the amount of press I got
just from being the stupid guy who bought the ball for a million dollars. It was worth it, 'cause I
get to talk about my companies now.**"* → Chris: *"**That's a shrewd business person.**"*

✅ **This is the source story behind the Daywalker / bilingual / emotion-through-the-cognitive-lens
self-concept** that pass 17 merged into one concept. Cite this telling.

**His own answer to the question:** *"I wasn't sure which came first, the entrepreneur or the
designer — but **throughout growing up I've always wanted to create a business, because money was
scarce in our house, being immigrants.** I was just trying to do something until I found design."*

### ★★★ The Blind bidding years, and what Kier actually taught him

**The problem:** *"the first couple of years of starting Blind were hard. We were being bid against
really big companies. We're tiny, no-nothing companies. **We don't even know how to do a conference
call. And I got really tired of losing.** At a certain level I can win the project — but at the next
level **we're getting slaughtered. Their ideas were better. Their phone game was better** — **they
give good phone**, as they would say."* A fortuitous encounter with some producers taught him to bid;
then he hired someone who *"says: here's all the things you don't know about."*

**Kier McLaren's first lesson — "say what you think":**

> *"Number one is **to say what you think.** It's a really weird thing to say — like, that's
> important? … **What I learned many years, maybe decades later, is that we're all ready to do
> something, but we need someone to give us permission to do that thing.**"*

**The failure mode he names in himself:** *"Agency's like, 'let's walk you through the script.' And
you're like, 'oh fudge, okay — I know at a certain time I have to say something,' because they're
going to ask what ideas you have. **So the whole time I'm not listening to them. I'm just getting
ready to say whatever it is I'm going to say — which is a complete disconnect from what they just
said, because I'm not even present.**"*

**Kier's fix, verbatim:** *"**Why don't you ask them questions about what they just said? Ask them
what inspired this. What would they like to have happen? Who else is bidding on this project? What
kind of money do you have?** And whatever other questions you can think of."* Then: *"the first time I
did it, **I hit a bunch of dead ends.** So I went back to him — 'here are the dead ends.' 'Oh yeah,
just say this instead.' **That's all the training I got from him on how to do sales.**"*

**On why the relationship worked** — and it is not about the coach: *"he was a special guy, because
I've never experienced that before — **to have someone in your corner who understands business, who
can hear your BS and call you out on it, and also support you.** … **It's less important the person.
It's more important that you trust the person to do what they tell you to do. That's the critical
part.** … **If you can listen to your child and your child has good ideas, that will work out too.
We need to give someone permission to then give us permission.**"*
✅ **"He was my business coach for 13 years"** — consistent with the 2020 telling.

### ★★★ Money-talk, and drawing out rather than putting in

- *"One is **to be comfortable talking about money.** Until you learn how to talk about it, it's really
  awkward… And when you get comfortable with it, **the other person recognises they're talking to an
  entrepreneur, not a creative.** … Otherwise they're thinking: '**they're creative types, they're
  dreamers, they're living in fantasy land — but here in reality we need to get business done.**'"*
- ★★★★ **The core discovery insight:** *"one of the biggest lessons I learned from Kier is **the other
  person really just wants to hear their idea back at them, refined** — and test it a little bit. And
  then **they give you all the credit in the world.** I can't tell you how many times I've done these
  new-business calls where they're like: '**That was amazing. You made us think. My brain hurts. Thank
  you for all the ideas.** I don't remember giving you a single idea…'"* — with the example that stuck:
  a client who said **"epic but intimate"** and needed resolution around it.
- **The operating assumption:** *"go into that conversation **assuming the other person does not have
  the time or the language skills to tell you what they want**, and **assume that they're not telling
  you the truth — not because they're bad people, because they just don't know.** Use your language
  skills, your expertise, your curiosity to bring it out of them."*
- ★★★★ **The etymology he leans on:** *"the word **educate** doesn't mean *to put in*. The root word is
  **deduce**, which means **to draw out.** So when we say we're going to educate the client, **we're
  not telling them things. We're drawing out for them the truth.**"* → *"**You want to help people
  think, 'cause thinking is difficult.**"*
  > ⚠️ **Imprecise, and he hedges it himself** (*"I forget where I saw this from"*). The
  > draw-out sense traces to Latin **_educere_**, not to *deduce*. **The insight stands; the
  > derivation as stated does not.** Record both.
- **The close:** *"that one needle in the haystack… they're like '**oh my god, it's been there the
  whole time**' — and you're like, '**yeah, you guys told me it was here. I just picked it up. You look
  like a freaking genius.**' And for that, **they will pay you.** You will have **the inside track and
  a competitive advantage that's legal and unbeatable.**"*

### ★★ Around the talk

- **Pre-talk acclimation — the house-plant metaphor:** *"**I feel like I'm like a house plant. You
  don't want to move it inside or outside too quickly.** And so **I need to acclimate and make myself
  feel comfortable — otherwise, when I'm speaking, I'm going to have the nerves.**"* Same instinct as
  the acclimation method he sells clients with. Prep habits: rehearsing his own deck *"so I don't feel
  like I'm surprising myself on stage,"* spell-check, and a second pair of eyes.
  ⚠️ He also notes he was **up until 5:30 a.m.** working on the deck, and wonders whether the absence
  of nerves is *"the sleep deprivation."*
- **On Simon Squibb**, after the May interview: *"I think I'm pretty committed to the content game.
  And then you meet other people who are **way more committed and way more successful**, and you see —
  **wow, there are levels to this.**"* He describes the six-person travelling team and the model:
  *"he's built an **infinite business model** — the money he spends to make content is less than the
  money he gets from advertising and sponsor deals. **So he can do this forever.**"* And his own
  version of the ambition: *"**I need five more** [of my collaborator] — so I'm **slowly building this
  organically with a very small budget** relative to the kind of money he's working with."*
- **Post-talk debrief:** *"sometimes people are like **the silent giants — they move, but you don't
  hear them move.** And I think I'll hear later. **That's always the true test** — when they're on
  social and ready to share or say something, I'll know how they truly feel."* Next stop **Singapore**,
  where *"we're going to take the ideas tested in this talk format and **turn it into a workshop where
  I can see people do things.**"* ✅ And he asks his collaborator for **what worked, what didn't, what
  can be improved** — *wisdom = experience + reflection*, practised.
- **A closing reframe:** *"**it's your interpretation of what happens that shapes your reality. You get
  to choose.** So maybe you're too spontaneous, too impulsive — **figure out where that's really
  good.**"*
