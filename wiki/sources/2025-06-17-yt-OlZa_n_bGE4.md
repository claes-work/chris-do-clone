# The Secret To Telling Your Story So People Listen! (2025-06-17)

> **Attribution:** two-hander with **Mo**, his regular on-camera foil. Mo supplies the **raw material**
> — a real medical scare — and is the subject of the workshop; **the teaching is entirely Chris's.**
> Mo's personal details (three children, Egyptian family culture, the ER visit) are **his** and are
> recorded only as workshop material, never as persona data.

## Summary

★★★ A **live storytelling masterclass** with a real story rebuilt on camera. Chris gives the
three-part structure, the reason films open on the explosion, and then does the thing that makes the
episode valuable: he takes Mo's flat, factual account of a medical scare, **interrogates it for
sensory detail, setting, dialogue and stakes**, and performs the rebuilt version out loud so the
difference is audible. Ends with two rules that sit in productive tension with each other — **selective
editing** and **the teenager rule** — both of which he states against himself.

## Key claims (2025-06-17)

### ★★★ Why storytelling is the original technology

> *"If we go back to the origin of humankind, **before we developed writing, oration is the most
> important skill** — and the people who told the best stories were very important in their villages
> and their tribe, because **they were the keepers of history. They're the ones who explain the world
> to everyone.** If there was a fruit or plant you're not supposed to eat, there's probably a pretty
> good story about it… so we can pass down this knowledge from generation to generation. They might
> have been considered **the shaman, the wise person, the medicine doctor.**"*

With the line he borrows: *"**stories are the operating system of the human mind.**"*
⚠️ Attributed to a name the captions render **"Eric me"** — unresolved; recorded as spoken, **not
asserted**.

### ★★★ The three-part structure — credited to Kindra Hall

> *"**Kindra Hall**, in her book ***Stories That Stick***, distils it down to something really super
> simple. There's **the normal** — the way things are. There's **an explosion.** And now there's **a
> new normal.** Just focus on those three parts: **how are things now? What was the explosion? And
> what happens afterwards?**"*

### ★★★★ But start with the explosion

> *"Here's the interesting thing. If you're watching a popular film or a TV show, **what do they
> always begin with? The explosion.**"*

**The James Bond illustration:** he skis down a mountain being shot at, escapes on gadgets and
athleticism, *"and then you hear the ding-ding-ding-ding — and now you know I'm in a James Bond
film."* Then the title sequence, **then** the actual beginning. *"**That's why you say cut to the car
chase. Cut to the chase.**"*

**Why it works:** *"the explosion hooks — **we're disoriented. Like, what's going on?** And then what
do they do? Usually there's a flashback: four months ago, three weeks ago, three hours ago. **They
understand that they have this precious first few minutes to hook you in, or you're going to be
gone.** We know that **action works — and action translates across language barriers.**"*

### ★★★★ The live workshop — the whole method, demonstrated

Mo tells the story flat: abdominal pain, urgent care, acid-reflux medicine, no relief, ER, CT scan, no
hernia, better medicine, fine now.

**Chris's diagnosis:**

> *"**You did it perfectly for me** — because **you kind of told us the facts. I didn't get any
> sensory detail. I didn't get any dialogue. You didn't pull me into your world** — because **a story
> is supposed to allow me to see the world through your eyes.** … You almost did it **as a biographer
> might write your story, from the outside**: Mo went through this, he had pain, he went here, did
> X-rays, turned out to be nothing."*

**The rule he states from it:** *"when you tell a story, **you need to put me into your shoes, allow me
to see the world through your eyes**, and you need to **include sensory details** and hopefully **make
it rich with dialogue. And dialogue allows us to experience it without you telling me about it.
You're showing me.**"*

**Then he extracts the missing pieces by interrogation**, which is the actually teachable part:

| What he asks for | The question | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory detail** | *"Take me back there and describe the sensation."* | *"like a balloon blown up just to the edge of popping — and inside that balloon there was a stone, a weight, a grape, pushing against the rubber"* |
| **Setting** | *"What day was this? … What time?"* | *"Saturday… 8 a.m."* |
| **Stakes** | *"Let's say it was really serious and your worst fears had come true. **What's at stake if this is actually the end of you?**"* | *"I'm the provider for my three children… that would be a tremendous cost on the family."* |

> ★★ **An aside about his own interviewing** — *"this is why I like dancing with you: **you do what
> you're supposed to do. You don't try to outsmart me. You don't try to get all weird and edit
> things**, and you don't try to have all the answers ready to go — [otherwise] **I got nothing to
> work with.**"*

**Then he performs the rebuild live**, opening on the explosion and threading in everything he just
extracted: *"**It's Saturday morning. I wake up with an immense pain in my belly** and I don't know
what's going on, but I'm reaching down and **I feel this lump in my abdomen** — and it feels like from
the inside out somebody's been blowing up this balloon and it's about to burst. **And I immediately
start to think about my children**…"* — followed by the cultural detail, the phone call to the
brother as dialogue, the supplements as levity, the ER wait (*"there's just perspiration on my face. I
feel like I need to explode, but I can't"*), and the release: *"**there's this calming feeling that
washes over my body — that whatever it is, I'll get through this, and it's okay.**"*

Mo's own verdict on the difference: *"even though I'm the one who went through it, **I could feel
myself as if I was there with you. Almost like a shadow** — I was in the ER room, I was laying next to
you in the bed."*

### ★★★ The establishing shot

> *"Nine out of ten times they start with a **very specific setting**, so it orients us. **It's called
> an establishing shot in movies — and you need to do your establishing shot in your story form.** …
> It doesn't need to be more than that."*

The alternative example he improvises: *"**I'm 18. It's summer and it's hot and sticky in New York
City. Hot like hotter than hot — like 103 degrees, but with humidity it's like 155 degrees.**"*

### ★★★ The me-too mechanic — and making the story not about you

> *"Then I reveal essential details that **build up tension and paint in some of the gaps** — because
> it's like, why is a stomach pain a big deal? — and then **you bring in a little levity**: maybe I
> shouldn't have taken all those supplements. And then hopefully the audience is like, 'yeah, I've
> done something stupid like that,' **so they see themselves in your story.** … **Slowly you're
> getting the audience to raise their hand to say: yeah, that's me. Come on. Me too.** And **you
> become super relatable.**"*

> *"So **you make the story not about you. You make it about why this matters to you.** But **not all
> stories have to have this big revelation, this life lesson.** It could just be **a tender moment
> that we can all say: this is part of the human experience.**"*

### ★★★★ Selective editing — stated against himself

> *"The other part to being a good storyteller is **selective editing.** And there's an expression
> that I love so much: **don't let facts get in the way of a good story.** Because I'll tell stories
> from the stage and **my kids [say], 'that's not exactly how it happened.'** Like — **yeah, but that
> story is boring and no one cares about that story. And you know what, boy? I got the mic. I'm on
> stage. I'm going to tell a story the way I want to tell it.**"*

> ⚠️ **Record this with its tension intact.** It is his stated *performance* ethic, told against
> himself and with his children as the fact-checkers — and it sits **against** his documented insistence
> elsewhere on citation, dating, and never fabricating. Both are his. The distinction he appears to be
> drawing is **stage storytelling vs. claims of fact**, but he does not state that boundary explicitly
> here, and it should not be supplied for him.

### ★★★★ The teenager rule

> *"Pretend you're **a teenager with raging hormones.** They tend to be **hyperbolic about
> everything.** It's the worst day of my life. It's the best day of my life. I'm a total disaster. She
> loves me, she hates me. He's going to kill me, I'm dead. **And the reason they do that is because
> they're in a heightened emotional state** — so everything rides on that emotional tightrope. And if
> you look at some of the best animated films, **they understand how to exaggerate certain parts. It's
> the most of the most of whatever it is we're going to think and feel.**"*

**The taco test:** *"**this is the best taco you're ever going to eat in your life. And if I had one
taco to eat before I get buried, this is the one**"* — versus *"yeah, it was a day. That food was all
right. It was an interesting experience."* → *"**Is it literally the best taco? Probably not. Would
you want to eat this before you die? Probably not. But if you tell the story that way**…"*

**And the example he clearly likes best:** *"My dad's a big, strong man. He doesn't speak a lot, but
he does certain things. **And when he held me in his arms, I felt like a forklift had lifted me up. I
felt safe, like this was bulletproof armour and nothing could stop us.** **Is that real? No. But it's
a much better story.**"*

### ★★ He pre-empts the comments

> *"Some people are going to listen to this like, 'no, I prefer Mo's first story better.' **They always
> do that to me. They always do it.** So let me have it in the comments. That's fine. **Mo does not
> prefer his first story** compared to the one that was made afterwards, by the way."*
