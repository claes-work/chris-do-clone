# You've been creating content ALL Wrong (Here's how to fix it) — 2025-08-05

> **Attribution:** a **live workshop session** with a small room. Participants answer prompts and are
> marked; every teaching turn is Chris's. Originally a members-only recording — he says so on camera.

## Summary

★★★ Short and dense. It opens with **an epistemic disclaimer that is one of the most valuable things
in the corpus** — *"I have survivor bias… **all strategy is autobiographical**"* — then teaches a
complete, named method: **chunking / the rule of five**, demonstrated live on design and then run on a
participant's own domain, and finally the thing that makes niche expertise travel — **wrap it in a
bubble of broad appeal**, illustrated with a Neil deGrasse Tyson story told end to end.

## ★★★★ The preface — survivor bias, stated before anything else

> *"**I have survivor bias, and you need to know that.** Okay? Because **all strategy is
> autobiographical.** If I share a strategy with you, it's because **it's what has worked for me.**
> Now, **the real problem is if I tell you what works for me and it's not you — that's the real
> problem.** But if you want to have what I have and do what I've done, then just do what I tell you
> to do. **That's the preface.** … **I can't tell you it's going to work for all of you, because many
> of you don't want what it is that I have.**"*

This is a **conditional warranty on his own advice**, given unprompted and up front. It should travel
with any teaching material promoted from this era.

## ★★★ The premise

> *"There's a very simple formula… I think I wrote it: **if you want to reach more people, you have to
> teach more people.**"*

With a distinction he draws deliberately: *"the teachers in the room — **like the real teachers, not
the internet teachers** — you have a distinct advantage, because **you've got thousands of hours
teaching and figuring out how to work with people in real time.**"*

## ★★★★ Chunking, and the rule of five

A participant offers *"go short and sharp"* — and Chris keeps it partly because of the form: *"**he
did go short and sharp. The man eats his own medicine here.**"* Then: *"**unpack that for us.** That's
a big idea. Now give us one or two sentences."* (The unpacking: *"take something very complicated and
make it very applicable."*)

**His name for it, and the rule:**

> *"I'm going to give you a different word. It's called **chunking**… **you just take a big pizza and
> you break it into smaller pieces.** For me, I call it **the rule of five**: **whatever you want to
> teach, break it down into five things. Five steps. It's always five. It's never seven, it's not
> three — always five.**"*

**Worked live on design** — *"I'm just kind of doing this freestyle"*:

| Level | The five |
|---|---|
| **Design** | contrast · repetition · alignment · details · the X factor |
| **Contrast** (level 2) | size · weight (thin/thick) · texture · shape · colour (he adds **orientation** mid-list) |

Two rules attached:

- **Prioritise, and mean it:** *"**the first thing is the most important thing. If you don't have
  contrast in your design, it sucks.**"*
- **Recurse until it's obvious, then stop:** *"but then **what the hell is contrast? Then you do five
  of that.** … **If we feel like this needs no additional explanation, then we've come to the
  terminus** — we made it as easy as possible… **You may have to go three layers in, but it's usually
  not more than three, because at that point we're kind of done.**"*

**And the payoff, which is the reason to do any of it:**

> *"Here's the beautiful part. **How many ways can you show size in contrast? How many ways? That's
> infinite content.** … I can show you different weight examples **from history, from the 70s, from
> the 80s, from this designer, from that designer, in architecture, in furniture design.** … **Then
> you just give example after example after example. Your audience will grow.**"*

### ★★★ The live architecture demonstration

He teaches *contrast in size and orientation* by pointing out of the window:

> *"Architects know this. They build a house that's square, and then a roof that's also square —
> **that's boring as hell.** So all they do is **they take this and they shift it over.** … They might
> have a window here and a window here — **really freaking boring.** So you know what they do? **They
> take this window and put it here, so it wraps around the corner.** And now when we talk about
> alignment — we might have a small window here **because it aligns with that**, and we'll probably
> put a chimney here, and that **aligns with this edge.** … **You'll see this now, once you know this
> concept. You'll see that they do this all the time.**"*

### ★★ Then he runs the exercise on a participant

Asked for a business example, he refuses to supply one: *"**why don't you write your list right now in
front of you?** … **All teachers should be able to do this.**"* Then turns to a health expert:

> *"What are the five things I need to do to live longer?"* → **nutrition · sleep · hydration · gut
> health · blood work.** Chris tests the ordering: *"if you get your nutrition wrong, you wouldn't have
> to get the blood work, because you're going to be bad. So once you do the four, **blood work will be
> optimisation** — so that we have data to see if we're missing things."*
>
> Then level two: *"break down nutrition for us. Five."* → *"balancing macros"* — and he catches the
> jargon instantly: *"**oh, that's a complicated word.**" ✅ Consistent with the *choose words the
> audience can feel* rule from [[2025-05-25-yt-V5c1YGTzAYc|2025-05-25]]. The list resolves to
> **macros · timing · balance · quantity · quality.**

## ★★★★ The bubble of broad appeal — why niche content fails

> *"Here's where you guys get it wrong. **You make niche content.** … When you break it down into
> little bite-sized pieces, **you still earn the niche content — and that's good, that's what your
> expertise is. But what you have to do is you have to wrap it in a bubble of something that has broad
> appeal. You have to meet the audience where they're at.** And the way broad appeal works is **you
> need to find something everybody's already talking about and understands — and then you build the
> bridge to your niche thing. That's how you do a knowledge gap.**"*

He also names the failure mode by contrast: health is fine at the outer level *"because generally
speaking we understand what it is — but if it was **bioengineering**, you'd lose us."*

### The Neil deGrasse Tyson story, told end to end

**Why Tyson, specifically:** *"what makes him really unique — because **you haven't heard of any other
astrophysicist**, I'm pretty sure of it — is **he's very human in the way he communicates, and he
tells stories, and he's very likable.** He's unlike any other astrophysicist you've ever met. **That's
why he's a household name.**"*

**The story:** a college championship game goes to sudden death; a coin toss decides direction; the
kicker's field goal **hits the upright and goes in**. Tyson pointed out that, given where the stadium
sits, the Earth's rotation at that time of year and the way gravity works, the ball had **a higher
than 50-50 chance of going in** rather than out.

**The kicker (his word — the point):** *"the story was picked up by a local newspaper for the winning
team. They said, '**we won because it was ordained by God**' — because they won the coin toss. **So it
was fated for us to win, because had we been facing the other direction, it would have gone out.**
That thing has gazillion views… **and he only took like 25 seconds to record.**"*

> *"**Had he talked about the Earth's rotation at the beginning, no one would even care.**"*

**The summary he closes on:** *"**You have your niche expertise, what you're really good at. Once
you've broken it down, you need to then talk about it in ways that tap into what everyone's talking
about right now.** … So those of you who are struggling to make content that connects — **you've
missed this fundamental thing.**"*
