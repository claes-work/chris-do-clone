# Why Being 'Humble' Is Hurting You More Than You Think (2025-08-21)

> **Attribution:** a workshop excerpt with a live audience (an attendee named **Steve** and a
> colleague, **Ann**, are quoted; their lines are marked and are not persona data). The argument is
> entirely Chris's.

## Summary

★★★ Eight minutes, one contrarian argument, argued rather than asserted — and argued **with the room
disagreeing**. He rejects humility as a virtue, defines it from the dictionary rather than from the
inspirational poster, tests it with **the marble-jar experiment**, connects it to **tall poppy
syndrome**, and then makes the move that saves the argument from sounding like a defence of ego:
**arrogance is not the opposite of humility — it is overcompensation for low self-esteem.** He lands
not on *think highly of yourself* but on **a fair estimation.**

## Key claims (2025-08-21)

### The opening — a compliment he refuses

> *"One of my good friends texted me the other day: '**Chris, you know why I like you? … Because
> you're really humble.**' And I said, '**I don't think of myself as humble. Actually, I'm offended
> that you say I'm humble. I really am.**'"*

✅ A third instance of the same reflex — refusing a compliment rather than accepting it (cf. the Molly
refusal on [[2025-05-20-yt-efxmE9XX-hE|2025-05-20]] and *"and we all are, to a degree"* on
[[2025-06-29-yt-ngXYEOw8z_o|2025-06-29]]).

**The cultural claim that follows:** *"here and in many European cultures, **humility is a virtue.**
… **It's deep inside your culture, your psyche, your society.** Don't talk too much about yourself.
Don't think you're too good for anything. **And then we're like: why are we broke? Why do we have so
low self-esteem?** I'm not a rocket scientist, **but it kind of feels like it's the same thing.**"*

### ★★★ He makes the room define it — and the definitions collide

He collects definitions from the audience: *"to raise others above yourself"*; *"know enough to know
you don't know much"* (→ *"so basically, **I don't know anything.** Okay, **you see the problem
already**"*); and the poster line, *"it's not thinking **less of yourself**, but thinking **about
yourself less**."*

> ★★★ **The collision he points out:** *"that is **in direct conflict** with this. **When you put
> others above you, the inverse is you put yourself below them. And that's what you all are doing all
> the time.**"*

Then he goes to the dictionary rather than the aphorism — *"she wants to argue with me that **the
dictionary is wrong. You can't just make up your own terms**"* — landing on: **"having a low
estimation of one's own importance."** *"Then **the only debatable word now is *importance*.**"*

### ★★★★ The marble-jar experiment

> *"There's a jar. It's got **100 marbles** in it. If you have **a low estimation**, how many marbles
> in the jar?"* — 40. *"If you have **a high estimation**?"* — 140. *(An aside he enjoys: "**it's
> quite interesting that people use the same numbers on both sides.**")* *"**What is the best
> estimation of what's in the jar?**"* — 100.

> ★★★★ *"So **when you're humble, you're giving me a false estimation. It's bad for you. It's also bad
> for your client. It's bad for your team. It's bad for your family** — because **we need to know.**
> Right? In every other estimation: **how much fuel is in the car? How much food do we have left in
> the pantry? We don't want a high estimation and we don't want a low estimation.**"*

### ★★ The sales consequence, worked live

Asked how this plays in a culture whose tone of voice is humble, he runs it on the attendee:

> *"'Steve, what do you do?' 'Social media content.' '**Do you do a good job?**' 'Sure.' — **That's not
> humble.** The humble [answer] is: '**No, others do better. We're all right.**' **Me as a consumer,
> how am I supposed to feel about you, Steve? Should I rush out and buy from you?**"* — *"Probably
> not."* → *"**At the minimum, just be fair. An accurate estimation. Not high, not low.**"*

⚠️ Note the honesty about the limits of the method: when the attendee says he sells **drinks**, Chris
declines to force it — *"that's a tough one. Hold on… **it's a product-based thing**… **let's park
that one**, because we have to get into some creative marketing at that point."*

### ★★★ Tall poppy syndrome — where the heat is

> *"I have **a huge problem with this word, because it's connected to a lot of pain.** People who are
> like: '**be humble, Chris**' — **tall poppy syndrome.** And then what do we do? **We create a culture
> [where] when somebody does well, we cut them down. What kind of jacked-up culture is that?** So now
> **you're ashamed of doing it and you get attacked immediately.** Yet **where did the big companies,
> the big thought leaders and the big innovation come from? What country?** … You see how there's some
> relationship there. **It's really weird. I'd love to change that.**"*

The counter-case he names: *"in America we say — **you're doing great, good on you, you can do
anything. And you should.** And then we do. **It's called exceptionalism.**"*

### ★★★★ Arrogance is not the opposite — it is overcompensation

This is the move that keeps the argument from being a defence of ego:

> *"I believe why people think this is good is because **they're afraid the opposite is arrogance.**
> … **We think the opposite of this is this. It is not.** You know what arrogance is? **Overcompensating
> for low self-esteem. Anybody who's arrogant is not [operating from a] high estimation of themselves.
> It's a mask.**"*

> *"So I have **supreme belief in self, high self-love, self-esteem, self-confidence — hopefully not
> arrogant** — and I have really high belief."*

### ★★★ The concession, and where the real critique belongs

He does not claim he will win the argument:

> *"**I don't believe I can effectively persuade you to change your mind for 40 years of culture and
> training and society** — and **no one else in the world thinks like this. But maybe I can start
> putting you in that direction.**"*

> ★★★★ *"**The most successful people tend to think that anything is possible for them, and they don't
> feel any guilt or shame about being able to achieve that. How they TREAT OTHERS is where we have the
> critique. How they TALK ABOUT THEMSELVES is where we have the critique.** So **we don't have to
> achieve that and act in this way** — because **no one wants to be around anybody who talks about
> themselves all the time as if they're better than others.**"*

**The landing:** *"**So let's just go for a fair estimation. Not low. That's the problem.**"*

## L3 candidate

★★★ — **promote.** Three items: ★★★★ **the marble-jar experiment**, which turns an abstract virtue
argument into a testable one and gives the position its force (*"when you're humble, you're giving me
a false estimation"*); ★★★★ **arrogance as overcompensation for low self-esteem**, without which the
whole argument reads as a defence of ego; and ★★★ **the critique relocated** — *"how they treat others
is where we have the critique"* — which is what makes *fair estimation* a livable position rather than
a licence. ✅ Also a **third instance of the compliment-refusal reflex**, which is now a documented
behaviour rather than an anecdote.
