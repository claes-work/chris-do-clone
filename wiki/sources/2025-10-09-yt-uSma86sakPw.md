# How to handle client revisions (2025-10-09)

> **Attribution:** a short workshop excerpt with a live room. Two audience answers are marked; all
> teaching is Chris's.

## Summary

★★★ Eight minutes on **why late-cycle revisions happen**, and the answer is not what the room guesses.
It is not unclear proposals or bad questions — it is that **you never gave the client permission to
reject anything.** The fix runs through **stylescapes**, a **power-asymmetry lesson from Kier
McLaren**, and **Chris Voss's three yeses**, ending on a single instruction: **invite the no.**

## Key claims (2025-10-09)

### ★★★★ The diagnosis: you never created space for "destroy all of this"

He first makes the client's position sympathetic: *"if you've ever been a client working with a
professional doing something you don't know how to do — like designing a home — you're like: **at what
stage do I give you input?** And then before you know it, **the bathroom's done.** And you're like: **oh
my god, I hate that tile. I hate that colour.** Now what?"*

> *"**Most of us, as introverts afraid of conflict, we just eat it.** … **But clients are a little bit
> different. They're your clients for a reason. They're like: no, we paid. We're used to working with
> vendors. I don't like this. Change it.**"*

> ★★★★ **The cause, credited to his business coach:** *"**you do not create space for them to say
> 'destroy all of this.' And the whole point of creating stylescapes is for them to say 'destroy all of
> this'** — because **it was relatively little work.** And **problems are always cheaper to solve at the
> beginning than at the end.**"*

### ★★★★ The stylescape pitch, verbatim

> *"These are **stylescapes**. They're **low commitment but high fidelity** reference points to what
> you're going to get. **If you don't like something — if you don't love what you see here — let's not
> move forward. Let's get you to fall in love with this first**, because **it's not going to be that
> far from this.**"*

> *"And **feel free to say anything. Love this, hate this. And use the language you know how** — and
> **I'll ask you questions to interpret it in my world, and I'll say back to you what I'm hearing.** So
> **use your language.** Simple things like *love*, *hate* are fine by me. And then I'll ask: **what
> about this do you love?** 'Oh, it's the colour. It's the texture.' … **I want to get to truth.**"*

★★★ **The diagnosis of the creative's reluctance:** *"most of us are **kind of afraid to have that kind
of dialogue, because we want them to love what we love. And we'll pay for that every single time when
we ignore what their needs are. That's where the problem is. Identify the problem early and solve it.
Create space for someone to tell you: this ain't working.**"*

### ★★★★ The power asymmetry — a Kier McLaren lesson

> *"**Oftentimes as the owner or creative director, you hold a lot of power over the people that report
> to you. And you don't realise how much power you have.**"*

The failure it produces: *"'I need five ideas by tomorrow at 3.' 'All right.' **What happens at 5 the
next day? You don't have the ideas. The ideas are not as good. And you're upset.**"*

> ★★★★ **The fix, scripted:** *"'**I'd like to get these ideas done, but I need you to know you can tell
> me this is not possible — and let's figure out a solution. But if you say yes, then I expect this to
> be done.**'"*

> *"And then all of a sudden **we hear *no* a lot more often now. And it's not what you think. It's not
> the horrible thing that all bosses or managers think.**"* — the *no*s that arrive are useful: *"'sorry,
> because Amy just asked me to do this other thing'"* → *"okay, let me go resolve it"*; or *"'**I can do
> two really good ideas, maybe a third — but I can't do five.**' Can we do that? **Yes. We buy more
> time.**"*

> ★★★★ *"**You have to invite people to tell you no.**"*

### ★★★★ Chris Voss and the three yeses

From ***Never Split the Difference***: *"**there are three yeses, and only one yes is true.** … **We
are looking for the yes. He suggests: look for the *no*.**"*

| # | The yes | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *"**Yes because I understand — I don't agree.**"* | *"I do this with my wife all the time. She's telling me information. **Yes, I understand. Yes, it's factual. Yes.** 'What do you want to do?' **I don't want to do any of this, honey.**" |
| 2 | ★★★ **The shut-up yes** | *"It's because you're annoying. **I'm going to say yes to shut you up.**" |
| 3 | **The true yes** | *"Yes, I want to do this."* |

> ★★★★ *"**You're looking for the *no*, because the *no* is the only truthful answer that you can count
> on reliably.**"* → *"**no gives me power. No gives your clients power.**"*

**The technique:** *"**ask a yes question, but in the negative.**"* With Voss's own example — trying to
invite a best-selling author to speak, with seconds in an autograph line: *"'**Would it be crazy for
you to speak to my class of 20 students?**' — '**No.** Talk to my assistant, we'll book you.' **He
asked the question in the negative. He gave them the power to say no.**"*

⚠️ He flags the ethics himself, lightly: *"this gets into a little bit of, kind of, manipulation."*

### ★★★★ The synthesis

> *"**The reason why you get these late-cycle revisions is because you didn't get buy-in, you don't have
> clear definition, and you did not allow the client to say no.** Just remember: **they have the power,
> and they don't want to feel stupid.** So a lot of times **they just go along with it** — and then you
> get **that call an hour before midnight: 'I don't like anything. Change it all.'**"*

★★★ **And the process instruction:** *"we all have different processes and different approval points. So
**let them know: this is the part in the creative process where there's a lot of changes, and it's
welcomed and invited. This is the time for you to say all the things you want to say — because later on
it's going to be a lot more expensive, and I'm going to discourage you from making changes then.** You
don't want to get into developing a website **only to realise they hate the whole freaking thing. That
is expensive. But at the early stages, it's cheap.**"*
