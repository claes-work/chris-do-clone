# Reluctant Coach Wants To Build Personal Brand — Ep. 2 w/ Matt Essam (2025-10-16)

> ## ✅✅ **The second joint appearance.** This continues the exception documented at
> [[../entities/matt-essam|matt-essam]] — the thirteen **teaching-slot** videos remain Chris-absent and
> do-not-train, but this series is **a two-way debate**, and Chris opens it as *"**episode two of this
> ongoing dialogue with Matt Essam. He's a friend and a person who coaches for The Futur community.**"*
> Essam's positions are marked **[MATT]** and are **the skeptic's side of the argument** — quotable as
> what Chris is answering, **never as teaching**. Chris's material is marked **[CHRIS]**.

## Summary

★★★ **LANDMARK.** Where Ep. 1 argued *why* a personal brand is worth building, Ep. 2 answers a harder
question the skeptic actually asks: **what even is one, and how would I know if I had it?** Chris
supplies **a four-criteria test** distinguishing **reputation from brand** — the first such test in the
corpus — plus a decoupling of brand from wealth, an answer to the irreversibility fear, and **the
frozen-rice experiment**, which he flags as pseudoscience and uses anyway.

## ★★★ The recap he gives, which is the cleanest statement of the distinction

> **[CHRIS]** *"**Branding isn't about marketing.** Marketing is about **how you get sales, how you get
> conversions, how you get customers, how you make money.** And this is a very important endeavour.
> **But we should not mix these two activities up.** … When we are talking about building a brand,
> **it's a long-term thing without clear definitions of return on investment.** What you're doing is
> **finding out more about yourself and putting more of yourself out into the world**, and **aligning
> yourself with people in your tribe who believe what you believe.**"*

> ★★★★ *"**It's like you holding space. It's like the digital campfire — and you're the storyteller, and
> people gather around you to hear stories and to find communion or community in the group that gathers
> around you. It's relational. It's not transactional.**"*

## ★★★★ Reputation vs. brand — the four-criteria test

**The starting distinction:**

> *"**Everyone has a personal reputation. Not everyone has a personal brand. My mom has a reputation.
> She ain't got a brand.** In fact, **very few people actually have a personal brand. They might think
> they do, but they really don't.**"*

**How reputation is constituted** — a thought experiment: *"if we were to get **the 100 people that
know you** — your family, your friends, your partner, your co-workers — and assess what they all think
of you, we'd get some sense of it. **We throw away the edge cases**… **we look at the thing in the
middle.**"* → *"**It's not until this reputation exceeds your immediate primary and secondary circle of
people that we start to say you have a personal brand.** So **the people who don't know you, haven't
interacted with you, already have an impression of you.**"*

✅ **Marty Neumeier, borrowed and named:** *"**when enough people come to a similar gut feeling about who
you are, then you have a brand.** And **that's deliberately vague and nebulous** — because **what is
enough people? We don't know. But we know it when we know it.**"*

> ★★★★ **And the decoupling, which is the load-bearing move:** *"**You could be the poorest person —
> broke, living hand-to-mouth — and have an amazing personal brand.** Conversely, **you could be the
> most unknown person, be the richest mother-effer in the world, and have no personal brand. They're
> not connected concepts, and we have to decouple these ideas.**"*

### The four criteria

| # | Criterion | In his words |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Reach beyond your vertical** | *"**Do people outside of your vertical or your industry know who you are?** That's usually a good sign."* |
| **2** | **You can attach your name to a thing and add value to it** | *"**When you attach your name to something, that thing becomes valuable and people will buy it. Your name means something.** … And **it has to be outside of what you already do.**" |
| **3** | **You stand at the top of your vertical** | *"**You're not just another graphic design person who's speaking — you're THE graphic designer who's speaking about this.** … **the top five, top 3% of the people doing what you do.** Your reputation has exceeded that, so **you've become, at least within your peers, a household name.**" |
| **4** | **You can jump verticals** | *"**When you're done doing what you're doing and it no longer interests you** — because your personal brand is so strong — **you're like: hey guys, I'm going to do this thing now. It's not quite related to what I did before. And then the people are right there with you.**" |

**Criterion 2, worked with three cases:**

- **Cleon Peterson** — a graphic designer turned fine artist who released **an ~$800 resin sculpture**
  on pre-order and *"sold over 240 units… **we can all do the math**"* (~$240,000). *"**This is an
  incredible power that somebody has, based on the fandom he's been able to create.**"*
- **Twenty One Pilots merch** — *"a t-shirt is $30. The **manufactured** price is probably five or six
  bucks at most… **they've created $25 of additional value, because they've attached some version of
  their brand essence onto that thing.**"*
- **The counter-example, which is what makes the criterion sharp:** *"if [a coach] is doing one-on-one
  coaching, **that's not printing money. He's literally working for it.**"*

**Criterion 4**, via **Ryan Reynolds** and **The Rock**: *"**they're not selling acting. They're not
selling a 90-minute piece of linear entertainment. They're selling alcohol. They're selling mobile
phone plans. They're selling wallets.** … **They can sell anything at this point. And the pitch isn't
that hard. They just say: I'm doing this. And because we feel an affinity, a connection towards them,
all things being equal, we'd rather buy from them than their competitor.**"* → ★★★★ *"**That's what a
brand is.**"*

**And the spectrum answer**, for people with niche notoriety: *"they are **good business operators**.
There are people who build amazing boats. You don't know them. **Everybody in the industry, if you
wanted a boat built, would know them. But that's it. They're not known outside their vertical** — and
**they cannot leverage their brand until they're able to move forward.**"* With **Shawn Stussy** as
the crossing-over case: a California surfboard shaper who borrowed **his uncle's signature** as his
mark, put it on boards, then on a t-shirt — *"**Shawn Stussy is considered the godfather of
streetwear.**"*

## ★★★★ He gives the skeptic an out — repeatedly

This is the most notable feature of the episode: **he does not oversell.**

> *"If you were able to **run a great lifestyle business and be content and happy with that** — which
> sounds like that's what you want — **we don't need to work on personal brand. You just need to get
> your business in order and do what you've got to do, and be content with that.** And **there are
> millions of businesses like that where we've never heard of the person, they're doing really well in
> their life, and they don't have to work that much.**"*

> ★★★★ **The metaphor he uses on himself:** *"**you're like a fish who doesn't want to go to the boat.
> And I'm the fisherman trying to bring you to the boat** — and you're peeling out the line, and you
> sometimes come in because you're curious, and then you're like *no*, and you just run again. And I'm
> like: **well, I think there are a lot more people out there listening that are more like you than
> there are that are like 'I'm all in, Chris. Where do I sign up?'**"*

★★ He is also realistic about persuasion: *"**I don't believe any one person is going to listen to one
episode like: that's it, I was anti and now I'm all for.** Some people are like that — **but those are
the early adopters, the innovators.** The **middle majority** are still like: **no, I'm with team
Matt.**"*

## ★★★★ The actual argument — what happens when you stop working

> *"**There's something much bigger than what we're talking about here, which is: what happens when
> [you] stop working? [You] will make no more money. [You] won't have any assets that [you] can sell,
> generally speaking.**"* → *"**there are passive income vehicles, or ways for you to leverage your
> expertise — what people like about you, your community — that go beyond this.**"*

**And the income claim, with its conditions stated:** *"**I know this is going to sound really weird
and disconnected from reality. I apologise — send all your hate letters to Matt.** … **I believe that
in this world, with so many opportunities — especially if you have some resources behind you, if you
have a roof over your head, if you have a stable family life, if you have some money you can invest,
internet access and infrastructure like roads — the ability to make between $100,000 and a million
dollars a year is not as difficult as it used to be.** … **If all I ever wanted to do was make a
million dollars a year net, it's not that hard for me. And so I think there are bigger challenges
here.**"*

> ⚠️ **Worth recording as stated:** he **names the privileged preconditions explicitly** before making
> the claim, and flags in advance that it will sound disconnected.

★★★ **The magic sweet spot** — his description of what a strong brand actually produces: *"**opportunities
are being created for you without you even trying. That's where you get into the magic sweet spot.**
Event organisers are conspiring right now to figure out how to get you and who they've got to call for
an introduction… and **they're creating demand for things you don't even think about.**"* With the
cost, admitted: *"**the consequence is you do lose some of your privacy. Some — but that's all
manageable too.**"* And the correction of the skeptic's fear: *"**you're focused too much on the
recognised-on-the-street part, which is not what any even famous people want. They want their
privacy.**"*

## ★★★★ The irreversibility fear — and his answer

> **[MATT]** *"**I don't know if I can reverse it. I don't know if, when I go full in, I can ever go
> back.** … If I think about people that are famous, **the stuff that's bad that came out about them, I
> still remember. And even after they die, people still remember them.** … **You can't put the genie
> back in the bottle.**"*

> ★★★★ **[CHRIS]** *"**That's the risk of doing anything of consequence. Period. If you want to be an
> anonymous person and not be consequential, then you don't run that risk. But anybody who does
> anything consequential runs the risk that what they do can be interpreted as both good and bad — and
> that's going to follow you around.**"*

**And then he disputes the premise itself:** *"there are **a bunch of OG YouTubers nobody talks about
any more. Nobody even looks them up. So you CAN put the genie back in the bottle — and it's not even by
their choice.** They're either cancelled or they're no longer relevant… **I look them up, they're still
making content. It's just that no one's talking about them any more.**"* → *"**people's memories are
not that strong. We're bombarded with new things we're supposed to remember, and so it will
disappear.**"*

## ★★★★ The frozen-rice experiment — flagged as pseudoscience, used anyway

Heard from **Erwin McManus** at a conference. Three jars of cooked rice: one receives criticism and
negative speech, one receives affirmations and appreciation, and one is **the control — ignored
entirely.**

The affirmed jar ferments into something savoury; the criticised jar grows **black mould**. Then he
turns it into a question for Essam: *"what happens to the control?"* — *"Nothing. It just stays the
same."*

> ★★★★ *"**It actually has twice the rate of black mould in it.** … **And that's the same reaction I
> had** — which was: no, it's probably fine, very little has happened to it."*

> ★★★★ **The conclusion he draws:** *"**In this life it's best to receive positive energy. But it's
> WORSE to receive nothing — to be ignored, to know that your life, everything you do, does not matter
> at all.** So **I would choose positive energy, and then I would choose negative energy — and the
> thing that we don't want to do is just be completely ignored, like we're not relevant at all.**"* →
> *"**there's only one thing worse than everyone talking about you behind your back, and that's no one
> talking about you at all.**"*

> ⚠️ **He labels it himself**: *"a Japanese **quote-unquote physicist**… **pseudoscience stuff.**"*
> **Record the flag with the story.** The argument he builds on it does not depend on the experiment
> being true — it is an illustration of a position about attention, not evidence for one.

## ★★ The Taki Moore story — what made someone memorable to him

Three things, at a workshop, from a stranger he did not recognise:

1. **The ice cream.** A colleague was eating an ice-cream bar; Chris joked *"I didn't know we were
   serving ice cream."* — *"**you know what [he] did? He got up, barefoot, sprinted out**, went to the
   store, and said: 'Chris, I got you an ice cream bar.'"* → *"**I'm lactose intolerant.** I wasn't
   saying I wanted one… I just gave it to the next person. **But that caught my attention right
   away.**"
2. **The disclosure and the ask.** *"'Hey Chris, I'm so sorry. **I'm ADHD. I can't sit still. Is that
   okay?**' I'm like: **dude, move as much as you want. There's no rules here.**"*
3. **The bathroom approach**, still barefoot — *"'I know this is a weird place to talk.'"* → *"**that's
   the Asian OCD in me.** I'm like: oof."*

> *"**So those three things were what made him remarkable.**"*
