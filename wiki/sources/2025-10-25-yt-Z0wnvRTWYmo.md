# Your writing feels flat because you're missing this (2025-10-25)

> **Attribution:** a **live line-by-line workshop.** A participant reads their own draft aloud
> and Chris edits it in real time. **The story being workshopped is the participant's, not
> Chris's** — the 1996 Portland mortgage-office narrative belongs to them. Only Chris's
> teaching is persona data; the participant's lines appear only where needed to show what he is
> reacting to.

## Summary

★★★★ Eight rules of narrative writing, extracted from **one draft being repaired in public**.
The method is the value: he stops at the first sentence for a missing **establishing shot**,
expands **tension** before allowing relief, demands **sensory detail** in place of summary,
insists every paragraph carry a **named emotion**, and closes with the structural instruction
that makes the rest cheap — **start at the end.** ✅ It is the writing counterpart to his
speaking advice, and it doubles as an unusually concrete demo of what Content Lab is.

## Key claims (2025-10-25)

### ★★★★★ Think like a director — the establishing shot

He interrupts the draft's very first line, before any content critique:

> *"Okay, we have to pause. **There's no setting.** It's 1975, it's Charlotte, North Carolina,
> whatever it is. **I want you to think like a director.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**All films work this way. All films open with an establishing shot.** It's a giant
> spaceship. It's the wild wild west. It's the jungles of Brazil, the rainforest — **and then
> you know where you are.** And then what happens is **a story is told in increasingly close-up
> shots.** … **Wide vista, tighter, tighter, tighter — lightsaber, somebody's scratching your
> fingernail. That's how it's told.** So when you write your story, it's like that. **Wide —
> and then tighter, tighter, tighter.**"*

**The repair, in three words**, and his reaction to it: participant → *"1996. Portland, Oregon.
21 years old. Recently out of college."* → *"**See, now I know where I'm at. So you've oriented
me now.** Perfect."*

★★★ **The homework he assigns for it:** *"**next time you watch any movie, I want you to pay
attention to this.**"*

### ★★★★ Expand the tension before you resolve it

The draft says *"I quit my job."* He refuses to let it pass:

> *"**Let's expand that a little bit more. Can we do more than that?** 'I quit my job. I can't
> believe I said…' or 'I've had enough', or '**three weeks of pondering this question led to
> this moment**.' **So there's a lot of tension here. We want to expand the tension a little
> bit more.**"*

The mechanism, stated: *"**what we want to do is — we're always building tension.** 'I quit my
job… **but I desperately needed it, because rent was due.** I didn't have a lot of options,
**but I knew this wasn't it.**' **Okay, this is just basic writing stuff.**"*

> ★★★★ **And the ordering rule that follows from it, delivered at the end of the session:**
> *"this one is **dread, doom, fear, anxiety** — [then] **relief. Relief has to come very late.
> Because if you get the relief, I don't read the rest.**"*

### ★★★★ Sensory detail beats summary

The draft calls the boss *"an alcoholic."* Chris interrogates the word rather than accepting it:

> *"**How did you know?** … Have you ever been around alcoholics? … **Like you said — bloodshot
> eyes, staggered or slurred speech** — nothing? … **If you can recall — because those details
> take us right there.**"*

And the second repair: *"**instead of 'who happened to be an alcoholic'** — '**what I didn't
know: he was an alcoholic… which I only figured out later on.**' **You see, some of those
details really help your story.**"*

> ★★★★ **The general form:** *"otherwise it becomes **a generic story of observation**, versus
> **putting me in there and making me feel the sweat.**"*

★★★ **When the draft does it right, he says so immediately** — participant: *"red blotches all
over his face; six-foot, fifty-plus-year-old dude versus me, barely five-two, ninety-six pounds
wet"* → *"**Yes. That's great, by the way.**"*

### ★★★★ Write long, then cut — and the AI step

> ★★★★ *"**Now I'm going to tell you how to write a long-ass story with great details. Then you
> have to go back in and edit the sucker down.**"*

> ★★★ **The AI role he actually endorses — compression, not generation:** *"**you could speak
> this to GPT or Claude and then say: 'format this for LinkedIn.' And it will strip out what it
> doesn't think is necessary — but give it the juicy details.**"*

★★★ **The two-way rule underneath it:** *"**storytelling is the constant editing out and adding
in. When it's thin, we add in. When it's too much, we edit out.**"*

### ★★★★ Where the draft goes flat — and why

Reading the section where the participant lands the job in three flat sentences:

> *"**Your energy, the heat, starts to dissipate here. We need to always build heat and
> anticipation.** … **this part needs more work. It almost seems matter-of-fact — and nothing is
> like that.**"*

He then repairs it by **interrogation**, which is the transferable part: *"**Did you want this
job?** … 'One ad in the paper caught my attention.' **What did it say?** … '**It talked about
opportunity.**' **What was opportunity?** … '**Sales job, on-the-job training.**' **Do those
things appeal to you? … You should probably put some of that in there.**"*

> ★★★★ **And the setup he spots the writer having skipped:** *"**you set it up like you got
> rejected, rejected, rejected. So now you have to set up why you *didn't* get rejected. This is
> what I mean — this part's thin.**"*

★★★ **The ending, withheld:** *"'February 14, 1996, I officially started' — **this probably
needs to be phrased differently, so we don't know the outcome.**"* → his rewrite:
*"**I didn't know then, but what started out in 1996 would wind up being a lifelong career.**"*
→ *"**So the punchline could hit harder.**"*

### ★★★★★ Art is transferred emotion — so every paragraph needs one

> ★★★★★ *"**Art is: you have an emotion, and you create something that when somebody else sees
> or experiences it, has a shared emotion with you — having not spoken to you. When you watch a
> movie and you're crying, it's because that person wanted you to feel this emotion.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The instruction that follows:** *"**So in every paragraph you could ask yourself: what
> is the emotion? No emotion, there's no story.** … **What's the emotion? What's the emotion?
> What's the emotion? If there's no emotion — rewrite it. Rewrite it until you hit the
> emotion.**"*

★★★ **A cultural objection, and his answer.** Participant: *"growing up in my culture, you don't
really show a lot of emotion."* → *"**Well, screw that.**"* — and then the argument, which is
that the objection is a category error: *"**even if you did — let's just say you're in an
Italian family — you still wouldn't write like this.**"* ✅ i.e. expressive writing is a craft,
not a temperament.

> ⚠️ **He is not exempting himself.** The same *"in my culture you don't show emotion"* framing
> appears throughout the corpus as **his own** upbringing; here he is answering someone else's
> version of it. See [[../persona/biography|biography]] and the *"emotional immigrant boy"*
> line at [[2025-10-30-yt-3ikc89b-tkE|2025-10-30]], five days later.

### ★★★★★ Start at the end

The structural instruction he saves for last, and the one that makes everything else easier:

> ★★★★★ *"**The way you can do this is: you start at the end. If you know how the story ends,
> the writing of the story becomes really easy.** … It's like **we're going to make a meal, but
> we don't know what we're making — so we use lots of ingredients. It's really messy. It's not
> very efficient. And then it's like soup. But if you're like, 'I'm going to make beef
> Wellington' — well, what's the first step? The pastry part is kind of important. And you have
> a much clearer idea.** **So: start at the end. Where do you want this plane to land? What is
> the point you want to make?**"*

> ★★★★★ **How he does it live on stage** — the clearest description in the corpus of his own
> retrieval process: *"**if I'm on stage and somebody asks a question, I know how the story ends.
> Now I'm looking for a story beginning, because I know what the point is. The point is: let go
> of hate. So I'm searching my mind really quickly — when was the time when I held onto hate
> that really hurt me?**"*

→ **The editing licence it grants:** *"**if you start at the end, the story becomes a lot easier
to write — and then you can write crazy things, and you edit out every part of that story that
doesn't drive home this point better, because you know where it lands.**"*

### ★★★ Why templates fail

> *"**The problem is we want quick wins. We want templates — and that's the problem. We get
> exactly what we ask for: quick wins and templates. But we don't learn how to speak. We don't
> know what our voice is and what our ideas are. That's what I try and help people with.**"*

The live case: the participant admits their Instagram videos come from someone else's scripts —
*"I feel like it's coming from me sometimes"* → *"**I can look at it and say: this sounds
generic. I'll ask you: where are you in this? And we'll work on it.**" → *"**that's why I
stopped making it — because I was like, this is not me.**"* (participant)

### ★★★ Content Lab, described concretely

> *"**there are two components. Live feedback on your specific work** — so **if you haven't
> posted anything, if you haven't written anything, if you don't have any presentations, it's
> not right for you.** Second part is **a series of self-study modules** that teach discrete
> principles about **storytelling, about writing, about finding your brand voice.** … **The thing
> that makes it different is the feedback you're going to get from me.**"*

> ★★★ **The gap he says it fills:** *"**what non-creative people don't understand is: they put
> stuff out into the universe and you get weird engagement feedback — because you're not getting
> it from someone who's been there before telling you: tweak this, change that, alter this.
> That's what I do.**"*

★★★ **And the boundary he draws around it**, consistent with his stated indifference to
mechanics: *"**no engagement groups, no weird black-hat stuff. It's all above board, white hat.
And I don't really care about schedules or posting times and optimising for any of that. I'm
sure some of you want to do that, and that's okay if you do. But my style is: when I feel
inspired, I write. Luckily, I'm inspired a lot, so I write a lot.**"*

## L3 candidate

★★★★ — **promote.** Three items, all of which are *named, teachable rules* the corpus currently
lacks in writing form: ★★★★★ **the establishing shot** — *think like a director; wide, then
tighter* — which is the most concrete structural advice he has given on openings;
★★★★★ **"no emotion, there's no story"** together with its definition of art as *transferred
emotion*, which grounds his storytelling advice in a stated aesthetic rather than a technique;
and ★★★★★ **"start at the end"**, which additionally explains **how he retrieves stories live on
stage** — *"I know how the story ends, now I'm looking for a beginning"* — a mechanism for his
own speaking that has been visible in the corpus for years but never explained until here.
