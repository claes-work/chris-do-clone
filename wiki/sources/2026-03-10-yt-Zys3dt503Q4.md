# If I started over as a graphic designer, I'd do this to hit \$100k (2026-03-10)

> **Attribution:** a **solo teaching video**, no guests. Everything here is Chris's except the
> named books and the two credited sources (**Phil M. Jones**, **Kier McLaren**).

## Summary

★★★★★ **The most complete practical roadmap in the corpus** — a full career path from beginner to
a sustainable six-figure design practice, in five named stages: **survival → stability → systems →
scale → sustain.** Nearly every stage contains something the wiki does not have anywhere else: a
**typography reading list**, the **tracing method he used in design school**, the **three-to-five
portfolio rule**, the **imagination gap**, **geographic arbitrage**, two **verbatim scripts**, and
★★★★★ **Kier McLaren named with the revenue figures attached** — \$2.2M to \$3.9M.

## Key claims (2026-03-10)

### ★★★ The premise

> *"**Most designers never make it past survival mode. It's not because you're not talented. It's
> because someone has not shown you how to do this.**"* — with the credential: *"over the past 10
> years, I've helped thousands of creators just like you."*

---

## ★★★★★ Stage 1 — Survival

**The symptoms:** *"you're saying yes to too many things"* · logos, social posts, flyers, *"some
paid, some free for the exposure"* · *"**the work isn't good, it's not consistent, and you're not
working with great people and building long-term relationships**"* · *"money is unpredictable,
you're exhausted, and you're questioning the value that you are able to provide."*

> ★★★★ **The reframe:** *"**But this stage isn't failure. It's just a lack of awareness.**"*

### ★★★★★ The reading list

He names the three core tools (**Illustrator, Photoshop, InDesign** — *"if you don't have access,
find the free or low-cost alternative and you'll be just fine. But I'm an Adobe guy. I talk about
what I know"*), then the books, which are the durable part:

| Book | What it's for, in his words |
|---|---|
| ***Stop Stealing Sheep*** — **Erik Spiekermann** | typography fundamentals |
| ***Typographic Design: Form and Communication*** — **Rob Carter, Philip Meggs** | typography as a system |
| **Josef Müller-Brockmann** / Swiss graphic design | *"**amazing work that you need to study and EMULATE for a period of time, until you figure out what's happening**"* |
| ***Geometry of Design*** — **Kimberly Elam** | *"grid systems… **so that you understand what's working underneath all that design stuff that you admire**"* |
| ***A Smile in the Mind*** | *"**teaches you conceptual design, communication design**"* |

> ★★★★ *"**Read those books cover to cover. Read it multiple times, until you start to understand
> the principles.**"*

★★★ **On software fluency:** *"**learn not just one way of doing, but multiple ways of doing
something — and learn the very best way to do something, so you're not fussing around on the
computer.**"*

### ★★★★★ The tracing method — from his own design school

> ★★★★★ *"**In the beginning, if you're literally copying and tracing things, that's totally fine.
> BUT YOU CANNOT EXHIBIT THAT WORK AS ORIGINAL WORK.** You can clarify: I'm merely tracing this —
> and literally show the before."*

> ★★★★★ **How he learned:** *"**I remember back in design school, what I would do is pick up a book
> on Swiss design, put over a piece of tissue paper, and literally draw with a ruler and pen what's
> happening on that page — until I started to get a feel for proportions, spacing, and
> contrast.**"*

★★★★ **Filming it is the by-product, not the point:** *"you'll initially attract other designers on
their creative journey — but eventually, **if you do this consistently enough and it's well
produced, you'll start to catch the eye of art directors and designers who can see you developing
and growing.** And they're going to keep you in their mind, **so that when you ultimately reach
out, you're not going to be reaching out as a cold stranger.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The instruction most people ignore:** *"**I can't emphasise enough for you to SLOW DOWN
> in this stage and just really enjoy it. It's the work that very few people do — and they skip
> this process, and then they wind up struggling for the rest of their creative careers.**"*

★★★★ **Why the masters and not the feed:** *"**the work that you look at is going to be fresh. It's
going to look new — but it's created decades ago, and potentially even decades before you were
born. So we want to learn these timeless principles of design that will withstand the test of time.
WE DON'T WANT TO BE CHASING TRENDS HERE.**"*

### ★★★★★ Three to five pieces

> *"Everyone always asks me the same question: how many pieces do you need in order to go get
> meaningful work? **And the answer is going to surprise you. It's not 50. It's not 20. It's not
> even 10. It's somewhere between THREE TO FIVE pieces that show a consistent level of thinking and
> aesthetic.**"*

> ★★★★★ **And the reason, which is the whole argument for editing:** *"**edit out everything that
> doesn't fit within this criteria. Because when we look at work and we see something bad or not
> well thought out — WE ASSUME THAT THIS WILL BE THE THING THAT YOU DELIVER TO US.**"*

★★★★ **A biographical aside:** *"**my younger brother, who is a working professional visual effects
artist, learned typography from me.** … once he learned how to manipulate type on a screen, it
taught him a lot of other principles about design — composition, scale, contrast, repetition,
consonance and dissonance — **and now he's been a working freelancer for over 25 years.**"*

## ★★★★★ Stage 2 — Stability

> *"**The only way I know how to do this is to say no to supposed opportunities.**"* — and the cost
> of not doing it: *"**every single thing that they ask you to do is a new thing for you to learn
> every single time. THAT WILL ERODE YOUR CONFIDENCE. It doesn't allow you to build strong
> skills.**"*

### ★★★★★ The follow-five method — a complete outreach system

**1. Follow deliberately.** *"**Pick a handful of people. Figure out what's in common with them and
follow them, and make sure that you subscribe to everything they do** — podcast, newsletter. **Five
per month.** … you may want to **unfollow other people** so that when these people post something,
you see it."*

> ★★★★★ **2. Be early.** *"**It's important that you respond and be one of the EARLIEST responders.
> So the window is roughly the first 15 minutes.** And think about it: **when a smaller company
> posts something, they just literally hit send or upload — they're going to hang around for a few
> minutes.**"* → over time *"they're going to start to recognise: **hey, Bob or Mary consistently
> shows up asking thoughtful questions.**"*

> ★★★★★ **3. Wait for the signal before you DM.** *"I recommend **at least 30 days of interacting**
> with someone. … **and I would wait to slip into their DM until they respond or comment on
> something that you've done. NOW YOU KNOW THEY SEE YOU.**"*

> ★★★★★ **4. The message — freestyled on camera:**
>
> *"'**Hey Bob, I really admire your company's work. I've been following you for a number of years
> or months. And if there's ever an opportunity where you have overflow work that you can't do
> in-house, I'd love the opportunity to help you out. It's important for me to work with people
> like you, and I'm willing to work on a trial basis for whatever dollar amount that you think is
> fair. Please give me an opportunity. Here's a link to my work. Let me know if there's a good fit
> here.**'"*
>
> → *"**Now, I just freestyled that. Write your own version — but get to the core of the message.
> Make sure it's genuine.**"*

★★★★ **The tell he checks:** *"**if you don't follow them, that's a really poor sign that you're a
fan — cuz clearly you're not a fan, cuz you don't even follow them.**"*

**5. One follow-up, then stop.** The second message — *"**I just want to make sure this is floating
back to the top… if you don't have any work opportunities, I'd love to take 15 minutes of your time
to get a review of some of the work that I'm doing**"* — and then:

> ★★★★★ *"**If you still don't hear back from them, just chill out for a while. Don't hit them with
> another message, cuz they're going to feel like: man, this is super aggressive and it's very
> spammy. TREAT PEOPLE THE WAY YOU WANT TO BE TREATED.**"*

> ⚠️ **With the consequence stated from his own inbox:** *"please, please, please do not do what 99%
> of the people out on the internet do to me all the time — **because what you'll get from somebody
> like me is an immediate delete and block, and that kills the opportunity.**"*

### ★★★★★ The imagination gap

> *"**If you want to work with biotech companies, the portfolio project should look like biotech
> companies.** … It's really good when someone who's in that space looks at your portfolio and
> goes: **wow, we could have used this on our last campaign.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**You want to CLOSE THE IMAGINATION GAP. You don't want to make them work that hard to
> figure out how the heck is this person going to help me. When you do that, you're requiring too
> much of their cognitive processing power — and they're just going to move on to the next person
> who shows up who's closer to what they want than you are.**"*

★★★ **With the matchmaking analogy:** *"**we don't want to take a big risk when we hire someone
thinking: will they get this or won't they? WE WANT A SURE THING.**"*

### ★★★★★ The case study, in five parts

> *"**A case study isn't just your work. A case study includes WORDS AND IMAGES that tell a story
> about the problem that you solved and how you came up with a solution.**"*

1. **Who's the client** — a brief introduction.
2. **Identify the problem** — *"they needed packaging that reflected their values… that was
   inconsistent across different touchpoints."*
3. **Show process** — *"**the ideational stuff, the stuff that you worked through to arrive at an
   ultimate design. If you just show the design, people are a little confused as to how you work
   and what it's like to work with you.**"*
4. **The finished thing.**
5. **Mock-ups** — *"**do not underestimate the value and power of mock-ups. They allow us to see
   design in multi-dimensional ways** — large scale, or small like on a business card, on social,
   on somebody's hat. **This allows us to feel like this was a real project.**"*

> ★★★★★ **What he looks for in the process section:** *"**I want to see a BREADTH of exploration. I
> don't want to just see little variations to one design. I want to see that you tried this thing
> and this thing over here and a couple things in between — and logically you figured out that
> these two or three things are the best options, and you whittle it down.**"*

★★★★ **The file-hygiene tip that makes it possible:** *"**it's much harder to recreate this work
later.** If you're working on a logo sketch, you might have one file where you overwrite every
single iteration. **So what you should do is just copy, drag over, and paste, and work on the next
version — this way you have little artifacts of all the different things you've tried.**"*

### ★★★★★ Geographic arbitrage

> *"You want to focus on doing design work for **markets that can afford you.** At least here in the
> United States, it's on the coast — **San Francisco, Los Angeles, New York, and down to Miami.
> They tend to have better budgets** — companies that are used to working with people who have a
> higher cost of living."*

> ★★★★★ *"**This is where arbitrage can work really well in your favour. If you're from a small
> country, if you're from a little island, or if you're from the middle of the US — cost of living
> is much lower. YOU DON'T HAVE TO CHARGE THE LOCAL RATE, because the people buying this stuff
> already expect to pay this kind of price.**"*

> ★★★★★ **And the objection, answered:** *"'**But Chris, they called us cuz they wanted a better
> deal. I'm in South America, I'm in Asia, I'm in Thailand…**' — **the reason people call people in
> those markets is they're looking for a bargain. They're looking to exploit the fact that there's
> a low minimum wage where you live, and they want to pay you less. BUT IF YOU PRESENT LIKE
> EVERYONE ELSE, THEY WILL BE FORCED TO HIRE YOU AND PAY YOU THAT RATE — BECAUSE YOU'RE THAT
> GOOD.**"* → *"you need to learn to act, to walk, and to present like a professional in these
> coastal cities."*

### ★★★★★ Price on the call

> ★★★★★ *"**You need to tell them what it's going to cost ON THE CALL. For a lot of you, you're
> going to be scared to do this. But if you don't do this and you kick this down the road in a
> proposal — YOU'RE GOING TO BE IN THE PROPOSAL BUILDING BUSINESS, NOT IN THE DESIGN BUSINESS.**"*

★★★★ **Then read the reaction:** *"**gauge their reaction to the price. And if they hem and haw,
ask: 'I feel or sense some hesitation on your part. What do we think?' And then they'll tell you
the budget.**"* — including, occasionally, upward: *"'**it should be much higher than this. We're
prepared to pay more. We want to make sure you have all the resources to do this project
correctly.**' **This is really good news for you.**"*

### ★★★★★ When cheap work is allowed

> ★★★★★ *"**There is absolutely no point, in my opinion, to do low-budget work that also looks like
> ass. What are you getting out of that? A horrible client experience, no money, and a whole lot of
> resentment.**"*

So require at least one of three: ① *"the project is going to turn out great"* · ② *"it's exactly
in the vertical or the industry that you want to work in"* · ③ *"they're really great people and
they're going to encourage you to do the very best work that you can do"* — or you're freelancing
inside a bigger firm with people you admire.

> ★★★★ *"**You don't always have to make money upfront if what you're getting is of greater value
> than the money you would have received.**"*

## ★★★★★ Stage 3 — Systems

### ★★★★★ The five-step process, and the line under everything

> *"**Develop a five-step process. What are the five things that every project goes through?**
> Typically it's **discovery** — figuring out what the problem is — **design exploration, final
> design, applications, and quality assurance.**"* → document it on your website.

**The script:** *"'**All our clients go through a five-step process. It's how we can ensure the work
always turns out good. Are you good with me following this five-step process?**'"* →

> ★★★★★ **And if they say no:** *"'**Great. Well, we can do it your way — but I can no longer
> guarantee the outcomes. If you're okay with that, I'm okay with that.**'"*

> ★★★★★ **The idea it all rests on:** *"**Clients do not choose the best option. THEY CHOOSE THE
> LEAST RISKY ONE. Your job is to appear to bring them the least amount of risk.**"*

### ★★★★★ Blind, 1995 — the fullest telling, with the payoff quantified

> *"**When we first started my production company called Blind in 1995, I did what you did.** I did
> logo design for somebody who would pay me for that. We would do broadcast design. We would do
> main titles. I would do a little web design. **All of these things we did for anyone who would
> pay us.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**And it became very apparent to me really early on — within MONTHS of doing this — that
> outside of my core skill set, which was logo design, everything that came in, I WAS AN AMATEUR.
> I was struggling to figure things out.** … So much in fact that **one of our clients sent one of
> their senior animators to BABYSIT the project, cuz they weren't sure we could pull it off.**"*

> ⚠️ Note the word: **"babysit"** here, *"overlook"* at [[2025-10-30-yt-3ikc89b-tkE|2025-10-30]] —
> the same event, told with a harsher verb. Both are his.

> ★★★★★ *"**And I knew then and there: if I'm not going to drill in and be focused on one thing,
> I'm never going to be really great at anything.**"* → motion design → *"**Those skills still live
> with me, but I didn't market those. I didn't accept those projects until I got really good and
> well known for doing motion design.**"* → twenty years later, asked whether he knows anything
> about print and logo design: *"**And to that I smiled. This is what I know how to do really
> well.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The payoff, quantified for the first time in the corpus:** *"**This allowed me to grow
> from doing TENS OF THOUSANDS of dollars for a project, to HUNDREDS OF THOUSANDS, and even up to A
> MILLION DOLLARS for a project. This is the reward of you being a specialist and being really good
> at something.**"*

### ★★★★★ Pattern recognition is leverage

> ★★★★★ *"**If you're repeatedly exposed to the same problem, as you spot patterns — that's what
> intelligence is. IT'S PATTERN RECOGNITION. And when you see patterns, this equals LEVERAGE. And
> leverage equals power and money and value and everything else that you desire.**"* → *"once you
> have this kind of leverage, you're able to command a higher price and **shift away from selling
> your time to selling outcomes or deliverables.**"*

### ★★★★★ Work that can be done off-site

> ★★★★★ *"**Any of these skills that you do ON-SITE does not allow you to grow your practice —
> because they'll still see you as LABOUR. And labour is measured against time.**"* → off-site
> buys three things: *"**you can set your own hours. You can employ other people to help you. And
> you can use any tools and software that you want to make your work process faster.**"*

### ★★★★★ Why hourly is broken

> ★★★★★ *"**Hourly-based billing or day rates punishes you for being efficient. It disincentivises
> you to invest in equipment, processes, plugins** — everything else you need to make your work go
> faster.**"*

> ★★★★ *"**When you price based on hour, you create a lot of anxiety in the buyer's mind.** And
> you've all been here before: **how long will this take? What will this cost? And the answer is: I
> don't know. I can only give you a best guess.** So we **remove the anxiety about the unknown by
> giving them a flat project fee** — they know it's not going to exceed that price unless they
> change the scope or make too many revisions."*

### ★★★★ The invoice number

> ★★★★ *"**Don't start the invoice at number 001** — it literally is the first invoice you've ever
> sent. And when a client receives this, **they're going to smile and say: oh yeah, this person has
> not been in business very long.** I highly recommend you **start the invoice at number
> 00201.**"* → *"**I know these are all optics — but perception is reality here.**"*

### ★★★★★ The \$2,500 minimum, and the script

**The math:** \$100,000 ÷ 10 months = **\$10,000/month** ÷ 4 projects = **\$2,500 minimum per
project.**

> ★★★★★ *"When a client says '**I have \$1,500. I have \$1,800**' — whatever they say — **you're
> going to smile. You're going to stay really cool. And you're going to look back at them very
> STOICALLY and say: 'My minimum project fee is \$2,500. IT'S POLICY. Is there a way that we can
> figure out to give you what you want and that you're able to meet my minimum? Otherwise, I can't
> take you on as a client.'**"*

✅ **"It's policy"** is the same word he reaches for at
[[2025-12-11-yt--_sTrDwoZyM|2025-12-11]] — *don't explain.*

> ★★★★ *"**You don't have to get into value-based pricing. You don't even have to have a value
> conversation.**"* — and the buyer's-side reframe: *"**\$2,500 might sound like a lot for you. But
> in the buyer's mind, if they have a bigger problem to solve — \$2,500 is easy cost for MEDICINE
> to make a problem go away.**"*

### ★★★★★ Raise prices when demand exceeds capacity

> *"**When you have more demand than the capacity to do the work, this is a great opportunity for
> you to raise your prices. The market is literally telling you: we want more of you, and there's
> not more of you to give. So the only way you can control demand is by raising the prices.**"*

> ★★★★ **With his own case:** *"**in my own life, every time I raise my speaking rates, it does
> slow down a little bit. AND THAT'S THE WHOLE POINT. But eventually it picks back up — and then
> now you're doing the same amount of volume at a much higher price.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**If it were me, I'd rather work half as much, charge twice as much, while I'm making the
> exact same amount of money.**"* — and what the recovered time is **for**: *"**you'll save
> something left for yourself, so that you can work on your process and you can do personal
> development. You can learn more. You can read books. You can attend webinars, go to networking
> events, go to conferences and just learn from other great people.**"*

### ★★★★ Productise

> *"**creating packages and pricing to automate your workflow**… **you start treating your creative
> work as an OFFER and not just OUTPUT.** And here's where you start seeing consistent \$5–8,000
> months. **And if you're not here right now — relax. You're closer than you think. You're just
> missing some leverage.**"*

**How:** map the customer journey *"birth to delivery"* — onboarding documentation, file structures,
a **Trello** board so clients can watch the trafficking, a calendar with milestones, a spec sheet.
Then templates: a **brand style guide template**, and **mock-up templates** (t-shirts, signage,
merch, caps, delivery trucks, packaging tape). *"**It will be a lot of work upfront — but every time
you use it, you're going to save so much time and energy.**"*

★★★ **Payment terms, stated as house standard:** *"**50% upfront, 25% when you hit a key milestone,
and 25% upon delivery, net 14 days.**"*

### ★★★★★ Where you show the work changes who calls you

> ★★★★★ *"the reason I'm concerned about **Fiverr and Upwork** is because **people who generally
> visit those sites really want to pay the least amount of money, have very low standards, and
> really cannot appreciate good design.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The swap-meet analogy:** *"**I can be the same person — but if I'm going to a swap meet
> versus a high-end retail store, my mindset is very different. I don't dare to even ask in a
> high-end retail place for a discount. I'm just either going to buy it or I'm not. But I feel the
> exact opposite at a swap meet — the whole game of it is they overprice so you can negotiate and
> get the price lower. YOU ALWAYS FEEL LIKE A JACKASS IF YOU CAN'T GET IT FOR LESS.**"* → *"**be
> careful where you present your work and where the clients come from, because they have a very
> different mindset.**"*

> ⚠️★★★★ **And he flags his own bias, unprompted:** *"**Look, I'm old school — or maybe I'm just
> old. Maybe I'm an old fool, not even an old-school kind of person. I HAVE A LOT OF BIAS AGAINST
> THESE KIND OF WEBSITES.**"* ✅ Recorded **with** the disclaimer; removing it would misrepresent
> him.

★★★★ **The alternative — spec work:** *"**I'd rather do work for friends or family, or do spec
projects on my own** … there's really no point for you to do low-quality work for stupid clients
and get paid nothing. **I'd rather just stay at the wall and do nothing.**"* → *"**do a redesign of
a big brand that kind of missed the mark and do your own take on that**"* — citing a designer known
for hypothetical logo fixes: *"**none of that is real, but it generates so much interest and leads
for him that they turn into real paying clients.**"*

## ★★★★★ Stage 4 — Scale

### ★★★★★ The referral script — credited to Phil M. Jones

> *"**Our friend Phil M. Jones recommends asking for a referral right then and there** — when they
> express genuine gratitude for the high-quality work that you've done. **They're most primed to
> help you in that moment.**"* (from ***Exactly What to Say***)

> ★★★★ **With a self-disclosure:** *"**In the past, I've not been really good at this. I'm a little
> sheepish about asking for referrals.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The staged script:**
>
> *"'**Can I ask you for a favour?**'"* → yes →
> *"'**Do you know one person who is like you, who would need something like what I've been able to
> do? Don't worry — I'm not going to ask you for their name and contact information right now. But
> do you know one other person?**'"* → yes →
> *"'**Okay — so when you said yes, who are you thinking?**'"* → a name →
> *"'**Can I ask for one more favour? Could you reach out to them and let them know a little bit
> about me, so that when I reach out I won't be a stranger to them? And if it's okay, I'd like to
> follow up with you in a week to see if this was done.**'"*

★★★ **Why it works:** *"**people like to hang out with people just like them. It's kind of how it
works.**"*

### ★★★★ The three Ps

**People** (design, project management, marketing) · **Product** (the systems and templates from
stage three) · **Promotion** (*"**systems so that your content creation game's on point and you're
being very consistent, so that you can generate leads even while you sleep**"*).

> ★★★★★ **The trap in this stage:** *"**when things are going really well… DO NOT TAKE YOUR EYE OFF
> the need to create content, because this is what fills the top of the funnel awareness. If you
> slow this down, what inevitably will happen is the clients will dry up — and you're going to have
> this long build-up time again to generate leads. AND WE CAN'T JUST MAKE IT RAIN INSTANTLY.**"* →
> *"**spend at least 10% of your energy and your resources on marketing, content creation, editing,
> promotion.**"*

★★★★ **The delegation rule:** *"**If you can pay someone less than what you charge to do
[something] — you should definitely find someone else to help you.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**Protect the most valuable resource that you have — that's non-renewable, that's
> perishable — and that is called TIME. You can make more money, but you can't make more time.**"*

★★★★ **The lead math:** \$10,000/month at four projects and **a 50% close rate** → *"**you need
about 10 to 12 live leads every single month.**"* → *"**Don't become the bottleneck of your own
business.**"*

## ★★★★★ Stage 5 — Sustain

> *"**This is not a short game. This is a long game. Because if you love design like the way I love
> it — you want to do it until the wheels fall off the wagon. The only way you can do that is you
> must PROTECT YOUR PEACE.**"*

### ★★★★★ Multiply yourself — and the steak rule

> *"**Document your creative process so that new team members coming in can do the work the way
> that you do it.** And you're going to **elevate yourself to the person who's like the quality
> control person.** So when clients hire you, **they aren't literally hiring you to physically do
> all the work yourself. They're hiring you because of THE STANDARDS OF QUALITY that you're able to
> deliver.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The trap:** *"**We can fall into this trap where we OVER-DELIVER — where we feel like we
> have to get our hands into everything and put our thumbprint on the work, otherwise we don't feel
> good about ourselves.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The rule, which he repeats deliberately:** *"**Give the client the best work THAT THEY
> PAID FOR — not the best work that you CAN do. That's a big difference. Okay? I'm going to say
> that one more time.**"*

> ★★★★★ **The steak:** *"**Imagine you are in a restaurant and you order the T-bone steak, and they
> bring out the prime rib, or they bring out the filet mignon. Those are much more expensive cuts.
> Clearly that's the best steak that they can make — BUT THAT'S NOT THE STEAK YOU PAID FOR. And
> that's the fastest way for you to burn out and go bankrupt.**"*

> ★★★★ **With the floor underneath it:** *"**we have a standards of excellence, a quality of work.
