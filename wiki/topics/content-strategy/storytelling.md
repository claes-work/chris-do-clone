# Storytelling and communication craft

Chris Do's rules for telling a story — assembled here as **one curriculum**, because he teaches
it in two halves that never appear together in a single source: the **writing/texture** half
(2025-10-25) and the **structure** half (2026-01-10), with a **compression** layer
(2026-02-24) and a **naming** layer (2026-01-30) on top.

> **Why this page exists.** The material was scattered across four source pages and was being
> cross-linked to a `topics/communication` hub that **does not exist in this repo's taxonomy**
> (the seven domains are branding · business · content-strategy · design-craft · mindset ·
> pricing · sales-clients). Rather than invent an eighth domain, the craft lives here as a
> sub-page of content-strategy — the same pattern as
> [[../branding/authenticity-gap|branding/authenticity-gap]].

## 1. Structure — where a story starts and how it moves

### ★★★★★ Begin at the point of greatest conflict (2026-01-10)

> ★★★★★ *"**We don't begin the story at the beginning. WE BEGIN IT AT THE POINT OF GREATEST
> CONFLICT.**"* → *"and then **you reverse engineer, and you tell just parts of the story.**"*

**The diagnosis it answers:** *"**you have a wild, crazy, interesting story — but you're telling
it in a LINEAR way**, which is mostly what people do in a narration style."*
([[../../sources/2026-01-10-yt-tMAnP6EmF8g]])

### ★★★★★ "But," not "and then"

> ★★★★★ *"**You have to keep telling people WHAT'S AT STAKE. Cuz your story is 'and then, and
> then, and then' — WHICH IS NOT THAT INTERESTING. Instead, if it's 'BUT' or 'INSTEAD OF' —
> there's twists and turns.**"*

> ★★★★ *"**It's those little diversions, the turns in the road, THE FORKS IN THE ROAD, that make
> a story really interesting. And you have to SET UP AN EXPECTATION, AND THEN DISRUPT IT.**"*

### ★★★★★ Start at the end — stated twice, in two media

> ★★★★★ **For writing (2025-10-25):** *"**you start at the end. If you know how the story ends,
> the writing of the story becomes really easy.**"* — with the kitchen analogy: *"it's like we're
> going to make a meal but we don't know what we're making, so we use lots of ingredients. It's
> messy. **And then it's like soup.** But if you're like: I'm going to make beef Wellington —
> well, what's the first step? **You have a much clearer idea.**"* → *"**Where do you want this
> plane to land? What is the point you want to make?**"*
> ([[../../sources/2025-10-25-yt-Z0wnvRTWYmo]])

> ★★★★★ **For spoken story (2026-01-10):** *"**if you know how the story ends, you'll know how to
> begin — AND YOU'LL KNOW WHAT PARTS TO INCLUDE AND WHAT PARTS TO EXCLUDE.**"* → *"**only tell the
> parts that build up to that ending. Otherwise it's just extra information.**"* → and the verdict
> on a rambling draft: *"**not all of that is relevant to LANDING THE PLANE.**"*

★★★★★ **And the clearest account in the corpus of how he retrieves stories live on stage:**

> *"**If I'm on stage and somebody asks a question, I KNOW HOW THE STORY ENDS. Now I'm looking for
> a story BEGINNING, because I know what the point is. The point is: let go of hate. So I'm
> searching my mind really quickly — when was the time when I held onto hate that really hurt
> me?**"*

### ★★★★ The exercise

> *"**What are the top three turning points in your life?** … you should be able to quickly scan
> your memory bank and say: there's one, there's two, there's three. And then write about it
> like: **what was it that FLIPPED THE SWITCH for me?**"*

## 2. Texture — what makes a told story land

### ★★★★★ Think like a director: the establishing shot (2025-10-25)

He stops a draft at its first sentence, before any content critique: *"**Okay, we have to pause.
THERE'S NO SETTING.**"*

> ★★★★★ *"**I want you to think like a director. ALL FILMS OPEN WITH AN ESTABLISHING SHOT.** It's
> a giant spaceship. It's the wild wild west. It's the jungles of Brazil — **and then you know
> where you are.** And then **a story is told in INCREASINGLY CLOSE-UP SHOTS.** … **Wide vista,
> tighter, tighter, tighter — lightsaber, somebody's scratching your fingernail. That's how it's
> told.**"*

**The repair, in four words** — *"1996. Portland, Oregon. 21 years old."* → *"**See, now I know
where I'm at. SO YOU'VE ORIENTED ME NOW.**"*

★★★ **The homework:** *"**next time you watch any movie, I want you to pay attention to this.**"*

### ★★★★ Build tension before you resolve it

> *"**What we want to do is — WE'RE ALWAYS BUILDING TENSION.** 'I quit my job… **but I desperately
> needed it, because rent was due.** I didn't have a lot of options, **but I knew this wasn't
> it.**' **This is just basic writing stuff.**"*

> ★★★★ **The ordering rule:** *"this one is dread, doom, fear, anxiety — [then] **relief. RELIEF
> HAS TO COME VERY LATE. Because if you get the relief, I don't read the rest.**"*

### ★★★★ Sensory detail over summary

He interrogates an adjective rather than accepting it — *"he was an alcoholic"* → *"**How did you
know?** … bloodshot eyes, staggered or slurred speech — nothing? **If you can recall — because
THOSE DETAILS TAKE US RIGHT THERE.**"*

> ★★★★ *"otherwise it becomes **A GENERIC STORY OF OBSERVATION**, versus **putting me in there and
> making me feel the sweat.**"*

★★★★ **And for spoken story (2026-01-10):** *"**great stories have other senses, sensory detail.
It's rich in dialogue, and you're painting the scene. YOU NEED TO ACT OUT THOSE MOMENTS.** …
**when you're just saying it to camera like that — although it could be the world's most
interesting story — you're not giving it the best chance to succeed.**"*

### ★★★★★ Every paragraph needs an emotion — because art is transferred emotion

> ★★★★★ *"**Art is: you have an emotion, and you create something that when somebody else sees or
> experiences it, HAS A SHARED EMOTION WITH YOU — HAVING NOT SPOKEN TO YOU.** When you watch a
> movie and you're crying, it's because that person wanted you to feel this emotion."*

> ★★★★★ *"**So in every paragraph you could ask yourself: WHAT IS THE EMOTION? NO EMOTION,
> THERE'S NO STORY.** … **If there's no emotion — rewrite it. Rewrite it until you hit the
> emotion.**"*

★★★ **A cultural objection, and his answer.** A participant says that in their culture you don't
show emotion → *"**Well, screw that.**"* — then the actual argument, which is that the objection
is a category error: *"**even if you did — let's say you're in an Italian family — YOU STILL
WOULDN'T WRITE LIKE THIS.**"* ✅ i.e. expressive writing is **a craft, not a temperament.**

> ⚠️ **He is not exempting himself.** The same *"in my culture you don't show emotion"* framing is
> **his own upbringing** elsewhere in the corpus — see [[../../../persona/biography|biography]]
> and *"an emotional immigrant boy"* ([[../../sources/2025-10-30-yt-3ikc89b-tkE]]).

## 3. Compression — the discipline of leaving things out

### ★★★★★ Write long, then cut

> ★★★★ *"**Now I'm going to tell you how to write a LONG-ASS STORY with great details. Then you
> have to go back in and EDIT THE SUCKER DOWN.**"* → *"**storytelling is the constant editing out
> and adding in. When it's thin, we add in. When it's too much, we edit out.**"*

★★★ **The AI role he endorses — compression, not generation:** *"**you could speak this to GPT or
Claude and then say: format this for LinkedIn. And it will strip out what it doesn't think is
necessary — but give it the juicy details.**"*

### ★★★★★ How comedians do it (2026-01-10)

> *"**You'll see that master storytellers and comedians usually will tell you something — BUT THEY
> ONLY TELL YOU THE PARTS.**"*

Recounting a bit about someone concealing lactose intolerance on a first date, he draws the
lesson **from the omissions**: *"**he's setting up only the parts that make sense. He didn't tell
you what she looked like, where they were, what they ate.** … **So a lot of editing. So you think:
where does this story end, and how do I begin it with A MAXIMUM AMOUNT OF TENSION? And once you
have them hooked, they can't get off.**"*

### ★★★★★ The repeatable story — credited to his business coach (2026-02-24)

> ★★★★★ *"**This is a concept I learned from my business coach: YOU HAVE TO TELL A STORY THAT'S SO
> EASILY PACKAGED THAT WHEN YOU'RE NOT IN THE ROOM, THEY CAN TAKE THAT AND SHARE IT WITH SOMEONE
> ELSE.**"* ([[../../entities/kier-mclaren|Kier McLaren]];
> [[../../sources/2026-02-24-yt-939NOouclvM]])

> ★★★★★ **Why people fail at it:** *"**it's a lot of work for you to be telling every single
> person what it is that you do. The goal is to tell a repeatable story. AND WHAT GETS IN THE WAY
> IS YOU FEEL COMPELLED TO TELL THE WHOLE STORY — not just the part that's memorable, different,
> and unique.**"*

★★★★ **The test:** *"**the first problem with telling your story is: IS IT TOO COMPLICATED? If you
make something too complicated, I WON'T BE ABLE TO TELL THAT STORY.**"* → the room's answer:
*easy, simple, direct* → *"**very direct. Not a lot of adjectives.**"*

> ✅ **The format that tested it.** In the same episode, contestants had to brief **a total
> stranger** who then pitched on their behalf to judges. **The stranger is the absent room.**
> (The device is the show's, not his — but it operationalises the lesson exactly.)

### ★★★★★ Make a fact visual

Asked whether to lead with facts or story: *"**if you bombard me with facts, it's overwhelming.
STORIES ARE THE MOST NATURAL WAY THAT WE COMMUNICATE. FACTS ARE NOT.**"* — but given a statistic,
he **converts** rather than discards it:

> ★★★★★ *"**How much is that in VOLUME?** … That's the more interesting fact. **You could say:
> which is the equivalent to THREE EMPIRE STATES.**"* → *"**You make a fact more interesting by
> MAKING IT VISUAL. Make it something I can relate to.**"*

### ★★★★★ Say less

> ★★★★★ *"**You're going to try and tell them TOO MUCH. RESIST THAT INSTINCT, PLEASE.** **It's much
> more effective for you to say LESS and for them to REMEMBER — with a really strong hook.**"*

★★★★ **And the constraint diagnosis, for people with too many stories:** *"**you sometimes may have
an abundance of ideas, and it's overwhelming for people. YOU NEED A GOOD EDITOR** — and I don't
mean a person cutting your videos, but **a person who says: you need to just focus in on this.**"*
→ *"**You have to show CONSTRAINT. IT'S LIKE DRINKING FROM A FIRE HYDRANT — it's just too
much.**"* ([[../../sources/2026-01-10-yt-tMAnP6EmF8g]])

## 4. Naming — the layer most people skip

From the offer-design workshop at [[../../sources/2026-01-30-yt-LdPHurNaIec]], where he renames
three things in front of the room and the value changes without the work changing:

| What they said | What he renames it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *kickoff call* / *intro call* | ★★★★★ **discovery call** | *"**Same meeting, DIFFERENT FEELING about it.** Who wants to get on an intro call? Who wants to get on a **discovery** call? … **I want to discover something. LANGUAGE MATTERS.**"* |
| *mood board* | ★★★★★ **alignment** | *"**Mood board is what you DO. What they GET is ALIGNMENT.**"* |
| *checklist* | ★★★★★ **road map** | *"**Who wants a checklist? No one. Who wants a road map? I do.**"* — and, mid-demonstration: *"**Are you playing the game I'm playing?**"* |

> ★★★★★ **The governing rule:** *"**Don't talk about what you do. TALK ABOUT THE OUTCOME OF WHAT
> YOU DO. This is very important.**"* — anchored by ★★★★★ *"**People don't buy press-on nails. They
> want to show off to their girlfriends. It's a date night. GIVE THEM THE RESULT, NOT WHAT YOU
> DO.**"* (versus *"the foam insert, the leather overlays, and how comfortable our shoes are"* →
> *"Style. Affordable style. Urban. **Affordable urban style.**"*)

✅ This is the applied form of ★★★★★ *"**words shape worlds**"*
([[../../sources/2025-12-31-yt-POnPkjjT6Pw]]) — the same argument, run live on a deliverables
list.

## 5. Where the craft comes from — read writers

> ★★★★★ *"**Do you listen to The Moth Radio Hour?** … there's a reason why people are telling you
> to listen to it. **Cuz they know how to tell stories.**"* — with the observation that makes the
> recommendation useful: *"**here's the thing that surprised me but doesn't surprise me. ALMOST
> ALL THE ONES THAT HAVE ME IN TEARS — listening in the driveway while I'm still sitting at home —
> IT'S BECAUSE THEY'RE WRITERS. Duh. WRITERS KNOW HOW TO CRAFT A STORY.**"*

> ★★★★ **The prescription that follows:** *"**when you're doing top of dome, it can be a little
> sloppy and unstructured. So I would love for you to SIT DOWN AND WRITE your story.**"*

★★★★ **And the research standard he sets for teaching anything** (2026-04-07): *"**a learned
person, somebody much more intellectually curious, would say: WHAT ARE THE TOP FIVE BOOKS ON
STORYTELLING? And watch movies, read stories, watch plays — and start to understand THE INVISIBLE
STRUCTURE THAT BINDS THEM TOGETHER. What is the thing that makes the top 100 films of all time
great?**"* → then test the ideas in conversation and small workshops → *"**and then you start to
see what ideas stick. Now you have something.**"*

> ★★★★★ **What "learn" means to him:** *"**When I say learn, I don't mean READ, I mean LEARN** —
> that you can internalise it, you can share ideas. **Once you learn something, IT GETS MUTATED BY
> YOUR OWN BRAIN IN A GOOD WAY. You say it a little differently. You embellish this and remove
> that — and all of a sudden IT STARTS TO BECOME MORE YOURS AND LESS THEIRS.**"*

> ★★★★★ **And the difference between teaching a story and telling one:** *"**one way is
> mechanically saying: here's how you tell a story. THE OTHER, MORE INTERESTING WAY IS YOU TELL AN
> AMAZING STORY AND SAY: 'do you want to know why I did that? Here's what happened.' THAT becomes
> part of your brand.**"* ([[../../sources/2026-04-07-yt-d7bgouAH3ns]])

## ⚠️ Notes for use

- **The two halves are one curriculum.** Structure (2026-01-10) without texture (2025-10-25)
  produces a correct but bloodless story; texture without structure produces the *"and then, and
  then"* problem he names. **Cite them together.**
- **"Start at the end" appears in both**, in different words (*"where do you want this plane to
  land"* / *"not all of that is relevant to landing the plane"*). It is the hinge between them.
- ⚠️ **The 1996 Portland story workshopped in 2025-10-25 belongs to the participant, not to
  Chris.** His own 1996 is Blind, in Los Angeles. Do not let them merge.
- ⚠️ **Selective editing vs. the fidelity rules.** *"Don't let facts get in the way of a good
  story… I got the mic"* sits in tension with his insistence elsewhere on citation and never
  fabricating. He appears to draw a **stage-vs-fact** line but does not state it — see
  [[../../../persona/beliefs|beliefs]].
